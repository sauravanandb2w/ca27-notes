Executive summary

  • Part A: the 2026 Nobel (Robert E. Hall, Michael Woodford) honours the "microeconomic foundations of macroeconomic stabilisation". Keynesian fiscal activism broke down in the 1970s stagflation. New Keynesians ground stabilisation in household expectations (Hall) and in sticky prices and wages with forward guidance (Woodford). In India, the inflation-targeting framework (4% ± 2%) and forward guidance are the practical legacy.
  • Part B: RTI (2005) shifted governance from secrecy to transparency and is a tool to narrow the state–citizen information gap. The 2019 amendment changed commissioners' tenure and salaries; the 2023 DPDP amendment widened the personal-information exemption and removed the public-interest override for such information. Pendency (~3.85 lakh appeals) is the key symptom.

Instructor's meta-advice: when a question asks you to "discuss", bring out both the positives and the critique in the same paragraph. For RTI evaluation questions, state the government's justification for each amendment before critiquing it.

Part A — Macro stabilisation & the 2026 Nobel

1. The 2026 Nobel Prize in brief

  • Laureates: Robert E. Hall (Stanford) and Michael Woodford (Columbia); shared equally; announced 12 Oct 2026.
  • Citation: "microeconomic foundations of macroeconomic stabilisation".
  • Why it matters: their models underpin how central banks and governments think about stabilisation today. Their work responded to the 1970s oil shocks (see Part 3).

2. Classical economics — invisible hand; its collapse (1929)

  • Adam Smith and the classical school: markets self-correct through the invisible hand.
  • Mechanism: (1) self-interest — firms seek profit, consumers seek the best price; (2) price as signal — prices adjust demand and supply; (3) coordination happens automatically, so government non-intervention is the ideal.
  • Collapse: the Great Depression (from 1929) shattered the classical view and created the need for a new policy map.

3. Keynesian economics — demand management; the 1970s breakdown

  • J.M. Keynes: in a slowdown, falling consumer spending lowers demand → firms stop investing and hiring → unemployment rises → spending falls further (a downward spiral).
  • Loss of animal spirits: business confidence collapses; the state must pump demand.
  • Prescriptions: increase public spending and public investment; cut taxes to raise disposable income; investment multiplier; deficit financing; accept some inflation for lower unemployment (Phillips curve).
  • Breakdown (1970s): oil price shocks raised production costs → higher prices, lower profits, layoffs, and no new hiring. Governments kept spending: more money chasing few goods, so inflation rose while unemployment also rose. This is stagflation — the old map no longer worked.

4. New Keynesian economics — Hall (demand) and Woodford (supply)

Robert Hall — the demand side

  • Rational expectations / forward-looking households: people spend based on their expected lifetime income (e.g., a promotion next year), not just today's income. So a temporary government spending boost may not raise consumption — Ricardian equivalence (higher future taxes expected) limits the effect.
  • Random walk of consumption: consumption changes mainly when surprises occur (a lottery win or a sudden job loss); expected changes are already built into spending.
  • Hand-to-mouth households: poor households with no savings spend whatever income they get. So targeted transfers to the poor work better than economy-wide spending, which mainly raises savings among the well-off.

Michael Woodford — the supply side

  • Sticky prices and sticky wages: firms do not cut prices or wages immediately in a slowdown.
  • Menu costs: changing prices is costly (tags, reprinting, uncertainty), so firms keep prices unchanged. Lower demand then leads to lower output and layoffs.
  • Forward-looking pricing: firms set prices based on expected future inflation, not just past costs (cost plus markup).
  • Forward guidance: central banks signal the future path of policy to shape expectations, restoring business confidence (the animal spirits).
  • Monetary policy becomes the primary anchor, with fiscal policy targeted (not economy-wide).
Predictability matters: permanent, rule-based measures (e.g., a GST rate cut or an announced income-tax change) shape expectations better than one-off transfers.

Indian application

  • Inflation targeting: India moved to a flexible inflation-targeting framework (target 4% ± 2%, band 2–6%) in 2016–17. Businesses know inflation will stay in the band, which anchors prices (price stability).
  • Monetary policy stance: accommodative, neutral or calibrated tightening, announced openly at each policy meeting — this is forward guidance.
  • Targeted support: transfers to the hand-to-mouth poor; predictable tax changes for others.
  • Polycrisis response: since 2020, governments have combined monetary easing, strategic reserves (oil), food-security schemes and predictable tax relief — not spending alone.
  • COVID-era illustration (lecturer's suggestion): GST rate rationalisation and an income-tax slab raised to ₹12 lakh (the lecturer's example of predictability).

6. Critiques and live answer-writing

Critiques

  • Savings today ≠ spending tomorrow: forward-looking households may save today; business cannot predict when or where that saving will be spent.
  • Keynesian residue: some economists argue business investment (animal spirits) still drives aggregate demand; household expectations alone do not create investment.
  • Capitalist-control argument: the critics say investment decisions by those who hold capital shape the economy, which micro foundations under-emphasise.

Live answer-writing

  2. Collapse of Keynesian policy in the 1970s: oil shock, stagflation, why the old map failed.

Part B — Right to Information Act

7. Context, concepts and constitutional basis

Context

  • RTI (2005) completed two decades in 2025. The lecturer's point: UPSC asks about recent amendments (2019, 2023), so know them.
  • RTI shifted India from administrative secrecy to citizen-centric accountability.

Concepts — the idea of "public will"

  1. Caretaker, not owner: officials hold public information and money in trust, as trustees (cf. Gandhi's trusteeship idea from Class 18).
  2. "Sunlight is the best disinfectant": transparency reduces corruption and maladministration.
  3. Community good first: exemptions yield to larger public interest.

Vertical and horizontal transparency

  • Vertical: citizens hold the state accountable — the traditional RTI model.
  • Horizontal: private and non-state actors that deliver public services (e.g., passports, elections handled by private players) must also be transparent. The lecturer argues the debate is shifting towards this.

Constitutional basis

  • Art. 19(1)(a) (freedom of speech and expression) and Art. 21 (life with dignity — dignified life needs information). The lecturer's transcript says "191A"; the correct basis is Art. 19(1)(a), with Art. 21.
  • Cases: State of UP v. Raj Narain (1975) and S.P. Gupta (the "First Judges case", 1981) — the Supreme Court recognised the right to know.
  • Value of RTI: instrumental (a tool for accountability and democracy) and intrinsic (informed personal growth).

8. The Act — objectives and key sections

ProvisionWhat it says
s.2(h)"Public authority": any body set up by or under the Constitution or by a law, or owned or funded by government. Includes private bodies doing public activity (as the lecturer says) — verify wording.
s.4Proactive disclosure: public authorities publish specified categories of information voluntarily.
s.8Exemptions: national security, foreign relations, and personal information (unless public interest overrides — the public-interest override).
PIOEvery public authority designates a Public Information Officer; penalties apply for default.
AppealsFirst appeal to a senior officer; second appeal to the Central or State Information Commission.
Time limits30 days generally; 48 hours where life or liberty is involved.
The unique feature: the Information Commission is the highest authority with power to order any public authority to provide information. The Supreme Court's own office was brought under RTI in Subhash Chandra Agarwal (2019).

9. The 2019 amendment; the 2023 (DPDP) amendment

2019 amendment — institutional changes

  • Tenure: originally five years (or until age 65). The amendment made tenure "as prescribed by the Central Government"; the rules fixed three years PT.
  • Salary: originally equal to the Chief Election Commissioner's. The amendment left it to the Centre; the rules fixed ₹2.5 lakh (lecturer's figure; cite cautiously).
  • State commissions: their service terms were earlier set by the State; the amendment brought them under the Centre's control.
  • Government justification: the Election Commission is a constitutional body; the Information Commission is a statutory body, so their status cannot be equated.

2023 amendment — via the DPDP Act, 2023

  • Section 44(3) of the DPDP Act amended s.8(1)(j) of the RTI Act.
  • Before: personal information was exempt, unless disclosure served a larger public interest.
  • After: personal information is exempt, with no public-interest override.
  • Government justification: protecting personal data (after the Puttaswamy privacy judgment and the Srikrishna Committee). The lecturer's argument: this gives a blanket immunity.
  • Definition of "person": the DPDP Act's broad definition may include companies and other entities, widening the exemption further.

10. Data, evolution, significance, challenges

Data (as presented)

About 60 lakh RTI requests a year — among the highest in the world; positive reading: rising public awareness; negative reading: suo motu disclosure is weak.
About 63% of public authorities submitted the mandated third-party audit report; most scored below 50% on the CIC's ten-parameter disclosure, i.e., grade C.
Public authorities disclosing: ~754 (2021–22) to over 1,400 (2024–25).
Pending appeals: ~3.85 lakh across information commissions (as of 30 June 2026, 28 commissions).

Evolution

  1. Judiciary: Raj Narain, S.P. Gupta — the right to know.
  2. Grassroots movements: civil society (e.g., MKSS in Rajasthan).
  3. Legislation: Freedom of Information Act 2002 (not notified); RTI Act 2005.
  4. Judicial expansion (2019): Subhash Chandra Agarwal brought the CJI's office under RTI.
  5. Institutional decline: 2019 and 2023 amendments (the lecturer's view).

Lesson: social legislation succeeds when it combines top-down (judicial and legislative) and bottom-up (awareness and movements) pressure.

Significance

  • Reduces citizen–state information asymmetry; enhances transparency and accountability.
  • Exposed scams and governance failures (the lecturer cites CBSE's online evaluation and electoral-roll issues).

Challenges

  1. Vacancies: some commissions are defunct or lack commissioners.
  2. Backlog: ~3.85 lakh pending cases; long waits erode trust.
  3. Culture of secrecy: weak suo motu disclosure.
  4. Non-imposition of penalties: commissions often accept justifications, weakening deterrence.
  5. Personal risk: whistleblowers and RTI activists face threats (the Whistle Blowers Protection Act is not yet operational).
  6. Independence: the lecturer calls commissioners "caged parrots" (his own phrase; the Supreme Court used this description for the CBI — quote carefully).
  7. Federalism: control of state commissions' terms by the Centre.

Suggestions (lecture)

  • Public records office and officers in every public authority for proper archiving and proactive disclosure.
  • Operationalise the Whistle Blowers Protection Act.
  • Media and citizen engagement; bringing political parties under RTI (political accountability).
  • RTI over DPDP: give RTI an overriding role, rather than leaving the scope of "person" to the DPDP definition.

11. Live answer-writing — 2019 and 2023 amendments and the RTI mandate

Q. "RTI is a tool to bridge the information asymmetry between state and citizens." Evaluate how the legislative amendments of 2019 and 2023 have impacted its statutory mandate. — set in class
  1. Intro: A line establishing RTI's significance, e.g., citizens' right to know what government does.
  2. RTI as a tool: key provisions (s.4, s.8, PIOs, commissions) and examples (Vyapam, CBSE, electoral rolls).
  3. 2019 amendment (institutions): tenure, salary, state commissions — with the government's justification.
  4. 2023 amendment (personal information): DPDP s.44(3), no public-interest override; government's justification (privacy).
  5. Impact: pendency (~3.85 lakh), penalties, vacancies.
  6. Way forward: whistleblower law, public records office, RTI overriding DPDP, political-party disclosure.
  7. Conclusion: RTI as a tool still valid, but its scope has narrowed.
Structure: positives first (RTI as a tool), then the amendments' issues, then the government's justification, then suggestions. Keep the justification for each amendment in the same paragraph as its critique.

Glossary — quick revision

Invisible hand
Classical idea that markets self-correct through self-interest and prices.
Animal spirits
Keynes: business confidence that drives investment.
Stagflation
High inflation with high unemployment and stagnant growth (1970s).
Ricardian equivalence
Households save more when they expect higher future taxes to finance today's deficit.
Random walk of consumption
Hall: consumption changes mainly with unexpected shocks.
Menu cost
Cost of changing prices, causing sticky prices.
Forward guidance
Central bank signalling future policy to shape expectations.
Inflation targeting
Central bank targets a set inflation rate (India: 4% ± 2%).
Public-interest override
RTI principle that disclosure of exempt information can be required if public interest outweighs harm.
Horizontal transparency
Citizens' right to ask transparency from private entities delivering public services.
Caged parrot
Phrase used for an institution that is not independent (Supreme Court, on the CBI; the lecturer applies it to information commissions).
