1. Rapid-fire revision recap (prior classes)

2:45Instructor ran a true/false quiz recapping several previous-class topics before moving to fresh material. Key corrections flagged during the recap:

  • CCTS (Carbon Credit Trading Scheme):
    • Scheme administered by the Ministry of Power.
    • Bureau of Energy Efficiency (BEE) is the administrator.
    • Greenhouse-gas emission-intensity targets are notified by the Ministry of Environment, not the Ministry of Power.
    • Secondary aluminium — not primary aluminium — was the specific item newly included under the GHG emission-intensity target framework.
    • Cement and aluminium are both included under the emission-intensity targets; thermal power plants are not included there but are covered under the separate PAT (Perform, Achieve, Trade) scheme.
  • Pension schemes — UPS vs. NPS vs. OPS vs. Atal Pension Yojana:
    • The Unified Pension Scheme (UPS) has not replaced the National Pension System (NPS) — employees can choose either.
    • UPS guarantees some minimum pension amount.
    • Under NPS, the employee also contributes (10% of pay), unlike the Old Pension Scheme (OPS) where the employee made no contribution and government funded it entirely.
    • Under the Atal Pension Yojana, the government does not contribute; the nominee does receive a pension payout.
  • Drinking-water standards (ISI/BIS):
    • A dedicated BIS standard exists specifically for drinking water.
    • The standard accounts for E. coli/coliform content, not just heavy metals.
    • Permissible E. coli/coliform count in drinking water is zero per 100 ml, not "less than 3 per 100 ml."
  • Riverine dolphin survey:
    • The Indus River dolphin was already covered in an earlier survey, not included "for the first time."
    • The Irrawaddy dolphin was included in this survey for the first time.
    • India has signed the Bogota Declaration, which includes the Gangetic dolphin among the river-dolphin species it covers.
    • The Irrawaddy dolphin is not an obligate freshwater dolphin — it can also be found in lagoons.
    • Conservation status of the Irrawaddy dolphin is "Endangered," not "Critically Endangered."
    • Among river dolphins, only the Yangtze finless porpoise carries a "Critically Endangered" status; all other river dolphin species are listed as "Endangered."
  • Bactrian camels:
    • In India, Bactrian camels are found specifically in Ladakh's Nubra Valley, not generally across Ladakh and Rajasthan.
    • India's Bactrian camels participating in Republic Day-linked news were domestic Bactrian camels, not wild Bactrian camels.
    • The wild Bactrian camel's conservation status has not been "recently upgraded to Critically Endangered" — it was downgraded from Critically Endangered to Endangered.
Treat every "true/false" recap in this series as a live gap-check: if a statement trips you up here, that specific fact is exactly the kind of granular detail UPSC Prelims tests — note it down and revise it separately rather than only tracking the new topics.

2. UNCLOS and its three lesser-known bodies

6:58Instructor introduced BBNJ via a build-up through UNCLOS and its lesser-known specialised bodies, since 2025 marked UNCLOS's 30th anniversary of India's ratification — a "round-number anniversary" the instructor flagged as raising Prelims probability.

UNCLOS basics

  • UNCLOS (United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea): also known as the "Constitution for the Oceans."
    • India signed and ratified UNCLOS.
    • India ratified UNCLOS in 1995.
    • 2025 marked roughly 30 years since India's ratification.
  • Maritime zones under UNCLOS:
    • Territorial sea: up to 12 nautical miles from the baseline.
    • Contiguous zone: the next 12 nautical miles, i.e. up to 24 nautical miles from the baseline.
    • Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ): up to 200 nautical miles from the baseline.
    • Beyond 200 nautical miles, the water column is treated as the "high seas" (termed "the Area" under UNCLOS terminology).
  • Continental-shelf exception (UNCLOS Article 77):
    • If a country's continental shelf physically extends beyond 200 nautical miles, the seabed and subsoil there still belong to that country's continental shelf.
    • The country holds sovereign rights over resources (minerals, oil) found on/under that extended seabed.
    • The country cannot restrict freedom of navigation there — sovereignty does not extend to the water column.
    • The water column above that extended seabed is still legally treated as high seas.
  • High seas coverage: the high seas cover roughly 64% of the ocean area, and before BBNJ no single treaty comprehensively covered conservation there.

Three UNCLOS-linked institutions with Indian members

ITLOS (International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea):
  • Headquartered in Hamburg, Germany.
  • Adjudicates maritime disputes under UNCLOS.
  • India's newly elected member: Prof. Bimal N. Patel (transcribed in the lecture as "Vimal N. Patel" — see Supplementary research for the corrected name).
  • Elected at the 36th Meeting of States Parties to UNCLOS, held at UN Headquarters, New York, in June 2026.
  • Term runs from 2026 to 2035; he assumes office on 1 October 2026, succeeding India's outgoing member Neeru Chadha.
ISA (International Seabed Authority):
  • Headquartered in Kingston, Jamaica.
  • Grants mineral-exploration licences for the international seabed.
  • India already holds an exploration contract for polymetallic nodules in the Central Indian Ocean Basin (originally signed 2002, extended in 2017 and again in 2023).
  • India separately holds a second exploration contract for polymetallic sulphides on the Indian Ocean Ridge (signed 2016).
  • Polymetallic nodules: mineral concretions on the deep seabed containing an aggregation of multiple metals (e.g. nickel, copper, cobalt, manganese).
CLCS (Commission on the Limits of the Continental Shelf):
  • Headquartered in New York.
  • Adjudicates/reviews claims relating to continental-shelf limits.
  • India's member: Rajan Sivaramakrishnan (transcribed in the lecture as "Rajan Shiva Ramakrishnan" — see Supplementary research for the corrected name).
  • Term runs from 16 June 2023 to 15 June 2028.

3. BBNJ Agreement — the "High Seas Treaty"

Background and timeline

  • BBNJ: Biodiversity Beyond National Jurisdiction Agreement.
    • Also known as the "High Seas Treaty."
    • Adopted in June 2023 under the UN framework (UNCLOS).
    • Reached 60 ratifications — the threshold needed to bring it into force.
    • Entered into force 120 days after the 60th ratification, in 2026.
  • India's position:
    • India has signed the BBNJ Agreement.
    • India has not yet ratified it.
    • India's stated position: ratification will follow only after India makes the necessary domestic legal changes.
    • The Sanjay Upadhyay Committee has been constituted for this purpose.
  • BBNJ is the third implementation agreement under UNCLOS.
    • First: the UN Fish Stocks Agreement (ensures overfishing does not happen).
    • Second: the Implementation Agreement on Deep Seabed Mining.
    • Third: BBNJ.

Guiding framework and core objective

  • Guiding framework: ensure the high seas are treated as a global commons.
  • Core goal: conservation and sustainable use of marine biological diversity in the high seas.
  • Approach used: an ecosystem-based approach — species, water currents, and pressures are jointly/integratedly conserved rather than piecemeal.
  • Environmental impact assessments are required before harmful activities.
  • Precautionary principle applied:
    • Environmental protective action should not wait for conclusive scientific evidence of harm.
    • Harm is assumed by default from the outset, rather than waiting for proof.

Key mechanisms under BBNJ

  • Fair and equitable sharing of marine genetic resource benefits: if an organism/alga with economic or therapeutic value is found in the high seas, benefits from its use are to be equitably shared.
  • Area-based management tools: primarily the development of Marine Protected Areas.
  • Marine technology transfer and capacity building: a mechanism to transfer marine technology and build capacity, especially for developing countries.
  • Institutional architecture:
    • Conference of Parties (COP) — the main decision-making body, including on Marine Protected Area designations.
    • Clearing House Mechanism — a central platform to share information on genetic resources.
    • Scientific and Technical Body — provides scientific inputs needed for area-based management.

What BBNJ does NOT cover

  • Warships and related naval auxiliary vessels are excluded.
  • Military aircraft are excluded.
  • Commercial fishing/fish catch is excluded.
    • Fish conservation and genetic-resource-sharing linked to fish are, however, covered.
    • In other words, BBNJ does not restrict someone's ability to catch fish commercially, but it does cover conserving fish and sharing genetic-resource benefits derived from them.

Criticism / limitations flagged in class

  • The US has not signed UNCLOS itself, and separately criticises BBNJ for potentially hindering bio-prospecting research.
    • Bio-prospecting research: exploration to identify biological/marine species that could be economically or therapeutically (e.g. medicinally) valuable.
    • Criticism: BBNJ requires clarity on how it will coordinate with existing sectoral agreements (e.g. MARPOL, the Hong Kong Convention, the Ballast Water Management Convention), and a clear implementation roadmap is currently lacking.
  • Disputes remain over maritime territories in various regions.
  • Interconnectedness of marine ecosystems is a concern: activity in the EEZ or territorial waters spills over into the high seas, but BBNJ's focus is on the high seas with no corresponding regard for EEZ/territorial-water activity.
  • Reluctance of coastal states to assume greater responsibility in the name of trade and other economic activities is flagged as a standard criticism of ocean-governance treaties generally.
If a Prelims statement claims "there was no treaty before the High Seas Treaty to regulate pollution in the sea," mark it false — MARPOL already existed for ship-based pollution. BBNJ's distinguishing feature is that it is the first treaty for active conservation of high-seas biodiversity specifically, not the first ocean-pollution treaty of any kind.

4. Other maritime-pollution conventions

14:52Before returning to BBNJ's institutional details, the instructor mapped out the existing patchwork of ship-pollution conventions, to pre-empt a common Prelims trap about "no treaty existed before BBNJ."

MARPOL Convention

MARPOL (International Convention for the Prevention of Pollution from Ships):
  • Deals specifically with pollution from ships.
  • Falls under the IMO (International Maritime Organization), not under UNCLOS.
  • IMO is headquartered in London.
  • India is a member of the IMO.
  • India has ratified MARPOL's annexes.
  • Framed in class as a "negative treaty": it avoids/prevents pollution, but it does not itself promote conservation.
MARPOL's six technical annexes: (1) Oil · (2) Noxious liquid substances · (3) Harmful substances in packaged form · (4) Sewage from ships · (5) Garbage from ships · (6) Air pollution
Only Annexes I and II are mandatory for every MARPOL party; the remaining annexes are optional/opt-in (per Supplementary research — not explicitly stated in the lecture, added for completeness)
  • Ballast water is NOT covered by MARPOL.
    • Ballast water: water stored in a ship's hull to keep the ship balanced.
    • Environmental concern: ballast water taken on at one port (e.g. Panama) and discharged at another (e.g. South Africa) can carry fish, crabs, and other organisms that become invasive species at the discharge location.
    • This is instead covered by a separate Ballast Water Management Convention.
  • Radioactive substances are also not covered by MARPOL.
  • Oil-spill response (as opposed to routine oil-pollution prevention) is also not covered by MARPOL.
    • MARPOL Annex I covers prevention of routine oil pollution from ships.
    • A separate convention covers preparedness and response if an oil spill/wreckage actually occurs: the Oil Spill Preparedness and Response Convention (OPRC).
  • Ship recycling is also not covered by MARPOL.
    • This is instead covered by the Hong Kong Convention, which deals with ship recycling/ship-breaking.
Build a one-line mapping table for exam recall: pollution-from-ships → MARPOL · invasive species via ballast → Ballast Water Management Convention · oil-spill response → OPRC · ship recycling/breaking → Hong Kong Convention · high-seas biodiversity conservation → BBNJ. Each convention has a distinct, non-overlapping scope — a frequent source of statement-based Prelims traps.

5. RBI Priority Sector Lending (PSL) norms

34:53Instructor flagged multiple revisions to PSL norms across 2025 and 2026, noting textbooks may still show older figures.

What PSL is

  • Priority Sector Lending (PSL): an RBI-mandated credit-allocation framework.
    • There is no government subsidy on PSL loans.
    • The government does not itself lend the money.
    • Loans are not interest-free.
    • Banks are simply directed to allocate a prescribed proportion of their eligible credit toward specified priority sectors and borrower categories.
    • Objective: ensure sectors/borrowers often overlooked by banks (weaker sections, small farmers, small enterprises) receive credit, supporting financial inclusion and employment generation.
  • Base for calculating targets: whichever is higher of—
    • ANBC (Adjusted Net Bank Credit): credit already on a bank's balance sheet, after RBI-prescribed adjustments.
    • CEOBSE (Credit Equivalent of Off-Balance Sheet Exposure): the loan-equivalent value of off-balance-sheet commitments such as bank guarantees, converted using an RBI-prescribed formula.

Overall PSL targets by bank category

Domestic commercial banks: 40% of ANBC/CEOBSE (whichever higher)
Foreign banks with 20+ branches: 40%
Foreign banks with fewer than 20 branches: 40% overall, but only up to 32% for export credit plus 8% for any other priority sector (no agriculture or weaker-section sub-target)
Regional Rural Banks (RRBs): 75%
Small Finance Banks: 60% (revised down from 75% effective FY 2025-26)
Urban Co-operative Banks: 60% (agriculture sub-target not specified, since urban areas have limited agricultural lending)

Sub-targets within agriculture and weaker sections

Agriculture (overall): 18% of base — applies to domestic banks, RRBs, and Small Finance Banks
Non-Corporate Farmers (NCF), within agriculture: 14% of base (not 14% of the 18% agriculture figure)
Small and Marginal Farmers (SMF), within NCF: 10% of base (not 10% of the 14% NCF figure)
Micro enterprises: 7.5% of base — applies to all categories except foreign banks with fewer than 20 branches
Weaker sections: 12% of base for most categories
Weaker sections for RRBs specifically: 15% of base (the one exception to the 12% figure)
Medium enterprises + social infrastructure + renewable energy (combined): capped at 15% of base
  • Common calculation error flagged in class: the 14% NCF and 10% SMF figures are each a percentage of the total lending base, not a percentage of the 18% agriculture allocation or of each other.
    • Worked example: total lending ₹1,000 crore → total PSL target 40% = ₹400 crore → agriculture target 18% of base = ₹180 crore → NCF target 14% of base = ₹140 crore (not 14% of the ₹180 crore agriculture figure) → SMF target 10% of base = ₹100 crore (not 10% of the ₹140 crore NCF figure).

PSL Certificates (PSLC)

  • PSLC (Priority Sector Lending Certificate): a tradable instrument that lets banks buy/sell PSL-target compliance.
    • If a bank over-lends to priority sectors beyond its target, it can convert the surplus into a certificate and sell it to another bank that has fallen short.
    • Only the certificate (i.e. the compliance credit) is transferred between banks — the underlying loan itself is not transferred.
    • Certificates are issued and traded subject to RBI verification norms.

Shortfall mechanism — RIDF

  • Shortfall: the gap between a bank's actual PSL lending and its prescribed target.
    • Worked example: target 40% (₹400 crore) but actual lending only 38% (₹380 crore) → shortfall of ₹20 crore.
  • Rural Infrastructure Development Fund (RIDF), housed under NABARD: a bank's shortfall amount must be deposited/parked here.
    • RBI can also direct shortfall money toward other specified funds: MUDRA, SIDBI (Small Industries Development Bank of India), NHB (National Housing Bank).
    • Banks earn interest on the parked shortfall, but at a below-market rate.
    • Interest rate formula: bank rate minus a penalty spread, where the penalty widens as the shortfall widens (illustratively: minus 2 percentage points for a small shortfall band, minus 3 points for a larger band, minus 4 points for the largest band).

On-lending and co-lending — recent amendment

  • Direct lending: a bank lends money directly to the ultimate borrower (the usual method).
  • On-lending: a bank routes money through an intermediary, which then lends to the ultimate borrower.
    • Recent amendment: bank credit to the National Cooperative Development Corporation (NCDC), for onward lending by NCDC to cooperative societies for PSL-eligible activities, is now itself eligible to be counted toward the bank's PSL target.
    • Rationale: NCDC has better reach into cooperative societies than banks do directly, saving banks the time/effort of direct outreach.
  • Co-lending: a bank and an NBFC jointly lend to the same borrower.
  • Safeguards added alongside on-lending:
    • Verification and certification rules against double-counting, since the same end-borrower could otherwise be counted by two different lending banks.
    • External auditor/third-party certification mechanisms.
    • Refined RBI norms on how PSL recognition is certified for such routed lending.
For any PSL percentage question, first identify which "base" the percentage is measured against (total ANBC/CEOBSE, or a specific sub-category) — most calculation traps in this topic come from applying a sub-target percentage to the wrong base rather than to total lending.

6. DRDO scramjet technology & hypersonic missiles

1:03:52Instructor used a 2026 DRDO test as the hook to explain the underlying physics of rocket motors, jet engines, ramjets, and scramjets from first principles.

The 2026 DRDO achievement

  • DRDO's Defence Research and Development Laboratory (DRDL), based in Hyderabad, conducted a ground test of a full-scale, actively cooled scramjet combustor.
  • This combustor is intended for India's hypersonic cruise missile development programme.
  • Test milestones: roughly 700 seconds of sustained operation in January 2026, extended to roughly 1,200 seconds (about 20 minutes) in May 2026.
  • Longer engine life directly translates into longer missile range (illustratively: a 12-minute-capable engine might cover ~2,000 km, a 15-minute-capable engine ~2,500 km).

Engine types compared, in order of increasing sophistication

  • Traditional rocket motor:
    • Carries both fuel and its own oxidizer on board.
    • Fuel and oxidizer mix and combust, generating thrust that propels the vehicle forward.
    • Advantage: can operate even in the vacuum of space, since it carries its own oxygen source.
    • Disadvantage: carrying the oxidizer adds dead weight, reducing space available for payload.
  • Jet engine:
    • Uses ambient atmospheric oxygen instead of carrying an oxidizer on board.
    • Needs a mechanical compressor and a turbine to slow incoming air down to a speed at which combustion can be stably sustained.
    • Cannot operate in space, since there is no ambient oxygen there.
  • Ramjet engine:
    • Eliminates the mechanical compressor and turbine.
    • Uses the aircraft's own forward motion, combined with specially shaped air-intake vents, to slow incoming supersonic/hypersonic air down to sub-sonic speed for stable combustion.
    • Cannot operate from a stationary/rest position — needs an initial rocket-motor boost to reach the minimum operating speed first.
  • Scramjet engine ("supersonic combustion ramjet"):
    • Combustion occurs while incoming air remains at supersonic or hypersonic speed, rather than being slowed to sub-sonic speed first.
    • This lets the vehicle sustain hypersonic speeds (above Mach 5) that a conventional ramjet cannot reliably sustain.
    • Core engineering challenge solved: designing combustor materials able to withstand very high temperature and pressure without burning out.
    • Solution used: an actively cooled combustor made with an endothermic hydrocarbon fuel.
      • The fuel first circulates through the combustor's walls, absorbing heat (cooling the chamber) before combustion.
      • The same fuel then serves as the combustion material itself.
      • The fuel therefore performs two roles: cooling agent and combustion material.
    • Also cannot start from rest — needs an initial rocket-motor boost to reach the minimum operating speed (illustratively Mach 4) before the scramjet engine activates and the booster detaches.
    • Cannot operate in the vacuum of space, since it still relies on ambient atmospheric oxygen.

Hypersonic cruise missiles vs. hypersonic glide vehicles

  • Both types require an initial rocket-motor boost/acceleration phase.
  • Hypersonic cruise missile: uses sustained propulsion (e.g. a scramjet) throughout its flight after the initial boost.
  • Hypersonic glide vehicle: receives only the initial boost, then glides unpowered using the laws of motion and physics, with no sustained propulsion.
  • Both can travel at hypersonic speed (above Mach 5); the difference lies purely in whether propulsion continues after the boost phase.

7. Critical minerals: coking coal & MMDR Schedule I

1:17:12Direct follow-on from Class 10's MMDR Amendment Act 2026 topic, focused specifically on a coking-coal-related notification.

Coking coal vs. thermal coal

  • Coking coal: used specifically for metallurgical purposes — needed to make coke, a key raw material in steel production.
  • Thermal coal: used primarily for its calorific value — burned to generate heat, used for energy/power generation and heating.

What changed — coking coal added to Part D

  • Government notified coking coal as a critical and strategic mineral under the MMDR Act, 1957.
  • Notification issued 29 January 2026, amending the Act's First Schedule.
  • Schedule I's Part A (hydrocarbons and coal) now explicitly reads "Coal, including Coking Coal."
  • Coking coal has also been separately added to Part D (critical and strategic minerals).
  • Rationale: India has substantial coking-coal resources domestically, yet remains heavily import-dependent for it.
    • Government aims to expand exploration and reduce this import dependency via the National Critical Mineral Mission.

MMDR Act Schedule I — the four-part structure

  • Part A: hydrocarbons and coal (including coking coal).
  • Part B: atomic minerals.
  • Part C: metallic and non-metallic minerals.
  • Part D: critical and strategic minerals.
    • For minerals in Part D, the Central Government has the power to auction mining leases/licences.
    • Even though the Centre auctions Part D minerals, royalty proceeds from mining still go to the state government where the mineral is located.

Two distinct lists — critical minerals list vs. Part D

  • Critical Minerals List (a policy list the Centre separately maintains):
    • Recommended by the Veena Kumari Dermal Committee, constituted by the Ministry of Mines in 2022.
    • Recommended 30 critical minerals.
    • Purpose: to flag minerals India needs to secure supply of, so India does not become overly import-dependent on other countries.
  • Part D of Schedule I (a statutory classification, via the MMDR Amendment Act, 2023):
    • Currently lists 24 minerals.
    • 23 of these 24 were taken directly from the Veena Kumari Dermal Committee's list of 30.
    • 1 additional mineral (glauconite) was added to Part D by the government but was not among the Committee's original 30.
    • Purpose: specifically to settle which level of government auctions the mineral and where mining proceeds go.
  • Net effect: 7 minerals from the 30-mineral Critical Minerals List are not present in the 24-mineral Part D.
    • Named in class (transcription initially unclear on one name, since verified — see Supplementary research): antimony, bismuth, copper, germanium, hafnium, silicon, strontium.
  • Rare earth elements: all 17 rare earth elements (scandium, yttrium, and the 15 lanthanides) are included in Part D under a single combined entry, rather than being listed individually.

Atomic minerals reclassified as critical minerals

  • Six minerals were removed ("denotified") from India's atomic minerals list and moved into Part D's critical-and-strategic-minerals category:
    • Lithium, Titanium, Niobium, Tantalum, Zirconium, Beryllium.
  • Reclassification carried out via the MMDR Amendment Act, 2023.
  • Rationale: while listed as atomic minerals, only Central Government agencies (e.g. the Department of Atomic Energy) could handle them, barring private-sector exploration/mining.
    • Reclassifying them as critical minerals opens them to private-sector participation and auctioning.
    • Framed in class as a precursor step aligned with India's broader nuclear-sector liberalisation (the SHAKTI Act).
  • These six minerals are still associated with radioactive elements in their natural occurrence, which was the original basis for their atomic-minerals classification.

Critical vs. strategic vs. rare-earth — the underlying distinction

  • Critical minerals: minerals India depends on, where that dependence is a matter of economic security (e.g. copper for electronics) or a matter of technology/energy security (e.g. lithium for the EV ecosystem).
  • Rare earth elements: a specific set of 17 elements, important for today's technology (e.g. permanent magnets), not literally "rare" in abundance but difficult to extract in pure form.
  • The two categories overlap (rare earths are also treated as critical) but are conceptually distinct: rare-earth status is about extraction difficulty, critical-mineral status is about strategic dependence.

Revision kit (ARCS)

A — Active Recall: self-test before re-reading

1. What is BBNJ, and where does it fit among UNCLOS's implementation agreements?

BBNJ (Biodiversity Beyond National Jurisdiction Agreement), also called the "High Seas Treaty," is the third implementation agreement under UNCLOS — after the UN Fish Stocks Agreement and the Implementation Agreement on Deep Seabed Mining. Adopted June 2023, it reached 60 ratifications and entered into force in 2026 (120 days after the 60th ratification). It is the first treaty for active conservation of high-seas biodiversity specifically — MARPOL already existed for ship-based pollution, so it is not the first ocean treaty of any kind.

2. Name the three UNCLOS-linked bodies with a current Indian member, their headquarters, and their function.

ITLOS (Hamburg, Germany) adjudicates maritime disputes — India's member is Prof. Bimal N. Patel (2026-2035). ISA (Kingston, Jamaica) grants deep-seabed mineral exploration licences — India holds exploration contracts here for polymetallic nodules and sulphides. CLCS (New York) reviews continental-shelf-limit claims — India's member is Rajan Sivaramakrishnan (2023-2028).

3. Which convention covers which type of ship-related pollution: routine oil pollution, ballast water, oil-spill response, and ship recycling?

Routine oil pollution (and noxious liquids, packaged harmful substances, sewage, garbage, air pollution) — MARPOL, under the IMO. Ballast water/invasive species — the separate Ballast Water Management Convention. Oil-spill preparedness and response — the OPRC Convention. Ship recycling/breaking — the Hong Kong Convention. High-seas biodiversity conservation — BBNJ. Each has a distinct, non-overlapping scope.

4. In RBI's PSL norms, how are the 18% agriculture, 14% NCF, and 10% SMF targets actually calculated?

All three are separately calculated as percentages of the total lending base (ANBC/CEOBSE, whichever higher) — not nested percentages of each other. For ₹1,000 crore total lending: agriculture target = 18% of ₹1,000 crore = ₹180 crore; NCF target = 14% of ₹1,000 crore = ₹140 crore (not 14% of the ₹180 crore agriculture figure); SMF target = 10% of ₹1,000 crore = ₹100 crore (not 10% of the ₹140 crore NCF figure).

5. Distinguish a ramjet from a scramjet, and explain why India's 2026 DRDO test matters.

A ramjet slows incoming supersonic air down to sub-sonic speed (using the aircraft's forward motion and shaped intake vents) before combustion, so it cannot sustain hypersonic (above Mach 5) speeds. A scramjet lets combustion occur while air remains at supersonic/hypersonic speed, enabling sustained hypersonic flight — but this demands combustor materials able to survive extreme heat/pressure. DRDO's actively cooled scramjet combustor, using endothermic hydrocarbon fuel (which cools the chamber walls before serving as combustion fuel), ran for about 1,200 seconds in a May 2026 test (up from ~700 seconds in January 2026) at DRDL, Hyderabad — a building block for India's hypersonic cruise missile programme.

6. What changed with coking coal in January 2026, and how does the 30/24/17 numbering work for critical minerals?

On 29 January 2026, coking coal was notified as a critical and strategic mineral under the MMDR Act — Part A of Schedule I now reads "Coal, including Coking Coal," and coking coal was also added to Part D. Numbering: the Veena Kumari Dermal Committee (2022) recommended 30 critical minerals; only 24 of these sit in Part D of Schedule I (23 from the Committee's list plus glauconite, added separately by government — leaving 7 Committee-listed minerals outside Part D); all 17 rare earth elements are bundled into a single Part D entry within that 24.

UNCLOS's three bodies as a courtroom-mine-map trio: picture a courtroom in Hamburg (ITLOS) settling sea disputes, a mining office in Kingston (ISA) handing out deep-seabed licences, and a surveyor's desk in New York (CLCS) measuring where the continental shelf ends. The scramjet skips the windbreak entirely and manages to keep the flame burning even inside the gale, which is why it needs walls (the combustor) that can survive being blasted by fire and hurricane-force air at the same time — solved here by fuel that cools the walls before it burns.
