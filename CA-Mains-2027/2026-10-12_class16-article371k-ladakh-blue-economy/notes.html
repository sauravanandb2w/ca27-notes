1. Add-on to Class 12 — "Seeing Like a State" & women farmers

0:00Add this point to Class 12's Feminization of Agriculture:

"The state's drive for legibility can exclude those it cannot read." — idea from James C. Scott, Seeing Like a State (1998), American political scientist and anthropologist
  • To document and classify, the state always uses the most simplified grid, and simplified grids miss complexity.
  • Example: the state defines a "farmer" as someone with a record of rights (land title). Women farmers, whose work is activity-based rather than title-based, get excluded.
  • General lens for welfare exclusion: if you don't fit the grid, you're out. It applies to Census categories, SIR/voter rolls (a citizen without the right document) and PDS identification.
Reusable in any exclusion-error answer (DBT, PDS, voter rolls, farmer schemes). Pair it with the fix the class implied: activity-based identification instead of title-based identification.

2. Article 371K — introduction & context

4:33Planned structure: introduction, context, dimensions of federalism, integration debate, amphibian federalism, constitutional framework of Art. 371, Ladakh timeline, the 371K model, concerns, judgments, quotes, way forward.

Introduction (two layers)

  • General: the Indian Constitution's framework of asymmetric federalism is an adaptable mechanism to accommodate regional diversity, historical complexities and national-security or geopolitical vulnerabilities.
  • Specific: the Ministry of Home Affairs (nodal for UTs) has proposed inserting Art. 371K into Part XXI (Temporary, Transitional and Special Provisions) to set up a sui generis model of governance for the UT of Ladakh, balancing national security and local autonomy.

Context

  • The core tension is the Centre's need to control a geostrategic border region versus Ladakhis' demand for local autonomy.
  • There is an in-principle consensus with the two coalitions negotiating for Ladakh: the Apex Body, Leh (ABL / LAB) and the Kargil Democratic Alliance (KDA). This is itself an instance of dialogic federalism.

3. Dimensions of Indian federalism

~7:00Framework attributed in class to former CJI D.Y. Chandrachud.

(a) Origin

  • Partly from British policies of provincial autonomy under the Government of India Act, 1935.
  • After independence, the framers deliberately chose a centralised "indestructible Union" because of Partition and the separatist threats of the time.

(b) Structural features

Asymmetric federalism: states have unique relationships with the Centre (Art. 371–371J, Fifth and Sixth Schedules), unlike the symmetric US model.
Balancing federalism: the mix of rigid and flexible amendment procedures acts as a counterweight to centralisation.
Dialogic federalism: the Union and states discuss and negotiate shared decisions. Example: the GST Council.

(c) Dual framework

Identity federalismEfficiency federalism
Protects cultural, linguistic and tribal distinctiveness through tailored arrangements. Examples: Art. 371 series, Art. 29–30.Strengthens local democratic decision-making by decentralising power for efficiency. Examples: the 73rd/74th Amendments, 50% reservation for women in local bodies.

(d) Judicial eras

  • Pre-Bommai (before 1994) — centripetal era: rulings leaned towards the Centre. Example cited in class: India Cement Ltd v. State of Tamil Nadu (1990), which held royalty to be a tax and so curtailed states' power to tax minerals.
  • Post-S.R. Bommai (1994) — centrifugal era: pro-state autonomy. Bommai declared federalism part of the basic structure.
Update the India Cement example: in Mineral Area Development Authority v. Steel Authority of India (2024) a nine-judge bench overruled India Cement 8:1. It held royalty is not a tax and that states can tax mineral rights. This strengthens the "centrifugal shift" argument.

4. Integration: homogeneity vs constitutional pluralism; amphibian federalism

Integration as homogeneityIntegration as constitutional pluralism
Unified, centralised federalism: every state should have an identical relationship with the Centre. Special provisions and regional exceptions breed sub-nationalism, "zones of exclusion" and "soft sedition", weakening integration. Supports the abrogation of Art. 370 and a UCC.Unity in diversity: integration is an ongoing process of negotiation and accommodation. Asymmetric provisions and regional autonomy are the glue that binds diversity, not obstacles. This is "holding-together" federalism.
Amphibian federalism: ~16:00 the Supreme Court in State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (1977) called the Constitution "amphibian". Like an amphibian living in two environments, it works as a federation in normal times and shifts to a unitary mode in emergencies (e.g., Art. 356) to protect unity and integrity PT.

5. Constitutional framework — Art. 371–371J, Art. 239 family, objectives

19:35

  • Art. 371–371J give special arrangements to states: Maharashtra/Gujarat, Nagaland, Assam, Manipur, Andhra Pradesh/Telangana, Sikkim, Mizoram, Arunachal Pradesh, Goa, Karnataka.
  • Union territories:
    • Art. 239 — every UT is administered by the President through an appointed administrator, which is the source of central control.
    • Art. 239A — Parliament may create a legislature or council of ministers for certain UTs (Puducherry; J&K now under the 2019 Act).
    • Art. 239AA — special provisions for NCT Delhi.
    • Art. 240 — the President's power to make regulations for some UTs.
  • For Ladakh the government is not using the UT-legislature route (239A). It is borrowing the state-oriented Art. 371 route, which is the novelty.

Objectives of Art. 371 provisions

  1. Safeguard distinct social practices, customary law and culture (e.g., Nagaland, 371A).
  2. Protect land rights and local autonomy, so outsiders cannot change the demography and erode identity.
  3. Address regional backwardness (e.g., Vidarbha and Marathwada under 371(2); Hyderabad-Karnataka under 371J).
  4. Resolve ethnic conflict as part of peace accords (Mizoram 371G, Nagaland 371A); national security and law and order.

Ladakh's demands map onto all four: customary law and culture; land rights against outsiders; ecologically sustainable development; and a plural mix of Buddhists, Shias, Sunnis and other minorities.

6. Ladakh timeline & why not the Sixth Schedule

23:42

Before 2019 (part of the J&K state): 4 MLAs in the J&K Assembly; statutory Ladakh Autonomous Hill Development Councils (Leh 1995, Kargil 2003); land and job protections under Art. 370/35A.
2019: Art. 370 abrogated; J&K Reorganisation Act splits the state into the UT of J&K (with a legislature, Puducherry-style) and the UT of Ladakh without a legislature, run by the President through the Lieutenant Governor.
Result: Ladakh lost its special protections and elected legislative representation (still only 1 MP), creating a democratic deficit under LG and bureaucracy-led governance.
Demands: statehood, and Sixth Schedule status.
2024: five new districts announced (Zanskar, Drass, Sham, Nubra, Changthang).

Sixth Schedule — what was demanded

  • Autonomous District Councils act as "mini-parliaments" with legislative, executive, judicial and financial powers.
  • Applies only to four north-eastern states — Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, Mizoram — with 10 ADCs PT.

Why not the Sixth Schedule for Ladakh

  1. Design mismatch: it is meant for tribal districts within states, not a whole UT. Applying it to a UT blurs the state–UT distinction (UTs exist for central administrative control).
  2. Geostrategic location: Ladakh borders China and Pakistan, a highly sensitive border region that needs strong central administration.

Hence Art. 371K is a compromise.

7. The 371K model — significance, concerns, way forward

Proposed structure (as reported)

UT level: a directly elected representative body, with legislative powers over land and land rights, culture and language, environment and natural resources (some reports add forests).
District level: the existing Hill Development Councils continue (2 at present, extendable to the 5 new districts).
Grassroots: Panchayati Raj.
No draft text yet; executive, financial and police control remain open questions.

Significance

  • Fixes the post-2019 democratic deficit and the political dissatisfaction behind it.
  • Meets the core concern of ABL and KDA: land protection against non-resident purchase and settlement, and cultural preservation.
  • A tailor-made, customised asymmetric model that avoids full Sixth Schedule, Puducherry/Delhi or J&K-style autonomy while serving both local and national interests.

Concerns 31:25

  1. National security in a border UT must not be compromised.
  2. Overlapping jurisdictions: a constitutional body (371K), statutory hill councils and PRIs may all claim power over land.
  3. Unprecedented design: Art. 371–371J are for states (about 12). Extending them to a UT without a legislature is new, and it must not violate the basic structure.
  4. Risk of symbolic autonomy: an elected body without real powers, with the LG on top. Cautionary example: Manipur's Art. 371C Hill Areas Committee, whose decisions remain subject to an assembly where the valley holds the majority PT.
  5. Plural demography (Buddhists, Shias, Sunnis, others) makes "cultural preservation" delicate to operationalise.

Quotes 35:46

"However good a Constitution may be, it is sure to turn out bad because those who are called to work it happen to be a bad lot. However bad a Constitution may be, it may turn out to be good if those who are called to work it happen to be a good lot." — B.R. Ambedkar (on constitutional morality)
Paraphrased in class: the Union's strength lies not in the indiscriminate accumulation of functions but in the disciplined refusal of responsibilities that do not properly belong at the national level (principle of subsidiarity). — attributed to K. Santhanam (Constituent Assembly member; later headed the Santhanam Committee)

Way forward 37:45

  • Entrench the subjects (land, culture, ecology) explicitly in the Constitution, as Art. 239AA does, rather than leaving them to "Parliament may by law".
  • Demarcate institutional boundaries between the elected body, hill councils and PRIs.
  • Continue dialogic federalism with stakeholders. Locals are the "eyes and ears" of border security, so autonomy and security are complementary.

9. Blue economy — context, key terms, data

46:21Planned structure: introduction, context, terms, data and reports, significance, challenges, case studies, government initiatives, way forward.

Introductions

  • Definition-based: the blue economy is an economic paradigm covering sustainable ocean-resource use, conservation, offshore energy and coastal governance.
  • Data-based: with a coastline of over 11,000 km, India's fisheries directly support ~28 million livelihoods.

Context 47:40

  • Sapta Dhara ("seven streams of strength"), announced in the PM's 80th Independence Day speech on 15 Aug 2026: (1) manufacturing, (2) agriculture and food processing, (3) technology and innovation, (4) Gati Shakti, (5) defence, (6) green and blue economy, (7) soft power PT.
  • Samudra Manthan — National Offshore Exploration Scheme (Cabinet, 31 Jul 2026): ₹84,084 crore, central sector scheme (MoPNG) to FY31; 60 deepwater wells; 600+ MMTOE reserve accretion; up to 50% support per deepwater well, capped at ₹675 crore.

Key terms

Blue economy: sustainable use of ocean resources for economic growth and livelihoods while preserving ocean-ecosystem health. Three pillars — economic, social, ecological — and India adds a fourth: (maritime) security.
Blue revolution: raising fish production and productivity through sustainable inland and marine fisheries, with biosecurity (e.g., against invasive species), environmental safeguards, and better incomes and nutrition.
Blue-green revolution: the blue revolution's productivity focus combined with green principles of sustainability.

Data 51:13

Recalculated coastline: 11,098 km.
India is the 2nd-largest fish producer; output 197.75 lakh tonnes (2024-25, record).
Seafood exports: record ₹62,408 crore (US$7.45 bn) in FY25 — frozen shrimp ~69% of dollar earnings; US ~36% of value.
Employment: ~28 million directly. Small-scale fishers ~90% of the workforce but ~10% of catch volume (lecture figure).
Women: ~44% of the fisher population (lecture, citing FAO).
Bay of Bengal cyclones cost small-scale fishers ~10–15 fishing days a year.
Pollution costs ~$2.2 bn a year (lecture, citing the World Ocean Summit 2025).

10. Fisheries — significance & challenges

Significance 53:34

  • Affordable, high-value protein to fight malnutrition.
  • Jobs and poverty alleviation (90% small-scale), plus women's financial empowerment.
  • Growth and forex through agri-allied exports.
  • Natural infrastructure: mangroves and coral reefs act as bio-shields for coasts.
  • Critical minerals through deep-sea mining (clean-energy transition).
  • Maritime infrastructure and security reinforce India's role as a net security provider.

Challenges 55:37

  1. Sustainability — a vicious cycle: over-exploitation → depleted stocks → more aggressive gear (bottom trawling) → further depletion and possible extinction. Bottom trawling kills juveniles and brings large discarded by-catch.
  2. Informality and the low-income trap: fragmented small fishers → low productivity → low investment → low income → low productivity. Poor infrastructure means 20–30% post-harvest losses.
  3. Credit: per an RBI paper, allied activities get only ~10% of agricultural credit.
  4. Policy: policy is agriculture-centric; Wildlife Protection Act schedules (e.g., some sharks) make accidental by-catch a legal risk; CRZ rules constrain coastal communities.
  5. Environment: cyclones (10–15 lost days), pollution ($2.2 bn a year).
  6. Trade: dependence on the US (tariffs on shrimp); competition from Ecuador, Vietnam, Thailand and Indonesia; developed-country subsidies.
  7. Technology: low R&D; aquaculture is ~75% of output but faces shortages of quality fish seed; weak biosecurity research.
Flowchart (draw in answer): Informal/fragmented → low productivity → low investment → low income → back to low productivity (low-income trap).

Quotes

"Blue economy is our new frontier for growth — a pathway to prosperity, sustainability and national strength." — attributed in class to a Union minister ("Dr. Singh"); verify the exact speaker before quoting
"Whichever way we turn, we are drawn to the seas… Our future security and prosperity are closely linked with the freedom and resources of the oceans." — attributed to Jawaharlal Nehru

11. Case studies, government initiatives & way forward

Case studies 1:01:05

  • Odisha's Marine Spatial Plan (April 2026): first Indian state to roll out an MSP in the programme's second phase. GIS zoning for commercial use, livelihoods and conservation; MoU with NCCR (MoES) and Norway's support under the India–Norway ocean initiative. Phase 1 covered Puducherry and Lakshadweep.
  • New Zealand's Māori aquaculture model: guarantees Māori co-governance rights and a share of benefits from marine aquaculture, a global benchmark for indigenous participation.

Government initiatives

  • PM Matsya Sampada Yojana (PMMSY): blue revolution; has a central sector component and a centrally sponsored component. Aims at production and productivity, post-harvest infrastructure, fisher welfare and social security, and technology.
  • PM Matsya Kisan Samridhi Sah-Yojana (PM-MKSSY): formalisation of the sector through a work-based digital identity for fishers on the National Fisheries Digital Platform. This mirrors the activity-based identification idea from Part 1.
  • Technology: harbour modernisation, post-harvest infrastructure and cold chains; mariculture (sea farming, e.g., cage culture); traceability and quality.
  • Market models: "catch-to-customer" or "shore-to-door" through e-commerce.
  • Recirculating Aquaculture Systems (RAS): wastewater is treated and recirculated, saving water PT.

Way forward 1:05:21

  • Combine fishers' traditional knowledge with satellite weather forecasting and remote sensing.
  • Eco-labelling, e.g., for Lakshadweep's pole-and-line (and hand-line) tuna. It is sustainable with no by-catch, earns premium prices, and traceability assures quality. The Centre announced in Sep 2025 that it would seek a global eco-label (MSC-type); not yet certified.
  • Market diversification beyond the US.
  • Reservoir-based cluster value chains, with post-harvest facilities near the source.
  • Community co-management (the Māori model) and access-and-benefit-sharing royalties for fishers.

Glossary — quick revision

Legibility (James C. Scott)
The state's simplification of society into grids; it excludes those who don't fit.
Asymmetric federalism
Different units have different relationships with the Centre.
Dialogic federalism
The Union and states negotiate shared decisions (GST Council).
Identity vs efficiency federalism
Protecting distinctiveness vs decentralising for better governance.
Centripetal vs centrifugal era
Pre-Bommai pro-Centre vs post-Bommai pro-state jurisprudence.
Amphibian federalism
Federal in normal times, unitary in emergencies.
Sui generis
Unique, in a class of its own.
Symbolic autonomy
An elected body without real powers.
Blue-green revolution
Fisheries productivity plus sustainability.
By-catch
Unintended, non-target species caught and often discarded.
Mariculture
Sea farming, e.g., cage culture.
Marine Spatial Planning
GIS-based zoning of sea uses.
Eco-labelling
Certification of sustainable catch that earns a price premium.
