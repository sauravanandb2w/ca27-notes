1. Topic 1: Mineral rights, taxes & the fiscal-federalism problem

2:46Suggested answer structure for this topic: introduction, context, key terms/concepts, constitutional framework, important judgments, legal framework/amendment act, debates, data, need for reforms, challenges, case studies (domestic and global), way forward, conclusion.

3:24Introduction framing suggested in class: fiscal federalism is tested where a resource is nationally regulated but locally born. Minerals are exactly such a resource.

  • With the passing of the MMDR Amendment Act, the centre-state contest over the fiscal benefits of mineral wealth was brought once again to a constitutional flash point.
  • This topic is relevant across all three GS papers.
    • GS-1: UPSC has previously asked a related question here
    • GS-2: policy, governance, and social-justice dimensions (tribal/vulnerable communities on mineral land)
    • GS-3: economy and industrial-economy dimension

Context cited

  • Parliament passed amendments to the MMDR Act, 1957.
  • The main feature of the amendment: it restricts state powers to impose levies on mineral resources.
  • Several states — the lecture named Jharkhand, Karnataka, and Kerala — decided to challenge the constitutionality of the amendment act (confirmed: Congress-ruled Karnataka, Kerala, and Telangana are jointly preparing the Supreme Court challenge, with Congress separately working to bring Jharkhand on board — see Supplementary research).
  • Instructor's added point: the bill was reportedly passed without extensive floor discussion in Parliament, in an urgent manner.
    • Usable as an example of weak parliamentary scrutiny of bills
    • Also usable as an example of Centre-state friction
    • Instructor floated the concept of a "Legislative Impact Assessment" (LIA) — assessing a law's likely impact before enactment, to reduce the risk of the law immediately being challenged in court

Key concept: fiscal federalism

Fiscal federalism (definition used in class):
  • The constitutional and functional mechanism through which revenue-sharing powers and expenditure responsibilities are allocated between different tiers of government.

Mineral rights — three distinct legal aspects

  • Mining activity: the extraction of minerals.
  • Mineral: the raw ore itself once extracted.
  • Mineral-bearing land (MBL): the land in which the mineral is held.

Major vs. minor minerals

  • Minor minerals: specifically listed in the appendix/schedule of the MMDR Act (around 50-51 minerals, e.g. sand).
    • Regulated by states, not by the Centre
  • Major minerals: not specifically listed in the Act's minor-minerals appendix (e.g. coal, iron ore).
    • Regulated by the Centre

Taxes, fees and levies on minerals

  • Auction premium: the winning bid amount paid to be allocated a mineral block.
  • Royalty: a percentage of the ad valorem (value-based) amount paid to the state government for every unit of mineral extracted.
  • Tax on MBL: a land tax imposed on the mineral-bearing land itself.
  • Other levies and charges: according to the central government, around 14 different such levies/charges are imposed by states, including GST.
Tax vs. levy vs. fee/charge (distinctions drawn in class):
  • Tax: a mandatory legal payment for a general purpose. The payer gets nothing specific in return; government spends it for general purposes.
  • Levy: an umbrella term for any mandatory payment, imposed for a more specific purpose than a general tax but still compulsory once imposed.
  • Fee/charge: a payment made for a specific purpose or service in return (e.g. a toll fee in exchange for permission to travel a route for a period).

The cascading-tax problem — cause and effect chain

  • Multiple layered taxes/levies (auction premium, royalty, land tax, transit charges, GST, statutory contributions) cascade on top of each other.
  • Cascading raises the effective cost of the extracted mineral/ore.
  • Higher domestic mineral cost reduces India's cost-competitiveness against imported minerals.
  • Industries (steel, cement, etc.) prefer importing cheaper minerals over buying costlier domestic ore.
  • Reduced demand for domestic ore affects the primary (mining) sector itself.
  • Weakness in the primary sector feeds through to lower overall industrial growth and economic development.

Statutory payments — DMF and NMET

District Mineral Foundation (DMF) Trust — Section 9B, MMDR Act:
  • A non-profit trust set up in every district affected by the negative externalities of mining.
  • Objective: to work for the benefit of people affected by mining (an equity-oriented instrument).
  • Contribution rate: 10% of royalty paid, for mining leases granted after the 2015 MMDR amendment.
  • Contribution rate: 30% of royalty paid, for "grandfathered" leases granted before 2015 (mainly coal mines).
National Mineral Exploration Trust (NMET) — Section 9C, MMDR Act:
  • Funds exploration research to identify new mineral-bearing blocks.
  • Private investment in exploration is limited because the research may not find anything usable, so the Centre funds this activity instead.
  • Contribution rate: around 2% of royalty paid.
  • More blocks found through this research can then be auctioned, increasing production.
  • Overall money flow: of all the taxes, levies, and statutory contributions discussed, roughly 90-95% flows to the state where the mineral is located; the Centre retains only a small share (chiefly the ~2% NMET contribution) for exploration-research activity.
The "mining activity / mineral / mineral-bearing land" three-way split is the base unit for this entire topic — every subsequent constitutional entry (49, 50, 54) and every case law dispute maps onto which of these three the tax is actually being levied on.

2. Topic 1: Constitutional framework & case law timeline

Constitutional entries in play

  • Entry 54, Union List: regulation of mines and mineral development.
    • Parliament used this entry to enact the MMDR Act, 1957, assuming overall regulatory control of the mining sector.
  • Entry 50, State List: taxes on mineral rights.
    • Explicitly made "subject to any limitations imposed by Parliament by law" — i.e., subject to Entry 54.
    • Effect: states have the power to tax mineral rights, but only within limits Parliament sets under its regulatory (Entry 54) power.
  • Entry 49, State List: general taxes on land and buildings.
    • States separately argued that mineral-bearing land is simply "land," and used Entry 49 to justify taxing it as land — layering an additional charge on top of Entry-50-based mineral-rights tax.
Frame this constitutional position as a checks-and-balances design: Entry 54 gives the Centre regulatory primacy; Entry 50 gives states a taxing power but keeps it subordinate to Parliament's law; Entry 49 is a general land-tax power states tried to stretch to cover MBL.

Case law timeline

India Cement Ltd. v. State of Tamil Nadu (1989, seven-judge bench):
  • Question of law: whether states have any power to charge royalty, cess, or similar taxes on mineral rights.
  • Held: royalty is a tax.
  • Consequence: since royalty is regulated by the Centre-enacted MMDR Act, states were held to lack legislative competence to tax mineral rights at all.
  • Net effect: states were almost completely barred from imposing any mineral-related tax.
State of West Bengal v. Kesoram Industries Ltd. (2004):
  • The 1989 position was challenged again.
  • Held: royalty is not a tax.
  • Consequence: states regained the power to tax mineral rights, creating a conflicting-authority situation (both Centre and states now claiming taxing power over the same activity).
  • States began actively imposing new mineral-related taxes following this judgment.
Mineral Area Development Authority v. Steel Authority of India (MADA, 2024, nine-judge bench, 8:1 majority):
  • Question of law: to conclusively settle the conflicting Centre-state taxing powers left unresolved since 1989/2004.
  • Held: royalty is not a tax (overruling India Cement Ltd., 1989/1990).
  • Held: states possess legislative competence to tax mineral rights under Entries 49 and 50 of the State List, subject to any limitation Parliament imposes under Entry 54.
  • Also permitted states to recover past unpaid dues retrospectively (later clarified: dues recoverable only from 1 April 2005 onward, with payment staggered in installments over 12 years starting 1 April 2026).
  • Consequence flagged in class: this retrospective-recovery permission, layered on top of the existing cascading-tax problem, was criticized as a form of "tax terrorism," and was seen as a trigger for a sharp fall in mining-sector investment and foreign investment.
  • Justice B.V. Nagarathna dissented from the majority.
M.C. Mehta v. Kamal Nath (1996-97) — Public Trust Doctrine (used as a general conclusion-worthy case, not specific to mining):
  • Supreme Court held that natural resources are held by the state as a trustee on behalf of the people.
  • The state cannot permit private appropriation of a natural resource where that appropriation harms the public interest.
  • The state's trusteeship duty runs to both the present generation and future generations.
  • Instructor's framing: usable as a safe, generic conclusion case for any answer involving natural resources, public interest, or sustainability.
Case-law memory chain: India Cement (1989) says royalty IS tax, states lose power → Kesoram Industries (2004) says royalty is NOT tax, states regain power → MADA (2024) finally settles it: royalty is NOT tax, states keep the power (subject to Parliament), but the retrospective-recovery part of this ruling is exactly what the MMDR Amendment Act 2026 later claws back.

3. Topic 1: MMDR Amendment Act 2026, debates & live answer-writing exercise

Key provisions of the MMDR Amendment Act 2026

  • Section 9D(1): prohibits state governments from imposing any tax, cess, or other levy — by whatever name — on mineral rights or mineral-bearing land.
    • Covers levies based on mineral quantity, mineral value, royalty, or any other basis.
    • States may impose such levies only within conditions/restrictions the Central Government prescribes.
  • Section 9D(2): nullifies any levy that was not deposited with, or recovered by, a state before the amendment's commencement.
    • Amounts already deposited or recovered before commencement are not required to be refunded.
    • Instructor's key inference: this creates a fairness problem — mining companies that already paid their dues get no relief, while companies that had not yet paid are let off entirely. This is framed in class as "rewarding the non-payers."
  • Section 2 amendment: declares explicit Union control over mineral-bearing land, closing off the Entry-49 "land tax" route states had used.
  • The amendment also delegates additional rule-making (subordinate legislative) powers to the Centre.
Effect in one line: the Act legislatively reverses the fiscal-federalism gains states had just won in the 2024 MADA judgment — hence the instructor's framing of it as "neutralizing" or "overruling" MADA through ordinary legislation rather than a constitutional amendment.

Debate 1 — the Laffer curve argument

  • Laffer curve, defined in class: an inverted-U relationship between the tax rate and total tax revenue.
    • At 0% tax rate, revenue is zero.
    • At 100% tax rate, revenue is also zero (nobody wants to earn if all of it is taken away, or economic activity/capital simply migrates elsewhere).
    • An optimum tax rate exists between these two zeros, cited in class as roughly 35-40%.
    • Beyond roughly 45%, the tax rate enters a "prohibitive" zone where further rate increases start reducing, not raising, total revenue.
  • Applied to Indian mining: the effective mining tax rate in India is cited in class as over 50%, versus roughly 35-40% in other mineral-producing countries.
    • Because India sits in the "prohibitive" zone, mining capital tends to migrate to lower-tax jurisdictions instead of investing domestically.
      • This migration is easy to execute because mining capital is highly mobile.
    • Other consequences flagged: incentive for illegal mining, and incentive to import rather than produce domestically.
  • State-level illustration — levy maximization vs. production maximization:
    • Jharkhand imposed a new cess on mineral-bearing land; its mining revenue subsequently fell short of target — a "levy maximization" approach that backfired, consistent with the Laffer-curve logic.
    • Odisha did not impose new levies, and instead focused on auctioning more mineral blocks to raise overall production — a "production maximization" approach.
    • Instructor's framing: production maximization is the better strategy of the two.

Debate 2 — shared inheritance vs. unsustainable exploitation

  • The National Mineral Policy frames minerals as a "shared inheritance" belonging to all generations, present and future — echoing the public trust doctrine from M.C. Mehta v. Kamal Nath.
  • Since minerals are finite and non-renewable, exploration and production need to be sustainable.
  • Concern: if the MMDR Amendment Act's investment-facilitation push leads to faster, less regulated extraction, it risks becoming unsustainable exploitation, in tension with the shared-inheritance principle.

Debate 3 — geographical asymmetry of costs and benefits

  • Benefits of mining flow "outward and upward":
    • Outward: to the wider economy and connected industries (steel, cement, etc.).
    • Upward: to mining companies, their shareholders, and owners.
  • Costs of mining stay local and concentrated:
    • Development-induced displacement, with often inadequate rehabilitation.
    • Ecological degradation and pollution.
    • Health impacts on the local population.
    • In India, mineral-bearing land is disproportionately occupied by tribal and other vulnerable communities, sharpening the equity concern.
  • Related concept — resource curse: resource-rich states/regions can face de-industrialization because heavy reliance on resource revenue crowds out other forms of development. Jharkhand's reliance on mining for a very large share of its non-tax revenue is cited as an example (confirmed at 84.9% for FY 2024-25 — see Supplementary research).

Debate 4 — cooperative federalism vs. legislative imperialism

  • Cooperative federalism (the ideal): Centre and states act as partners, coordinating to reach a shared goal.
  • Legislative imperialism (the criticism of the 2026 Act): a top-down law imposed on states without their consent, cutting against the cooperative-federalism ideal.

Debate 5 — fiscal certainty for industry vs. fiscal autonomy for states

  • Industry's demand — certainty and predictability:
    • Predictable, non-retrospective tax regimes attract investment.
    • Comparison drawn in class to the telecom sector's AGR-dues dispute — described as a prior instance of "tax terrorism" that damaged investor confidence and contributed to the sector becoming a duopoly.
  • States' demand — fiscal autonomy:
    • States already lost most independent taxation power with the rollout of GST.
    • The MMDR Amendment Act removes their remaining Entry 49/50-based mineral taxation power, further eroding fiscal autonomy.

Debate 6 — uniformity/rationalization vs. local-needs-based taxation

  • Pro-uniformity view: a single uniform tax rate and rationalized levy structure across India (varying only by auction-determined block quality) attracts more investment — a "one-size-fits-all" model.
  • Counter view: a one-size-fits-all model may not suit a diverse country like India, since states understand and should be allowed to tax based on their own local requirements.

Counter-argument to the "fiscal federalism is destroyed" criticism

  • Roughly 90-95% of all mining-related money still ultimately flows to the state where the mineral is located (see Topic 1, Part 1).
  • The Centre's own retained share is small (largely the ~2% NMET contribution for exploration research).
  • On this reading, the Centre's post-2026 role is mainly regulatory (setting auction terms, conducting the auction, prescribing conditions) rather than direct fund capture.

Key data cited in class

Federation of Indian Mineral Industries (FIMI): India's effective mining tax rate is over 50% of revenues, versus roughly 35-40% in other major mineral-producing countries
Ministry of Mines: India holds over 1,500 mineral blocks, but mining contributes only around 2% of GDP
Mining's share of GDP is around 8-10% in Australia, South Africa, and Canada
Jharkhand: mining accounted for 84.9% of the state's own non-tax revenue in FY 2024-25 (confirmed — see Supplementary research)
India imported minerals worth around Rs 10 lakh crore in FY 2025-26 (figure recurs across secondary current-affairs sources citing a Ministry of Mines statement, but could not be traced to a primary document in the time available — treat as indicative, verify before citing precisely; see Supplementary research)

Challenges/concerns with the 2026 Amendment

  • Legislatively neutralizes the Supreme Court's 2024 MADA verdict.
  • Weakens fiscal federalism by removing states' Entry 49/50 taxing powers over minerals.
  • Effectively "rewards non-payers" by nullifying uncollected past dues, raising a fairness problem between compliant and non-compliant mining companies.
  • Does not resolve the underlying geographical-equity problem (local costs, national/global benefits).
  • Suggestion floated in class: a compensatory mechanism for revenue-losing states, modeled on the GST compensation-grant approach used during GST's rollout.

Best practice and global comparative models

Odisha — Budget Stabilization Fund (domestic best practice):
  • Odisha sets aside a portion of its mining-revenue surplus every year into a dedicated Budget Stabilization Fund.
  • This is a counter-cyclical fiscal policy: build a buffer in good years, draw on it when mining revenue dips.
  • Cited in class as a contributing factor to Odisha's strong ranking on a state fiscal-health index.
Indonesia — export restriction to force domestic processing:
  • Restricted/banned the export of unprocessed raw ore, mainly nickel.
  • Objective: compel domestic smelting/processing investment rather than exporting raw material.
Ghana — local benefit-sharing model ("Kana" in the auto-transcript — see Supplementary research):
  • Uses a statutory formula to share mining royalties directly with traditional mining communities and district assemblies.
Canada — provincial fiscal-autonomy model:
  • The Canadian constitutional design gives provinces authority over natural resources located within them.

Way forward

  • Institutionalize a consultative rate-setting mechanism between Centre and states — a body similar to the GST Council was suggested as a model.
  • Build a compensatory, revenue-neutral mechanism for states facing fiscal loss, similar to the GST compensation-grant model.
  • Shift the underlying policy philosophy from "levy maximization" to "production maximization" (per the Laffer-curve logic and the Jharkhand/Odisha contrast above).

4. Topic 2: SCO — organization, objectives & key terms

1:22:19Second topic: the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO), on completing 25 years since its founding.

1:22:27Suggested answer structure for grouping/organisation-type questions: introduction, context, concepts/objectives/key terms, data/significance, challenges, India-vs-China approaches, quotes, way forward, conclusion.

Introduction framing (transferable pattern for any India-linked grouping)

  • Whenever a question involves a grouping where India has a prominent presence, link that grouping explicitly to an India foreign-policy label in the introduction itself.
  • SCO: "a vital anchor for India's foreign policy of multi-alignment and its extended-neighbourhood outreach."
  • BRICS (comparison given in class): "a vital anchor of India's Global South leadership and multi-alignment."
  • SAARC/BIMSTEC (comparison given in class): "a vital foreign policy anchor of India's Neighbourhood First policy."

Context cited

  • SCO completed 25 years of its founding (established 2001).
  • The 26th SCO Summit was held in Bishkek, Kyrgyzstan.
  • Summit theme: "25 Years of the SCO: Together for Sustainable Peace, Development and Prosperity."
  • Instructor's currency-relevance point: Afghanistan is separately seeking entry into SCO, and the next summit is scheduled to be hosted by Pakistan — both developments likely to keep SCO in the news through the Mains exam window.
  • SCO membership has expanded twice since India and Pakistan joined.
    • Iran became the ninth full member in July 2023, at the virtual New Delhi Summit.
    • Belarus became the tenth full member in 2024, at the Astana Summit.

What kind of organization is SCO?

  • An intergovernmental, transcontinental (Eurasian) organization.
  • Began as the "Shanghai Five," with the original aim of resolving China's border disputes with Central Asian states after the disintegration of the USSR.
  • It is fundamentally a Chinese initiative — one of the rare instances of China building and leading a multilateral organization.
  • China subsequently extended the grouping's scope beyond its original border-dispute-resolution mandate to advance a broader agenda.

Key objectives — four clusters

  • Fostering mutual trust among member states.
  • Combating the "three evils": terrorism, separatism, and extremism.
    • Operationalized through RATS (Regional Anti-Terrorist Structure), a permanent mechanism based in Tashkent.
  • Regional economic and energy cooperation: enhancing trade and energy connectivity among members.
  • Cultural, scientific, youth, and environmental cooperation.

Economic architecture — financial cooperation mechanisms

  • SCO has proposed two financial-architecture arms to operationalize economic cooperation: an SCO Interbank Consortium and a long-discussed SCO Development Bank.
  • Progress on the Development Bank has stalled for roughly a decade.
  • Disputes center on capitalization, headquarters location, and voting-rights shares among members.
  • India has flagged concerns about disproportionate China-Russia dominance in any such body — the same asymmetric-multilateralism concern raised elsewhere in this topic.

Key analytical terms

Multi-alignment:
  • The practice of maintaining issue-based partnerships across competing geopolitical blocs and countries.
  • Essentially a diversification strategy in foreign policy.
Asymmetric multilateralism:
  • An institutional order that is multilateral in form (i.e., outwardly presented as a grouping of equals) but is fundamentally unequal and hierarchical in practice.
  • Applied to SCO: outwardly a transcontinental, intergovernmental, multilateral organization, but in practice a China-led and China-dominated one.
"Tarmac-and-steel" multilateralism vs. rule-based multilateralism:
  • Tarmac-and-steel model (China's approach):
    • Builds multilateral coalitions by focusing on physical/hard infrastructure.
    • International cooperation is not anchored in shared political ideology or values, but in infrastructure.
    • Typical example: China's Belt and Road Initiative (BRI).
    • Stated governance philosophy: sovereignty and non-interference in domestic affairs.
    • Reported real-world consequence: what critics term "debt-trap" or "checkbook" diplomacy — this is a consequence observed in practice, not a philosophy China itself claims.
  • Rule-based multilateralism (the Western/"Pax Americana" model):
    • Built by Western countries on shared rules, values, and political alignment (e.g., democratic governance).
Use the multi-alignment / asymmetric-multilateralism / tarmac-vs-rule-based trio as a reusable analytical toolkit for any China-led or China-heavy grouping question (SCO, BRI-linked groupings), not just for SCO specifically.

5. Topic 2: SCO — significance, challenges & India's approach

Significance by the numbers

10 full-time member states; India and Pakistan became full members at the 2017 Astana Summit
Around 40% of the world's population
Around 24-25% of the world's land mass
Around 20-25% of combined global GDP
Around 20% of global oil/energy reserves; a large share of global natural gas reserves cited in class (exact gas-reserve percentage unverified — see Supplementary research)
  • Other significance points raised in class:
    • Transcontinental, Eurasian reach gives SCO automatic geopolitical weight, spanning multiple major powers and continents.
    • RATS gives SCO an operational mechanism to combat terrorism, separatism, and extremism — a genuine regional security threat with spillover effects.
    • SCO is an important platform for Eurasian connectivity: China pushes its Belt and Road Initiative through the grouping, while India separately pushes the International North-South Transport Corridor (INSTC).
    • SCO functions as an institutional venue for diplomatic engagement between rival member states (India-Pakistan, India-China) even amid unresolved border disputes.

Challenges — the "virus of conflict" framing

Instructor's note: UPSC has previously asked a question framed around SCO being affected by a "virus of conflict" — treat this exact phrase as a reusable hook.

  • Internal conflicts:
    • Bilateral tensions: India-China, India-Pakistan, Iran-Pakistan.
    • Intra-Central-Asian border disputes, e.g. between Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan.
    • Sovereignty conflicts arising from connectivity initiatives, such as the China-Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC) as part of BRI passing through disputed territory.
    • Terrorism, separatism, and extremism spillover affecting multiple members, particularly linked to Afghanistan and Pakistan.
  • External conflicts bleeding into SCO cohesion:

Comparison point — SCO vs. QUAD

  • SCO and QUAD are two different multilateral architectures India belongs to at the same time.
  • SCO is Eurasian, China-led, and land/infrastructure-oriented, run on the "asymmetric multilateralism" logic described earlier in this topic.
  • QUAD (India, US, Japan, Australia) is Indo-Pacific, rule-based, and maritime/security-and-technology-oriented, with no permanent secretariat.
  • India's simultaneous membership in both groupings is itself a concrete illustration of the "multi-alignment" label applied to SCO in this topic's introduction.

Glossary — quick revision

Mineral-bearing land (MBL)
The land in which a mineral is held — one of three legally distinct aspects of mineral rights, alongside the mining activity itself and the mineral (ore) extracted.
DMF (District Mineral Foundation)
A non-profit trust, set up under Section 9B of the MMDR Act in every mining-affected district, funded by 10% (post-2015 leases) or 30% (pre-2015 leases) of royalty, to benefit mining-affected communities.
NMET (National Mineral Exploration Trust)
A Centre-run trust under Section 9C of the MMDR Act, funded by about 2% of royalty, financing exploration research to identify new mineral blocks for auction.
Laffer curve
An inverted-U relationship between tax rate and tax revenue; revenue is zero at both 0% and 100% tax rates, with an optimum rate (cited in class as roughly 35-40%) in between, beyond which further rate increases are counterproductive.
MADA (Mineral Area Development Authority v. Steel Authority of India, 2024)
Nine-judge Supreme Court bench (8:1) holding royalty is not a tax, restoring states' Entry 49/50 taxing power over mineral rights, and permitting retrospective dues recovery (later capped to transactions from 1 April 2005, staggered over 12 years from 1 April 2026). Overruled India Cement Ltd. (1989/1990).
Legislative imperialism
A top-down central law imposed on states without their consent — the criticism levelled at the MMDR Amendment Act 2026, contrasted with the ideal of cooperative federalism.
Multi-alignment
India's practice of maintaining issue-based partnerships across competing geopolitical blocs, rather than aligning fully with any one bloc.
Asymmetric multilateralism
An institutional order that is multilateral in form but unequal and hierarchical in practice — used in class to describe China's dominant role within the outwardly equal-member SCO.
"Tarmac-and-steel" multilateralism
China's model of building multilateral influence through physical/hard infrastructure investment (e.g. Belt and Road Initiative) rather than shared political values — contrasted with Western rule-based multilateralism built on shared rules and ideology.
RATS (Regional Anti-Terrorist Structure)
A permanent SCO mechanism based in Tashkent, operationalizing the objective of combating the "three evils": terrorism, separatism, and extremism.

A — Active Recall: self-test before re-reading

1. Trace the case-law arc that ultimately shaped the MMDR Amendment Act 2026's Section 9D.

India Cement Ltd. v. State of Tamil Nadu (1989, seven-judge bench) held royalty IS a tax, barring states from taxing mineral rights. State of West Bengal v. Kesoram Industries (2004) reversed this, holding royalty is NOT a tax, letting states tax again. Mineral Area Development Authority v. Steel Authority of India (MADA, 2024, nine-judge bench, 8:1) settled the conflict: royalty is not a tax, states retain Entry 49/50 taxing power subject to Parliament's Entry 54 limits, and states could recover dues retrospectively (later capped to post-1 April-2005 transactions, staggered over 12 years from 2026). The MMDR Amendment Act 2026's Section 9D then legislatively reversed the states' MADA-era gains by barring state taxes on mineral rights/MBL and nullifying uncollected past dues.

2. What is the Laffer curve, and how was it applied to India's mining sector in this lecture?

The Laffer curve is an inverted-U relationship between tax rate and tax revenue: revenue is zero at both 0% and 100% tax rates, with an optimum (cited as roughly 35-40%) in between. India's effective mining tax rate was cited as over 50%, placing it in a "prohibitive" zone. Consequence: mining capital, being highly mobile, migrates to lower-tax jurisdictions, reducing domestic investment; also creates incentives for illegal mining and import substitution over domestic production. Jharkhand's new cess undershooting revenue targets, versus Odisha's production-maximization approach, were given as contrasting real-world illustrations.

3. What are DMF and NMET, and how are they funded?

DMF (District Mineral Foundation), under Section 9B of the MMDR Act, is a non-profit trust in every mining-affected district, funded by 10% of royalty (post-2015 leases) or 30% (pre-2015 leases), aimed at benefiting mining-affected communities. NMET (National Mineral Exploration Trust), under Section 9C, is funded by about 2% of royalty and finances exploration research to identify new mineral blocks, since private investors are reluctant to fund exploration with uncertain returns.

4. What are the "three evils" that SCO's RATS mechanism combats, and where is RATS based?

Terrorism, separatism, and extremism. RATS (Regional Anti-Terrorist Structure) is a permanent SCO mechanism based in Tashkent, Uzbekistan.

5. What is "asymmetric multilateralism," and how does it apply to SCO?

Asymmetric multilateralism describes an institutional order that is multilateral in form (outwardly a grouping of equal member states) but is fundamentally unequal and hierarchical in practice. Applied to SCO: although structured as a transcontinental, intergovernmental multilateral organization, it is in practice a China-led and China-dominated grouping, given China's outsized economic and trade weight and its role as the organization's original founder and driving force.

Supplementary research — filling gaps

MMDR Amendment Act 2026 — verified

Confirmed: the President assented to the Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Amendment Act, 2026 on 17 August 2026. Section 9D bars state governments from imposing any tax, cess, or other levy on mineral rights or mineral-bearing land, covering levies based on mineral quantity, value, royalty, or any other basis; states may only levy such charges within Centre-prescribed conditions. Levies not deposited or recovered by a state before the amendment's commencement are nullified, but amounts already collected are not refundable. The amendment is widely described as an attempt to claw back ground the Union lost in the Supreme Court's July 2024 mineral-royalty verdict. Source: SCC Online explainer; Drishti IAS; PRS Legislative Research, bill text.

Mineral Area Development Authority v. Steel Authority of India (MADA, 2024) — verified

Confirmed: on 25 July 2024, a nine-judge Supreme Court bench headed by then-CJI D.Y. Chandrachud held, 8:1 (Justice B.V. Nagarathna dissenting), that royalty under the MMDR Act is not a tax but a contractual consideration for mineral-extraction rights, and that states' power to tax mineral rights under Entry 50 is not limited by the royalty Parliament imposes. This overruled the seven-judge India Cement Ltd. v. State of Tamil Nadu ruling (decided 25 October 1989, sometimes cited as "1990" in secondary sources). The Court later clarified retrospective application would not apply to transactions before 1 April 2005, with dues payable in installments over 12 years from 1 April 2026. Source: AZB Partners; Mondaq.

M.C. Mehta v. Kamal Nath — verified, nuance added

Confirmed: the principal Supreme Court judgment was delivered on 13 December 1996 (with a related compensation order in 2000), arising from forest land near the Beas river in Himachal Pradesh being allotted to a private resort (Span Motels). The Court applied the Public Trust Doctrine, holding natural resources are held by the state in trust for the public, present and future generations, and cannot be transferred for private gain where this harms ecological balance. The lease was cancelled and restoration/compensation ordered. Source: Case overview; Indian Kanoon, full judgment.

DMF and NMET contribution rates — verified

Confirmed: District Mineral Foundations, set up under Section 9B (inserted by the MMDR Amendment Act, 2015), receive 10% of royalty for leases granted after the 2015 amendment and 30% of royalty for leases granted before it. The National Mineral Exploration Trust, under Section 9C, receives 2% of royalty. Source: PMF IAS, DMF explainer; NMET, MMDR Amendment Act 2015 text.

FIMI effective mining tax rate — verified, figures refined

Confirmed direction and rough magnitude: industry body FIMI (Federation of Indian Mineral Industries) has long characterized India's mining sector as among the highest-taxed in the world. More precise figures found: an effective tax rate of around 54% for existing mines and 58% for new auction-route mines (including corporate tax, DMF, and NMET), compared with roughly 31.3% in Mongolia, 37.6% in Chile, 39.7% in Australia, 39.7% in South Africa, and 44.2% in Namibia. The lecture's rounder figures ("over 50%" vs. "35-40%") are directionally accurate; cite the more precise comparator figures above if exact numbers are demanded. Source: Business Standard; FIMI note on mining taxation.

26th SCO Summit, Bishkek 2026 — verified
