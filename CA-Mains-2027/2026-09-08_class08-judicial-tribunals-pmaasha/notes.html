1. Topic 1: Judicial appointments — significance, case law & global comparisons

0:00Class opens by directly continuing Class 7's judicial-appointments/collegium discussion, which had ended mid-topic.

Significance of judicial reforms

  • Democratic legitimacy.
    • Cited: Lord Hewart's dictum — justice must not only be done but must also be seen to be done
    • An ultimate "currency" of counter-majoritarian institutions like the judiciary is public trust
    • Judicial independence is necessary to secure this trust
    • Judicial accountability is equally necessary to secure this trust
  • Checks and balances.
    • India's separation-of-powers doctrine rests on checks and balances, not a rigid separation
    • Requires a distribution of power
    • Requires mutual control and accountability between organs
  • Constitutional conscience / diversity.
    • Diversity in appointments strengthens constitutional conscience
    • Specific gap flagged in class: greater women's representation is needed in India's higher judiciary
    • A live debate exists on regional benches of the Supreme Court, to improve access/representation
  • Preserving judicial/constitutional morality.
    • The Supreme Court, as guardian of the Constitution, must itself protect constitutional morality
    • This in turn protects judicial morality — impartiality, accountability, restraint, integrity

Key judgments beyond the four Judges Cases

CPIO, Supreme Court of India v. Subhash Chandra Agarwal (13 November 2019):
  • Question: does the office of the Chief Justice of India fall under the RTI Act?
  • The matter had earlier gone to the Delhi High Court, where the Supreme Court's registry had argued the CJI's office should not fall under RTI
  • Supreme Court ultimately held the CJI's office does fall under the RTI Act
  • Key observation cited in class: judicial independence is not secured by the secrecy of "cloistered halls"
  • Court held: increased transparency does not, by itself, threaten judicial independence
The "Media One" judgment (2023) — sealed-cover jurisprudence:
  • Arose when the government submitted its reasons for a security-based telecast ban on a Malayalam news channel (MediaOne) to the court in a sealed cover
  • The channel itself was not shown the sealed-cover contents
  • Instructor's framing: this violates natural justice, since a party cannot meaningfully oppose a case it is not permitted to see
  • Court held: "sealed cover" secrecy is antithetical to a transparent and accountable system
  • Instructor's critique: the Court itself does not consistently apply this transparency principle to its own judicial-appointments process

Insider criticism of the collegium

  • Fali S. Nariman — credited in class as an architect/early supporter of the collegium system.
    • Later criticized it as having become "not receptive at all" to inputs and scrutiny from the Bar
  • Justice Ruma Pal (referenced, continuing from Class 7) — described the collegium as "the best-kept secret" of the country.

A domestic best-practice example

  • Kerala High Court's model cited as a positive practice: reportedly over 90% compliance rate in voluntary asset disclosure among its judges.

Comparison with UK's Judicial Appointments Commission (JAC)

FeatureIndia (collegium)UK (Judicial Appointments Commission)
Origin Judicially evolved through the First, Second and Third Judges Cases
  • Not mentioned anywhere in the Constitution's text
  • Not created by any ordinary legislation either
 A statutory body created by the Constitutional Reforms Act, 2005
  • Has clear legal sanction
Composition CJI plus the four senior-most Supreme Court judges
  • Composed entirely of judges
 A diverse, 15-member body
  • Includes judicial members
  • Includes members from the Bar/advocates
  • Includes independent lay persons
  • Chair of the JAC must itself be a lay member (not a judge or lawyer)
Transparency Closed-door meetings
  • Described in class as "completely opaque"
 Public advertisement of judicial and tribunal vacancies
  • JAC handles both judiciary and tribunal appointments, unlike the collegium, which handles only higher-judiciary appointments
Judicial independence / executive role Evolved specifically to secure judicial independence, recognized as basic structure
  • Final formal appointing authority remains the President/executive
  • Memorandum of Procedure (MoP) has not been reformed since the Third Judges Case, a live point of friction
 A statutory independent body — prevents even parliamentary interference, despite UK's parliamentary supremacy
  • Formal appointment is via the Crown, which plays only a ceremonial/nominal role
Eligibility Article 124(3): bench route, bar route (10 years' practice), and distinguished-jurist route (legal scholars/academicians) Allows academic legal scholars to become judges too, though without a formally separate "distinguished jurist" category
On "why does the UK keep the monarchy despite being a democracy" tangents: the class framing was that the Crown today has no real power and exists mainly as continuity/tradition, which is precisely what allows the UK to enjoy near-absolute parliamentary supremacy. Useful only as a side-comparison point, not a standalone answer topic.

Comparison with USA's judicial appointment model

FeatureIndiaUSA
Origin/constitutional basis Article 124 Article II, Section 2 of the US Constitution
Appointing process President appoints (originally "in consultation with" the CJI); no formal legislative role President nominates; requires confirmation by the US Senate, via its Judiciary Committee
Political-patronage risk Executive influence exists, but the collegium system was built specifically to check it If the President's party also controls the Senate majority, confirmation becomes easy
  • This creates a structural channel for political patronage
  • Example cited: judicial rulings aligning with a party's policy positions, e.g. abortion-rights rulings
Transparency Closed-door, opaque Highly publicized, televised Senate Judiciary Committee hearings — brings transparency even though nomination itself is not fully insulated from politics
Eligibility Bench, Bar, or distinguished-jurist routes under Article 124(3) No separate formal categories, but legal scholars can also be appointed — considered similarly diverse
Term of office Fixed retirement age of 65 years Lifetime appointment, subject to "good behaviour" (removable only via impeachment)
  • Instructor's inference on the US lifetime-tenure model:
    • A President serves a maximum of two terms (up to 8 years)
    • Within that window, a President can appoint judges who then remain in office for 20-30+ years
    • This entrenches a particular political/ideological orientation on the bench well beyond that President's own term
    • This is the biggest criticism of the US model: it risks long-term political patronage affecting judicial independence

2. Topic 1: Suggestions & live answer-writing exercise

Suggestions for reforming India's system

  • Activate the "distinguished jurist" route under Article 124(3), as suggested by Justice Ujjal Bhuyan (transcribed unclearly as "Justice Buan"; identified via Supplementary research), speaking at NLU Delhi's convocation, 30 August 2026.
    • Would allow elevation of academic scholars/legal experts directly to the bench
    • Current practical obstacle: Bar Council rules generally prohibit legal academics from practising law, creating friction with this route
  • Establish a permanent collegium secretariat.
    • Would systematically track vacancies
    • Would publish eligibility criteria
    • Would publish candidate profiles
    • Present system, by contrast, is largely ad hoc with no such standing mechanism
  • A reformed National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) model.
    • The original NJAC (99th Constitutional Amendment) was struck down in the Fourth Judges Case for undermining judicial pre-eminence (three judicial + three non-judicial members, diluting judicial primacy)
    • Instructor's suggestion: a reformed version modeled on the National Tribunals Commission (NTC) — discussed later in Topic 2 — could preserve judicial pre-eminence while still broadening composition
  • Scrutiny safeguards: screening of candidates for relatives already in the system ("uncle judges" concern, from Class 7), and publicly defined selection criteria.

Live answer-writing exercise (timed, in-class — Previous Year Question, 2024 Mains)

Discuss the evolution of the collegium system in India. Critically examine the advantages and disadvantages of the system of appointment of judges of the Supreme Court of India and that of the USA.
— Direct PYQ (2024 Mains), used for a full timed writing exercise in class

31:00Suggested structure discussed in class:

  • Introduction: since this is clearly a judicial-appointments question, cite Article 124 directly and state the original constitutional text (President appoints "in consultation with" the CJI).
  • Evolution:
    • 1980s: constitutional text as written, executive primacy in practice
    • First Judges Case (1981): Court held consultation does not mean concurrence — executive primacy continues
    • Second Judges Case (1993): Court reversed this — consultation now means concurrence, collegium is born
    • Third Judges Case (1998): the collegium is institutionalized (CJI plus four senior-most judges)
    • Conclusion of this section: this is a judicial evolution, not a constitutional or ordinary-legislative mechanism
  • Advantages and disadvantages — use a table, India vs. USA:
    • India's advantages: judicial independence, protects candidate dignity from a public/politicized confirmation battle
    • India's challenges: "structural asymmetry" on transparency — the same judiciary that in Media One held sealed-cover secrecy antithetical to accountability keeps its own appointments process fully opaque
    • USA's advantages: legislative role (Senate) provides a check; publicized, televised hearings bring transparency; can appoint legal scholars too
    • USA's challenges: political patronage risk via lifetime tenure and Senate-majority alignment, discussed above
  • Way forward: reference to the UK's Judicial Appointments Commission as a comparatively stronger model — an independent statutory body with diverse composition.
  • Conclusion: tie back to a quote on transparency or judicial independence (e.g. Lord Hewart's dictum: justice must not only be done, but be seen to be done) and note that digital transparency in appointments is a pressing need.

3. Topic 2: Tribunals — rationale, data & evolution

37:19Instructor's outline for this topic: constitutional/legal framework, rationale, data, challenges, key judgments (framed as an executive-vs-judiciary debate), the Tribunal Reforms Act 2026, its provisions and views on it, global best practice (UK's JAC again), and suggestions.

40:53Suggested introduction framing: "The tribunal system in India is a constitutional instrument, established to infuse specialization and speed into dispute resolution. The enactment of the Tribunal Reforms Act, 2026 marks the latest legislative effort to ensure ease of justice."

Constitutional and legal framework

  • Articles 323A and 323B, inserted by the 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976, via new Part XIVA.
    • Article 323A: deals with administrative tribunals
    • Article 323B: deals with tribunals for other matters (taxation, environment, etc.)
  • Individual tribunals are each established by their own separate substantive law.
    • Example: the National Green Tribunal Act establishes the NGT
    • Example: company law establishes the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT)
    • Example: the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code establishes related tribunal jurisdiction

Judicial admonitions framing the topical context

  • CJI's remark (cited in class): tribunals have become "a liability for the judiciary and a headache for the government."
    • Liability for judiciary: divergent tribunal orders eventually still land back in the regular court system on appeal, defeating the original purpose of reducing judicial burden
  • "No man's land" framing: tribunals have become a zone where no one — tribunal members, government, or the parent ministry — takes real accountability for systemic weaknesses.
  • Template framing: the tribunal-reform model is cited in class as a possible template that could later be adapted for judicial appointments themselves (see the NTC-based NJAC-reform suggestion in Topic 1).

Key data cited in class (government/parliamentary sources)

Tribunal case pendency (as of 2025, per government data): around 5.36 lakh cases pending across various tribunals
Highest individual-tribunal pendency: Debt Recovery Tribunals (DRT), around 2.45 lakh pending cases
Standing Committee on Law and Justice finding: around 95% workforce dependence on contract/temporary staff in the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT)
"State of Tribunals" report, 2025: around Rs 25 lakh crore worth of capital, spread across 3.5 lakh+ cases, locked in tribunal pendency
  • Instructor's framing of the "locked-in capital" problem:
    • Pending disputes (e.g. loan-recovery cases against defaulting companies) keep bank collateral tied up for years
    • Illustrative example cited: Kingfisher Airlines-type stalled recovery cases
    • A delayed auction of a stalled/closed asset typically fetches a lower price than an earlier auction of the same asset would have (asset value/condition degrades with time)
    • This directly undermines ease of doing business, and by extension, investment and economic growth

General positives of the tribunal system

  • Specialization/domain expertise: technical disputes (environment, taxation, insolvency) benefit from adjudicators with specialized knowledge.
  • Procedural flexibility: not bound by strict procedural codes like the CPC or BNSS/BNS; guided by principles of natural justice instead.
  • Speed and efficiency: tribunals are meant to dispose of matters within specified timelines.
    • Example: the NGT is required to dispose of cases within 6 months
  • Cost-effectiveness: litigants can appear personally without mandatory legal representation, reducing financial burden.
  • Unburdening the higher judiciary: tribunals were created to reduce the case load on regular courts.

Concerns with the tribunal system

  • Severe vacancy crisis: vacancy → no quorum → no hearing → rising pendency (a chain repeatedly emphasized in class).
  • No unified service conditions:
    • No consistent salary, tenure, or qualification standards across tribunals
    • Short tenures (originally 3 years) discourage talented candidates from joining
  • Conflict of interest:
    • Parent ministries want control over "their" tribunals
    • Government itself is typically the largest litigant before many tribunals, giving it an incentive to retain control
    • This directly feeds the executive-vs-judiciary tussle discussed below
  • Operational inefficiency: lack of proper infrastructure and permanent staff (NCLT's ~95% contract-staff dependence, cited above).
  • Loss of domain-specific expertise: technical-member positions often remain unfilled, so benches sometimes function without adequate technical input.
  • "Judicial mimicry": tribunal procedures have grown as lengthy and delay-prone as the traditional court system they were meant to be faster than.
  • Gender representation / glass-ceiling issue: qualified women members exist, but face a glass ceiling in recruitment/selection and elevation.
For a "challenges of tribunals" question, the instructor explicitly recommended drawing a flowchart (vacancy → no quorum → no hearing → pendency → operational inefficiency) rather than writing out 6-7 separate prose points, since a flowchart is self-explanatory and saves space under time pressure.

Evolution of the tribunal system — the judiciary-vs-executive tussle, four phases

  • Phase 1 — "Effective substitute" era:
    S.P. Sampath Kumar v. Union of India (1987):
    • Supreme Court upheld the constitutional validity of Articles 323A/323B
    • Held tribunals could act as an effective substitute for High Courts, provided they had equal efficacy and independence
    • At this stage, tribunals sat outside the traditional court hierarchy — no appeal route through High Courts to the Supreme Court
  • Phase 2 — "Supplemental, not supplant" era:
    L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997):
    • Overruled Sampath Kumar
    • Held tribunals function only as supplemental to High Courts, not as substitutes
    • Held tribunal decisions remain subject to judicial review by High Courts
    • Grounded this in judicial review being part of the basic structure of the Constitution
    • Direct appeals from tribunals bypassing the High Court system were struck down
  • Phase 3 — "Independence of tribunals" era (repeated government-vs-Madras Bar Association tussle):
    • Madras Bar Association I (2010): laid down guidelines to ensure judicial independence within tribunals — the debate shifts explicitly to independence from this point
    • Rojer Mathew v. South Indian Bank (2019): struck down the Tribunal Rules, 2017 for lacking judicial independence (excessive executive control over appointments); also struck down the short 3-year tenure prescribed by those rules as discouraging talented candidates
    • Government's response: introduced Tribunal Rules, 2020, extending tenure to 4 years
    • Madras Bar Association IV (2020): struck down the 2020 rules too — tenure change was still inadequate, and the search-cum-selection committee remained bureaucrat-dominated; recommended a National Tribunals Commission (NTC)
    • Government's response: Parliament enacted the Tribunal Reforms Act, 2021
    • Madras Bar Association V: struck down the Tribunal Reforms Act, 2021 as an impermissible "legislative override"
      • Parliament cannot simply re-enact provisions already declared unconstitutional
      • Doing so violates constitutional supremacy
      • Doing so also violates the rule of law
      • Crucially, the Court this time explicitly directed the Union government to establish an NTC within 4 months
Fig. — four-phase tribunal evolution at a glance
Mnemonic used in class: MBA (Madras Bar Association) here has nothing to do with "Master of Business Administration" — but the "business" association is apt anyway, since the case series is fundamentally about ease-of-doing-business harms from tribunal dysfunction. Memorize the repeating cycle: government rule → MBA/Rojer Mathew challenge → Supreme Court strikes down → government tries again → repeat, culminating in the court-mandated NTC.

4. Topic 2: Tribunal Reforms Act 2026, NTC & live answer-writing exercise

National Tribunals Commission (NTC) — composition and functions

NTC: a statutory, permanent body established under the Tribunal Reforms Act, 2026, administering 16 major tribunals.
  • Composition: one chairperson (a retired Supreme Court judge or retired High Court Chief Justice) plus two judicial members (former High Court judges) plus two technical members (with ~25 years' domain experience) — a five-member body
  • Judicial pre-eminence maintained: 3 of 5 members (chairperson + 2 judicial members) have a judicial background; the chairperson also holds a casting vote in case of a tie
  • Functions: recruitment and selection for tribunal vacancies; reviewing performance of tribunal members; overseeing inquiries into complaints; managing the National Tribunals Data Grid (NTDG)

Search-cum-selection committee (SCSC) process

  • Constituted by the NTC whenever a vacancy arises in any of the 16 tribunals (an ad hoc, vacancy-specific mechanism).
  • Recommends only a single name per vacancy, plus one name as a waiting list — a reduction from the earlier practice of forwarding two names, from which the executive picked one.
    • Significance: this reduces executive discretion in the final appointment
  • A government secretary (from the relevant parent ministry), nominated by the central government, is a part of the SCSC — giving the executive a limited but real voice in the process.
  • Strict timeline: the appointment process must be completed within 3 months, intended to resolve the "empty chair" (vacancy-to-pendency) problem.
  • The NTC secretariat proactively tracks upcoming tenure expiries, to begin recruitment before a seat actually falls vacant.
  • Standardized, extended tenure: five years (up from the earlier 3-4 year cycle); retirement age capped at 70 for chairpersons and 67 for members.
  • National Tribunals Data Grid (NTDG): a digital tracker for vacancies, qualifications, and pendency data, intended to bring data-driven accountability.

Positives of the Tribunal Reforms Act, 2026

  • Institutional autonomy for tribunals, since the parent ministry itself is excluded from the NTC (though it retains limited SCSC representation).
  • Judicial pre-eminence preserved via the 3:2 judicial-majority composition and the chairperson's casting vote.
  • Addresses the "empty chair" crisis via the NTC secretariat's proactive tracking and the strict 3-month timeline.
  • Standardized five-year tenure is expected to attract more talented professionals.
  • Data-driven accountability via the NTDG.

Continuing concerns

  • Executive veto retained: final appointment authority still rests with the executive, which can indefinitely delay acting on NTC recommendations — a structural problem the instructor noted also exists in judicial appointments.
  • Residual bureaucratic influence: technical-member representation and the government secretary's SCSC seat still give the bureaucracy a grip on the process.
  • Pre-screening of complaints: complaints against tribunal members are first screened by the parent ministry before NTC inquiry — a "prior sanction"-like filter, echoing the Section 17A debate from Class 7.
  • Delegated-legislation concerns: many operational details (qualifications, service conditions, salary/allowances) are left to rules framed later by the executive, rather than being codified directly in the Act.
  • Lack of fiscal autonomy: the NTC, though statutory, remains financially dependent on government budget allocation.

Statements cited in class

The first threat to tribunal justice is often not a dramatic assault on independence, but an empty chair.
— Attributed in class to a Minister of State (name transcribed as "Jan Chri" — see Supplementary research)
The Act will ensure ease of justice along with ease of doing business.
— Union Minister for Law and Justice Arjun Ram Meghwal (transcribed as "Arjun Mewal"; identified via Supplementary research)

Way forward suggested in class

  • Codify qualifications and service standards directly into the primary legislation, instead of leaving them to delegated legislation/rules.
  • Establish fiscal independence for the NTC, e.g. via direct budget allocation rather than routing through the parent ministry.
  • Improve registry infrastructure and staffing capacity — permanent staff and technology adoption over reliance on contract employees.
  • Mandatory pre-litigation mediation for suitable civil disputes, linked to the Mediation Act, 2023.
  • Judicial impact assessment: evaluate the likely impact of any proposed law on the functioning of courts/tribunals before enacting it (a suggestion traced back to the Rojer Mathew judgment).
  • Continue referencing the UK's Judicial Appointments Commission as the global best-practice benchmark, since it recruits both judges and tribunal members through one open, transparent process.

5. Topic 3: PM-AASHA — components, data & challenges

Note: this topic begins around 1:36:41 and the lecture recording (this segment) ends at 1:59:12, with the instructor deferring the planned live answer-writing exercise to the next class. Notes below cover only what was actually taught in this session.

1:37:09Context: covered because of a PIB article, since scheme-based current affairs from PIB sometimes appear directly in Mains.

1:37:18Suggested introduction framing: "The Green Revolution successfully converted India from a 'ship-to-mouth' country into one of the world's largest producers of various agricultural crops. However, the underlying policy framework remains deeply unbalanced" (a general-purpose introduction usable for any agriculture-policy answer).

Background context

  • Government spends an estimated Rs 2.28 to 2.72 lakh crore annually on food subsidies (exact figure fluctuated in class discussion; see data box) to manage large cereal buffer stocks.
  • This happens even as India continues importing pulses and oilseeds — a policy tension the instructor flagged repeatedly.
  • PM-AASHA (Pradhan Mantri Annadata Aay SanraksHan Abhiyan) launched September 2018, as the government's flagship price-support framework.
    • Objective: guarantee remunerative returns to farmers
    • Objective: simultaneously maintain price stability for consumers

Four components

1. Price Support Scheme (PSS) — producer-centric.
  • Government procurement via agencies like NAFED and NCCF (not FCI, which handles only cereals)
  • Applies mainly to pulses and oilseeds
  • Procurement is not open-ended: capped, ordinarily at 25% of a state's production, extendable up to 30% of national production in some cases
  • Currently 100% procurement applies for urad and masur specifically, reflecting acute need in those crops
2. Price Deficiency Payment Scheme (PDPS) — producer-centric.
  • No physical procurement involved
  • Government pays the difference between MSP and the actual market price directly into the farmer's bank account
  • Capped at a maximum of 15% of the MSP value — government does not cover the full deficiency if the gap exceeds that cap
3. Market Intervention Scheme (MIS) — producer-centric.
  • Targets perishable/horticultural produce not covered under MSP (e.g. tomato, onion, potato)
  • Activated when market price falls more than 10% below the previous normal season's rate
  • Procurement again via NAFED/NCCF; any financial loss from the intervention is shared between the Centre and the state
4. Price Stabilization Fund (PSF) — consumer-centric (the only consumer-facing component of the four).
  • Aim: control food inflation for items like tomato, onion, and potato ("TOP")
  • Model: procure/build buffer stock when there is excess supply at harvest; release into the market when prices spike, to cool prices down
  • Release can also happen via branding/retail channels
Except for PDPS, every PM-AASHA component ultimately depends on physical procurement — which is precisely where the scheme's infrastructure and fiscal constraints bite hardest. This single observation can anchor an entire "challenges" paragraph.

Digitization tools

  • e-Samridhi (developed by NAFED) and e-Samyukti (developed by NCCF): digitize farmer registration and eligibility verification for PM-AASHA payments, so procurement-linked payment can be processed once eligibility is confirmed.

Key data cited (PIB)

PM-AASHA budgetary allocation: approximately Rs 5,400 crore in FY 2024-25, rising to approximately Rs 6,900 crore in FY 2025-26 (a steady year-on-year increase; exact figures given verbally in class and not fully consistent — see Supplementary research)
Actual government procurement under the Price Support Scheme covered only about 2.9% to 12.4% of India's total pulse production over the preceding five-year period cited in class

Significance of PM-AASHA

  • Crop diversification.
    • Cited: Dr. M.S. Swaminathan's framing of India's food-security strategy as resting on two planks — assured price and assured market
    • PM-AASHA extends this logic to non-cereal crops
    • Intended to reduce water-intensive paddy/wheat monocropping
    • Intended to promote pulses, oilseeds, and horticulture instead
  • Addresses the "cobweb phenomenon."
    • Cobweb phenomenon: farmers overproduce in response to a price rise, causing a subsequent price crash; farmers then underproduce in response, causing a subsequent price rise — a self-perpetuating oscillation
    • PM-AASHA's price floor (support) and price ceiling (stabilization) interventions are intended to dampen this cycle
  • Nutritional angle: supports higher pulse production, addressing protein/micronutrient deficiency ("hidden hunger").
  • Reduces import dependence on pulses and oilseeds.
  • Corrects "organized neglect" of non-cereal crops caused by decades of cereal-centric policy (rice/wheat-focused procurement and subsidy).
  • Drives formalization: availing PDPS-type direct benefit payments requires documentation/bank-account linkage, nudging farmers into the formal system (a point echoed from an earlier MSP-related class).
  • Fiscal complementarity: reducing the cereal-subsidy burden is intended to free up resources for infrastructure investment elsewhere.
  • Ecological co-benefit: promoting leguminous/pulse crop rotation alongside other crops supports natural nitrogen fixation in soil.

Challenges

  • Vulnerability to trader-farmer collusion (flagged as a recurring risk, especially in the Price Deficiency Payment Scheme component).
  • Exclusion of tenant farmers: the scheme's beneficiary database is tied to land ownership records, excluding tenant farmers who are often the actual cultivators.
  • Infrastructure risk: three of the four components depend on physical procurement, which in turn depends on storage/warehousing infrastructure that is currently inadequate.
  • Questionable reliability as a market stabilizer: since actual Price Support Scheme procurement covers only ~3-12% of total pulse production, the scheme's real stabilizing effect on farmer incomes is limited.
  • Policy incoherence:
    • Domestic farmers are encouraged to produce more of a crop when prices rise
    • Simultaneously, pre-existing trade agreements (e.g. duty-free pulse-import commitments to specific partner countries, one cited as Mozambique) continue to bring in imports regardless
    • The combination of higher domestic output plus continued imports can itself crash prices — undermining the very price-support objective

Related study/example cited

  • A study by the Indian Council for Research on International Economic Relations (ICRIER) examined a Madhya Pradesh scheme (referenced as "Bhavantar Bhugtan Yojana") and found evidence of trader-farmer collusion — cited as a cautionary precedent for PDPS-type deficiency-payment models.

Way forward suggested in class

  • Formally include tenant farmers/cultivators, not just land-title holders, in scheme eligibility.
  • Establish district- or block-level pulse-processing hubs, to increase shelf life of procured produce.
  • Consider an income-support model (a fixed payment per acre) as a simpler alternative/supplement to price-based interventions.
  • Build a strategic buffer reserve of non-cereal items (pulses, onions, etc.), not just wheat and rice, to protect both producers and consumers.

Revision kit (ARCS)

A — Active Recall: self-test before re-reading

1. What did the Supreme Court hold in CPIO v. Subhash Chandra Agarwal (2019), and why is it in tension with the collegium's own opacity?

The Court held that the office of the Chief Justice of India falls under the RTI Act, and that judicial independence is not secured by the "secrecy of cloistered halls" — increased transparency does not, by itself, threaten judicial independence. The tension: the same judiciary that later (in the 2023 Media One case) held sealed-cover secrecy antithetical to a transparent, accountable system continues to keep its own judicial-appointments process fully closed-door.

2. Contrast India's collegium with the UK's Judicial Appointments Commission on composition and transparency.

India's collegium consists only of the CJI plus the four senior-most Supreme Court judges, meets behind closed doors, and handles only higher-judiciary appointments. The UK's JAC is a 15-member statutory body with a mandatory lay chairperson plus judicial, professional, and lay members; it publicly advertises vacancies and handles both judicial and tribunal appointments, making it more diverse and transparent.

3. Trace the tribunal evolution from Sampath Kumar (1987) to the Tribunal Reforms Act, 2026.

Sampath Kumar (1987): tribunals upheld as an effective substitute for High Courts, given equal efficacy/independence. L. Chandra Kumar (1997): overruled this — tribunals are only supplemental to High Courts, with judicial review by High Courts preserved as basic structure. Madras Bar Association I (2010) onward: focus shifts explicitly to tribunal independence; Rojer Mathew (2019) strikes down the 2017 Tribunal Rules; the 2020 Rules and the Tribunal Reforms Act 2021 are struck down in turn (Madras Bar Association IV and V) as inadequate or as an impermissible "legislative override." The Court explicitly directs the government to create a National Tribunals Commission (NTC), culminating in the Tribunal Reforms Act, 2026.

4. How does the NTC's search-cum-selection committee (SCSC) reduce executive discretion compared to the earlier system, and what discretion still remains with the executive?

The SCSC now recommends only a single name (plus one waiting-list name) per vacancy, versus the earlier practice of forwarding two names for the executive to choose between — reducing executive discretion at the recommendation stage. However, the executive still holds the final appointment veto and can delay acting on NTC recommendations indefinitely, and a government secretary from the parent ministry still sits on the SCSC itself.

5. What is the "cobweb phenomenon" and which PM-AASHA components are designed to counteract it?

It is a self-perpetuating oscillation: a price rise causes farmers to overproduce, crashing prices; the crash causes farmers to underproduce, causing prices to rise again, and the cycle repeats. PM-AASHA's producer-side floor mechanisms (Price Support Scheme, Price Deficiency Payment Scheme, Market Intervention Scheme) support prices when they fall, while the consumer-side Price Stabilization Fund releases buffer stock when prices spike — together intended to dampen both ends of the oscillation.

S — Stick with Encoding Tricks

Tribunal evolution image: a tug-of-war rope between government and the Madras Bar Association (MBA). Government ties a new knot (rules/Act); MBA tugs and the knot slips (struck down); government re-ties; repeat four times, until the referee (Supreme Court) finally forces government to build a permanent umpire's chair — the NTC.

PM-AASHA "3+1" image: three components (PSS, PDPS, MIS) stand on the producer's side of a seesaw, propping farmer income up when prices fall; one component (PSF) stands on the consumer's side, pushing prices back down when they spike. Only PDPS does not require a warehouse (no physical procurement) — everything else needs storage capacity, which is exactly where the scheme's infrastructure problem bites.

Collegium vs. JAC vs. US Senate image: three appointment "gatekeepers" — a closed room with five judges (India); an open hall with 15 mixed members including a lay chair (UK); a televised Senate hearing room (USA). The more open the room, the more transparent the process, but openness itself doesn't guarantee independence — the US room is open yet still exposed to party-political capture via lifetime tenure.

CPIO, Supreme Court of India v. Subhash Chandra Agarwal — verified

Decided 13 November 2019 by a five-judge Constitution Bench (CJI Ranjan Gogoi, Justices N.V. Ramana, D.Y. Chandrachud, Deepak Gupta, Sanjiv Khanna). Confirmed: the Court held the CJI's office falls within the ambit of the RTI Act, and that judicial independence is not incompatible with transparency, though disclosure must be weighed case-by-case against competing public-interest claims (a qualified, not absolute, transparency holding). Source: Indian Kanoon, full judgment; Supreme Court Observer.

"Media One" judgment — verified, case name identified

Decided 5 April 2023 by a bench of CJI D.Y. Chandrachud and Justice Hima Kohli, arising from the Centre's ban on Malayalam channel MediaOne on national-security grounds. Confirmed: sealed-cover proceedings were held to violate natural justice and the right to a fair hearing under Article 21; the Court laid down a "public interest immunity" procedure as a fairer alternative to sealed covers. Source: LiveLaw; SCC Online.

Fali S. Nariman's collegium criticism — verified

Confirmed: senior advocate Fali S. Nariman (widely credited as an early architect/supporter of the collegium system) described it as having become "not receptive at all," citing an episode where his request that the CJI meet Madras High Court lawyers on a judicial issue was declined, and "not even a single collegium member heard them." Source: search results citing Nariman's public remarks (exact original interview/article not independently pinned down in the time available — treat the substance as confirmed but avoid quoting a specific publication date without further checking).

UK Judicial Appointments Commission composition — verified, nuance added

Confirmed under the Constitutional Reform Act, 2005: the JAC has 15 commissioners — 6 judicial members (2 of whom must be tribunal judges), 2 professional (legal) members, 6 lay members total (including the Chair). The Chair must always be a lay member — someone who has never held judicial office and has never practised law — which is a stronger transparency/anti-capture safeguard than the lecture's general "diverse body" framing suggested. Source: Judicial Appointments Commission overview; Constitutional Reform Act 2005, Schedule 12.

Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Act, 2026 — verified, timeline refined

Confirmed: raises Supreme Court sanctioned strength from 34 to 38 judges (including the CJI), by amending the Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Act, 1956. Cabinet approved the bill 5 May 2026; an ordinance was promulgated 16 May 2026; Lok Sabha passed the bill 3 August 2026; Rajya Sabha passed it 12 August 2026; presidential assent followed. Source: Supreme Court Observer; SCC Online.

S.P. Sampath Kumar v. Union of India — verified

Decided 9 December 1986 (also cited as (1987) 1 SCC 124), addressing the constitutional validity of the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985. Confirmed: the Court upheld Articles 323A/323B and held tribunals could serve as an effective substitute for High Courts, but stressed that independence of tribunal chairpersons/members from executive interference was essential for that substitution to hold. Source: Indian Kanoon, full judgment; CaseMine commentary.

L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India — verified

Decided 18 March 1997 (AIR 1997 SC 1125 / (1997) 3 SCC 261) by a seven-judge bench. Confirmed: overruled Sampath Kumar; held judicial review under Articles 32/226/227 is part of the basic structure; tribunals under Articles 323A/323B supplement, but cannot substitute for, High Courts' power of judicial review. Source: Indian Kanoon, full judgment; Dhyeya Law case analysis.

Rojer Mathew v. South Indian Bank Ltd. — verified

Decided 13 November 2019 by a Constitution Bench. Confirmed: struck down the Tribunal, Appellate Tribunal and other Authorities (Qualifications, Experience and other Conditions of Service of Members) Rules, 2017 in their entirety for failing to protect judicial independence; also referred the broader question of using the Money Bill route (via the Finance Act, 2017) to amend tribunal structures to a larger bench. Source: Supreme Court Observer; SpicyIP.

Tribunal Reforms Act, 2026 / National Tribunals Commission — verified

Confirmed via multiple current-affairs sources: the Act establishes the NTC as a central body for appointments, administration, performance oversight, and service conditions across 16 tribunals; chaired by a former Supreme Court judge or former High Court Chief Justice, with two judicial and two technical members; appointments require CJI consultation; standardized 5-year tenure or age 70 (chairperson)/67 (members), whichever is earlier; the Act repeals the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021. Source: ClearIAS; Supreme Court Observer; PRS Legislative Research.
