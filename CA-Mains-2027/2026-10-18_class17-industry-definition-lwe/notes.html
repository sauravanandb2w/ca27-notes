Part A — Definition of "industry" (labour jurisprudence)

1. Context & the triple test

0:00Planned structure: introduction, concepts, legal framework, evolution in four phases, significance, challenges, way forward.

Why it matters: if a workplace is an "industry", its workers get legal protection — e.g., against sudden layoff and the right to strike.
IDA 1947 definition: very broad — any business, trade, undertaking, manufacture or employment, professional employment, handicraft.
Triple test (Bangalore Water Supply & Sewerage Board v. A. Rajappa, 1978, seven judges, Krishna Iyer J.): a workplace is an industry if it involves
  1. Systematic activity — organised, regular work (not a one-off or a home activity);
  2. Cooperation between employer and employees (teamwork);
  3. Production/supply of goods or services to satisfy human wants.
Profit is irrelevant — so charities, hospitals and educational institutions can fall within the test.

Labour is on the Concurrent List, so state and central laws differ — one reason for codification.

2. Why labour law exists — asymmetry and constitutional safeguards

  • Core asymmetry: individual workers have less bargaining power than capital. Ambedkar argued state intervention is needed to prevent the "dictatorship of the private employer".
  • Art. 23 prohibits forced labour. In People's Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India (Asiad Workers case, 1982), the Court held that paying less than the minimum wage amounts to forced labour.
  • Directive Principles: Art. 42 (just and humane conditions of work), Art. 43 (living wage), Art. 43A (workers' participation in management).

3. Economic efficiency vs. social protection

Economic efficiencySocial protection
Flexible labour laws, easy hiring and firing; less compliance and inspection; investment attraction. Rigid laws were called "regulatory cholesterol" in an earlier Economic Survey; they can keep firms small and informal.Statutory safeguards offset the bargaining imbalance; without them, market forces exploit labour. Over-deregulation risks exploitation.

Policy dilemma: over-regulation harms growth; under-regulation harms workers. The balance depends on context.

4. Legal framework: IDA 1947 → Industrial Relations Code 2020

  • Industrial Disputes Act 1947 (repealed; the Code took effect 21 Nov 2025): broad definition; the triple test was judicially read in.
  • Industrial Relations Code 2020, s.2(p): "industry" means any systematic activity carried on by cooperation between an employer and workers for the production, supply or distribution of goods or services to satisfy human wants. It incorporates the triple test in statutory text.
  • Exclusions in the Code: sovereign functions of government, charitable/philanthropic activity, domestic service, and home-made goods not produced as a systematic activity.
  • Related codes: Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code; Social Security Code; Wages Code. Factories Act and Contract Labour Act apply separately.

5. Phase-wise evolution & the 2026 verdict

Phase I — statutory and judicial ambiguity (post-1947): vague definition; conflicting High Court rulings; hospitals, universities and NGOs unsure of their status.
Phase II — worker-welfare era (1970s–): Bangalore Water Supply (1978) — the triple test; wide coverage; positive for dignity and worker protection. Negatives: charities and public services pulled in; hospitals and education could face strikes; criticised as judicial legislation (separation-of-powers concern).
Phase III — liberalisation (1991–): employers sought operational flexibility. State of UP v. Jai Bir Singh (2005) questioned the 1978 test as worker-oriented but was referred to a larger bench; the question stayed open for two decades.
Phase IV — the 2026 nine-judge verdict (20 Aug 2026): the Court retained the triple test for pending disputes under the repealed IDA (judicial stability), while future disputes are to be decided on the text and context of the IR Code 2020. The lecture describes this as a "dual-track" transition.
Verdict discrepancy: sources disagree on the vote (one report says 5:4 refinement; another reports a 4:4 split). Check the judgment text before quoting the bench's numbers. The Court also refrained from examining the IR Code's own definition.

6. Significance, challenges, way forward

Significance

  • Judicial stability for pending cases.
  • Interpretative independence: future courts read the Code, not the judge-made test.
  • Greater clarity: explicit exclusions for charities, sovereign functions and domestic service.

Challenges

  1. Interpretive divergence: the Phase I problem can return as High Courts read the new text differently.
  2. The "modern worker" boundary: IT and services employees designated "managers" lose protection even when doing worker-type work. Gig workers are "partners" and fall outside protection.
  3. Erosion of safety nets: explicit exclusions remove protection from NGO and hospital workers; the gig economy is a case in point (the government is moving to social security for platform workers).
  4. Litigation burden: workers must first prove the workplace is an industry, then prove they are workers — a multi-tier, slow process.

Way forward

  • Harmonised state rules (labour is a Concurrent subject).
  • Legislative clarity on who is a "worker".
  • A universal social-protection floor across industry, gig, NGO and public-service work.
  • Speedy dispute resolution to replace multi-tier litigation.
  • Align legal clarity with the Directive Principles for growth with labour dignity.

Part B — Left-wing extremism: post-2026 status

8. Concepts — LWE, Naxalism, Maoism; protracted people's war

  • Left-wing extremism (LWE): an umbrella term for armed groups with radical Marxist-Leninist or Maoist ideologies seeking to overthrow the state through armed struggle.
  • Naxalism: a form of LWE inspired by Maoism, named after Naxalbari (West Bengal), where an armed peasant uprising took place in 1967.
  • Marxism (general theory of class struggle); Leninism (a vanguard party, urban and factory-based, Russia); Maoism (rural peasant uprising against landlords, China). The Indian movement is primarily Maoist in character, with Marxist roots.
  • Protracted people's war (Mao): three strategic phases — (1) strategic defence ("hide and grow", building rural base areas); (2) strategic stalemate (guerrilla warfare, asymmetric); (3) strategic offence (attack, capture of urban centres). The lecture stresses that a movement can regress between phases.
Red Corridor: a contiguous forest and tribal belt where Maoists held territorial dominance and set up Janatana Sarkar (parallel governance).

9. Frameworks — greed vs. grievance; strong societies, weak states; conflict transformation

Greed vs. grievance

  • Grievance starts a movement: land alienation, displacement, denial of tribal rights, administrative neglect.
  • Greed sustains it: extortion, protection money, mining levies, control of resource-rich areas.

Strong societies, weak states (Joel Migdal)

  • In such areas, local strongmen (here, Maoist structures) provided livelihood and security, so people obeyed them; the state could not penetrate → governance vacuum → development deficit and alienation.
  • Related idea (Gunnar Myrdal): South Asian soft states fail to implement their own policies.

Conflict transformation (John Paul Lederach, Building Peace)

  • Peacebuilding framework: reconciliation; pyramid structure (top leaders, middle-level actors, grassroots — the middle is the crucial connector); process (may take generations); resources (schools, health, infrastructure); coordination.
  • Lasting change across four dimensions: personal, relational (bridging victim–cadre distrust), structural (changing unfair rules), cultural.

Data

Peak (2009–10): about 20 states and 223 districts (per former DGP Prakash Singh).
Early 2026: about 7–11 districts affected (sources differ; Singh's remarks in class).
Since 2014: over 12,000 km of roads and over 1,800 bank branches added (per Vikram Singh, citing the government).

11. Post-2026 status, emerging challenges & state responses

Status

  • Declaration: the Centre declared India free from armed Naxalism in March 2026; the PM repeated it in his 15 Aug 2026 speech.
  • Geographic shrinkage of the Red Corridor; operational decimation of cadres and leadership; forward operating bases; elite forces (CoBRA, jungle-warfare units) and better connectivity made the transition possible.
  • Shift from security dominance to governance credibility: civilian administration, hospitals, schools, local elections, development outreach (e.g., Chhattisgarh's Niyad Nellanar scheme), FRA and PESA implementation, Gram Sabha strengthening, and reverse-vasectomy care for former cadres (to allow family life and reintegration).

Emerging challenges

  1. Legal: surrendered cadres face cases, including murder charges; investigative lapses linked to alleged extrajudicial killings.
  2. Residual risk: remaining leaders and cadres could regroup into strategic defence again.
  3. Mutation: "overground workers" in urban areas, or links to transnational organised crime (drugs, arms).
  4. Development-induced displacement: private extraction in mineral-rich areas could re-ignite grievance.
  5. Social integration: surrendered cadres lack education and skills — need vocational training, adult education and livelihoods.

State responses

  • National Policy and Action Plan (2015), a four-pronged strategy: security; development; rights and entitlements; perception management. Operationalised as SAMADHAN.
  • Surrender and rehabilitation policy: financial incentives, housing, vocational training.
  • Clear, Hold, Build (CHB): clear the area of armed groups, hold it with fortified police stations, then build roads, schools and connectivity.
  • Digital connectivity through Digital Bharat Nidhi under the Telecommunications Act 2023 (levy on telecom firms to fund rural access).
  • Eklavya Model Residential Schools and special central assistance for LWE-affected districts.
  • Pandum cafés (Chhattisgarh): run by victims of Naxal violence and surrendered cadres, set up by the police and district administration — an example of social integration (Winning Hearts and Minds).
  • Planning Commission (Unnikrishnan Committee, 2008) had recommended land reforms and tribal rights; the lecture credits this as a root-cause diagnosis.

Comparison — Colombia's FARC

The FARC peace agreement converted an armed group into a political party with direct representation, ending the conflict through the democratic process. The lecture uses this to suggest direct political representation as one option for ex-insurgents in India, which is debatable.

Views

  • Prakash Singh (former DGP): the near-total decimation of Maoism is the biggest internal-security success after Punjab terrorism.
  • Vikram Singh (former DGP, paraphrased in class): the Red Corridor must become a development corridor.
