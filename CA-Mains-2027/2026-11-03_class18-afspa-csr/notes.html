Executive summary

  • Part A — AFSPA: an extraordinary law (1958) that lets the armed forces in "disturbed areas" use lethal force and arrest/search without warrant, and requires prior sanction for prosecution. The Centre extended it for six months from 1 Oct 2026 in parts of Manipur, Nagaland and Arunachal Pradesh. The lecture argues it secures only negative peace, and that it fuels alienation and fragile gains; the Supreme Court upheld it but laid down safeguards, and committees have urged repeal.
  • Part B — CSR: CSR sits at the intersection of market capital, corporate governance and state-led social policy. India's s.135 (Companies Act 2013) mandates 2% of average net profit for qualifying companies. Spending reached a record ~₹40,000+ crore in FY25, but it is concentrated (education ~34%, health ~21%, environment 7–9%), flows mostly to a few states, and raises debates on crowding out and "closed loop" foundations. The social stock exchange (SSE) is a proposed alternative.

Instructor's meta-advice: for "debate" questions, frame two debates (voluntary vs. mandatory; mandatory vs. SSE) and say which is more efficient. Use the data, and do not spend the answer on definitions alone. Revise concepts regularly — the lecturer warned that unrevised concepts (e.g., Agamben) are hard to recall in exam conditions.

Part A — AFSPA

1. Introduction & context

0:00Planned structure: introduction, concepts, key provisions, significance, concerns, judgments, committees, way forward.

  • Introduction: the Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act, 1958 grants special powers and legal protection to armed forces operating in areas notified as "disturbed". The lecturer's advice: for an introduction, state the option (what the law is) first, then the context.
  • Context: on 1 October 2026, the Centre extended AFSPA for six months in parts of Manipur, Nagaland and Arunachal Pradesh. Sources say the Gazette orders were published on 25 Sep 2026, effective 1 Oct (see verification).

2. Concepts

State of exception (Giorgio Agamben)

  • In response to a perceived emergency, the state suspends normal law ("the law suspends itself"). The measure is meant to be temporary, but it can become prolonged.
  • Those excluded from protection are homo sacer — no protection, and no one is punished for harming them. Their condition is bare life. Scholars apply this to AFSPA areas.

Negative vs. positive peace (Johan Galtung)

Negative peace = absence of violence. Extraordinary laws like AFSPA can produce it — but it falls apart once the law is lifted.
Positive peace = presence of justice, fairness, trusted institutions and resolved structural causes. Links to Lederach's conflict transformation (personal, relational, structural, cultural) from Class 17.

Security federalism

  • Police and public order are State subjects: local police are closer to people and accountable to elected governments.
  • Concentric circles of security (Supreme Court): the response should escalate with the threat. A law-and-order problem needs little action; an organised, inter-state insurgency needs central authority.
  • The tension: national-security priorities can produce a democratic deficit by affecting the right to life and personal liberty.

3. Key provisions

SectionWhat it does
s.3The Centre or the State may declare an area "disturbed" where there are severe security concerns.
s.4Operational powers for the armed forces: use lethal force (even causing death), arrest without warrant, enter and search without warrant.
s.5Arrested persons must be handed to the nearest police station "with the least possible delay" (no fixed 24 hours, unlike the ordinary CrPC/BNSS rule).
s.6Protection: no prosecution or suit against personnel without prior sanction of the Central Government.

Constitutional links

  • Entry 2A, Union List (inserted by the 42nd Amendment, 1976): the Union may deploy armed forces in aid of the state.
  • Art. 355: the Union's duty to protect states against internal disturbance.

4. Significance and concerns

Significance

  • Constitutional basis: Entry 2A and Art. 355 make it the Union's responsibility to act in internal disturbance.
  • Specialised, trained and unbiased force for severe security situations, which local police cannot handle.
  • Insulation from frivolous or politically motivated prosecutions through prior sanction.
  • Calibrated security instrument: it can be expanded or contracted as the situation changes (the Supreme Court has laid down guidelines for use).

Concerns

  1. Wide powers: lethal force and warrantless arrest. Human-rights groups call the law "draconian" and incompatible with modern human-rights standards.
  2. Immunity barrier (s.6): prior sanction is rarely granted, which limits victims' access to justice.
  3. Alienation: the combination of bare life and immunity breeds grievance. In a "strong societies, weak states" setting, people turn to alternative providers of security, including radical groups (see Class 17).
  4. Fragility of gains: the gains produced are negative peace. Manipur's 2023 violence — after AFSPA was revoked in the valley — is cited as an example of how quickly security can collapse.
  5. Ethnic security dilemma (Northeast): when the state cannot protect groups, each group arms itself to protect itself, and others respond in turn — an arms-race logic.
  6. Intergovernmental disagreement: either the Centre or the State can declare an area disturbed, so they can take conflicting positions (as in Manipur).

5. Judgments, committees, way forward

Judgments

  • Naga People's Movement for Human Rights v. Union of India (1997/1998): the Supreme Court upheld AFSPA's validity but laid down safeguards — the declaration must be reviewed (at least every six months), and the power must be used within limits. The lecture quotes the six-month review as one of the guidelines PT.
  • Extra-Judicial Execution Victim Families Association v. Union of India (2016): security personnel do not enjoy absolute immunity; there is "no licence to kill".

Committees

  • Justice Jeevan Reddy Committee (2005): recommended repealing AFSPA, calling it a "symbol of oppression". The Second ARC endorsed the recommendation.
  • Justice J.S. Verma Committee (2013): recommended removing offences against women from the immunity under s.6.

Way forward (lecture)

  1. Conditions, not the calendar: withdraw the law based on conditions (normalcy, stability, peace prospects), not six-month extensions.
  2. Build police capacity so the army is the last option, not the first.
  3. Mandatory periodic judicial review of disturbed-area notifications.
  4. Conflict transformation (personal, relational, structural, cultural) alongside security measures.

6. Roll-backs and reimposition in the Northeast

  • Meghalaya, Tripura: AFSPA was revoked after the security environment improved and civilian normalisation progressed (public trust and police capacity). The factors that decide revocation:
    1. Overall security environment (is there active conflict?);
    2. Civilian normalisation and public trust;
    3. Progress in peace negotiations (e.g., with Naga groups, revocation is not possible until an accord is reached);
    4. Trans-boundary and cross-border insurgent corridors, which need central coordination (e.g., Arunachal).
  • Nagaland: remains under AFSPA in many districts because the peace settlement is incomplete.
  • Manipur: reimposition is linked to ethnic conflict and the failure to make peace progress.
  • Arunachal Pradesh: extended in Tirap, Changlang and Longding (and parts of Namsai) — the reason given is trans-boundary security risk.
For map questions, draw Assam first and then mark Arunachal, Nagaland, Manipur, Mizoram, Tripura and Meghalaya around it.

Part B — CSR & development financing

8. Introduction, context and definition

44:00Planned structure: introduction, context, terms and concepts, constitutional and legal framework (s.135, Companies Act 2013), data, evolution in phases, significance, challenges, Supreme Court views, case studies, way forward. The lecturer says a question from this area is likely in the next exam.

  • Introduction: CSR and development financing sits at the intersection of market capital (money comes from business), corporate governance (how it is managed — mandated by law, handled by corporate committees) and state-led social policy (the government defines permitted areas).
  • Context: CSR spending hit a record ~₹40,794 crore in FY 2024–25 (up ~17%), from roughly 30,000 companies to ~72,000 projects (sources differ — see verification).
  • Context — social stock exchange: SEBI allowed companies to invest up to 10% of CSR funds in SSE zero-coupon zero-principal bonds issued by non-profits.

Definition

CSR is the social activity undertaken by companies to bring about a positive change in the society and environment in which they operate. It has two dimensions: how a company earns (ethically and responsibly) and how it spends part of its profit (giving back).

9. Thinkers: Friedman, Freeman, Carroll, Elkington, Gandhi; California consensus

ThinkerIdea
Milton Friedman"The business of business is business." The only social responsibility of business is to increase profits for shareholders, within the rules of the game.
R. Edward FreemanStakeholder theory: firms must serve all stakeholders — anyone affected by the business, including society and the environment, not only shareholders. (Do not confuse Friedman and Freeman.)
Archie CarrollCSR pyramid — four layers, not a hierarchy: economic (be profitable; the foundation), legal (obey the law), ethical (do what is just and fair; expected by society), philanthropic (give back; desired by society).
John ElkingtonTriple bottom line — the three Ps: profit (economic), people (social), planet (environmental). Success is measured on all three.
GandhiTrusteeship — wealth is held in trust for society.

California consensus

  • An emerging, technocratic philanthropy model led by tech billionaires and new-age donors (the lecture's example: Silicon Valley).
  • Traditional model: patient, long-term commitment to institution-building (schools, hospitals, trusts; India's trusteeship tradition).
  • California consensus: rapid, technology-driven change; scalability; a fixation on measurable numbers. Concern: this can distract from things that are hard to measure but socially valuable.

10. Constitutional and legal framework

Constitution

  • Art. 38: the state secures a social order for the promotion of the welfare of the people.
  • Art. 39(b): ownership and control of material resources distributed to serve the common good.
  • Art. 39(c): prevent the concentration of wealth.

Legal framework — s.135, Companies Act 2013

ElementProvision (as presented)
ApplicabilityCompanies with net worth ≥ ₹500 crore, or turnover ≥ ₹1,000 crore, or net profit ≥ ₹5 crore PT.
ObligationSpend 2% of the average net profit of the preceding three financial years on CSR.
GovernanceBoard-constituted CSR Committee to implement the policy.
Permitted activitiesSchedule VII of the Companies Act — poverty eradication, education, health, environmental sustainability, rural development, etc. PT.
PenaltiesFines for unspent CSR money; the earlier jail term was decriminalised.

Previously CSR was voluntary; s.135 made it mandatory for qualifying companies.

11. Data, evolution in phases, significance

Data (FY 2024–25, reported)

Total CSR spend: ~₹40,794 crore (record; ~17% year on year).
Education: ~34% (about ₹13,877 crore). Health: ~21% (₹8,531 crore; lecture says 22%). Environment and sustainability: only ~7–9%. Rural development: ~10%.
Geography: six or so industrialised states get ~60% of spending; Maharashtra is the largest recipient (₹8,631 crore). All eight north-eastern states combined received ~₹855 crore.
Aspirational districts: a small share (the lecture says ~2.15% since 2014; CRISIL's data for listed companies gives 12% of CSR spend in a different base year — verify before citing).
Per-capita: ~₹19 in aspirational districts vs. ~₹375 in Pune (lecture, from a study).

Evolution in phases

  1. Phase I — voluntary philanthropy (pre-1947 to the 1950s): business families set up charitable trusts (e.g., Tata, Birla trusts).
  2. Phase II — state-led model (1960s–2000s): the state led welfare; grants to NGOs and foreign aid for social infrastructure.
  3. Phase III — mandatory regime (post-2013): s.135, a 2% profit-sharing mandate.
  4. Phase IV — new models (present): blended finance, social stock exchange, social impact bonds; "constitutionalisation" of CSR through judicial pronouncements.

Significance

  • Dependable, non-tax development finance that grows with profits.
  • Social infrastructure: education (including remote areas), health clinics (important during COVID), environmental measures.
  • Last-mile delivery through NGOs at the grassroots, supporting inclusive growth and positive peace (see Part A).
  • De-risking investment: e.g., mangrove plantations protect coastal investments.
  • Constitutional and judicial links: in M.K. Ranjitsinh v. Union of India (2021), the Supreme Court held CSR cannot be divorced from corporate environmental responsibility.

12. Challenges, social stock exchange, way forward

Challenges

  1. From responsibility to tax: a mandatory 2% contribution acts like a tax, but spending is less transparent and less efficient than a government tax-and-spend.
  2. Tax avoidance: companies have an incentive to show lower profits to reduce CSR liability.
  3. Crowding out (Shivam Rajapal's study, as cited in the lecture): mandates push out voluntary good behaviour — companies treat CSR as a statutory task, not a commitment.
  4. Closed-loop model: companies create foundations that fund projects benefiting the company itself.
  5. Humanistic bias: spending concentrates on health and education; environment and future-generation needs are underfunded.
  6. Regional disparity: industrialised states get most funds; aspirational districts get little.
  7. Activity-based compliance and greenwashing: "tick-the-box" tree planting with no verification.
  8. Political interference: pressure to fund favoured projects, creating patron–client relations between business and politics.

Social stock exchange (SSE) as a model

  • Companies invest in pre-verified projects listed on the exchange through zero-coupon, zero-principal bonds — no need for a company-owned foundation (prevents closed loops).
  • Independent verification by the SSE and the regulator.
  • Ready projects absorb CSR money, reducing idle or unspent CSR.
  • Broad-based participation: any donor, not only profitable large companies, can contribute.
  • Non-profits in neglected areas can receive funds, which narrows the regional gap.

Way forward (lecture)

  • Outcome-based, not activity-based, spending. Move from fragmented projects to collective intervention with other companies.
  • Expertise, not just money: align CSR with each company's core competence.
  • Government matching contributions to raise spending in low-CSR (last-mile) areas.
  • National social return-on-investment (SROI) framework to measure the value per rupee.
  • Mandatory diversity components (e.g., a share for gender equality or environment).
  • Regional equalisation fund pooling CSR money for neglected states.
  • Geo-tagged national database of CSR projects, with sector, amount, implementer and outcome.

13. Live answer-writing — efficiency of mandated CSR

Q. "Mandating CSR transforms a voluntary activity into a tax." In light of this statement, debate the efficiency of mandated CSR. — set in class
  1. Intro: data (FY25 record; ~30,000 companies; ~72,000 projects) and a one-line definition.
  2. Background: s.135 (2% of average net profit); Schedule VII.
  3. Debate 1 — voluntary vs. mandatory: voluntary model (long-term, trusteeship) vs. mandatory (dependable finance, education ~34%, health ~22%, Harali and palliative-care examples). Mandatory model is efficient in volume and reach.
  4. Debate 2 — mandatory vs. SSE: mandatory suffers from closed loops, idle money and regional skew; SSE offers verification and broad participation. SSE is more efficient but has challenges (new market, verification capacity).

  6. Conclusion: triple bottom line, stakeholder theory, or Carroll's pyramid.
