1. Right to Protest

  • Derived, composite right — not explicit; built from Art. 19(1)(a) speech, 19(1)(b) assembly, 19(1)(c) association. Tension with 19(1)(d) movement.
  • Formula: maximum rights + minimum restrictions; state action must pass proportionality (legitimate aim → rational nexus → balancing).
  • Lohia (1966) concentric circles: security of state > public order > law and order > public inconvenience — only top two justify Sec 163 BNSS action.
  • Himat Lal Shah (1972): assembly is essential to democracy; streets are natural venues for expression, but reasonable regulation allowed.
  • Anita Thakur (2016): force must be minimum, reasonable, proportionate; none warranted if assembly peaceful.
  • Ramlila Maidan (2012): Sec 144/163 needs a real & imminent threat, not mere likelihood; negligence apportioned 3:1 state:organizer.
  • Amit Sahni/Shaheen Bagh (2020): protesters cannot occupy public space indefinitely — criticized as extra-constitutional.
  • Significance: popular sovereignty, continuous accountability (vs. 5-yr elections), drove RTI & anti-paper-leak law, protects marginalized voice (Mahad Satyagraha).
  • Key debates: spatial limits vs. visibility of dissent; state deterrence (FRT/drones → chilling effect) vs. proportionate response; democracy vs. mobocracy; rule of law vs. rule by law.
  • Reforms: mediation cells, civic assembly parks, surveillance data-purging SOPs, visible/uniformed policing + body cams, internet-shutdown safeguards, independent monitors.

2. Section 17A, PCA 1988

  • PCA pillars: undue advantage (any gratification, not just money), public duty, public servant (incl. judges, VCs).
  • Sec 17A (2018): prior sanction to investigate a public servant. Sec 19: prior sanction to prosecute.
  • Evolution: Single Directive (pre-1997) → struck down in Vineet Narain (1997) → Sec 6A DSPE Act (JS-and-above only) → struck down in Swamy (2014, Art.14) → Sec 17A (2018) extended to all servants → challenged in CPIL (2026).
  • CPIL v. UOI (13 Jan 2026) split verdict: Nagarathna J. — strike down entirely (conflict of interest, "old wine new bottle"). Viswanathan J. — uphold if read down via independent Lokpal screening. Referred to CJI for larger bench.
  • For 17A: prevents "play-safe syndrome"/policy paralysis, shields honest officers. Against: Art.14 issue, shields the corrupt too, kills the surprise element (evidence destruction risk).
  • Way forward: Law Commission 254th Report & Justice Viswanathan both favor independent (Lokpal) screening over government-filtered approval.

3. Judicial Appointments — Collegium

  • Art. 124: SC structure/strength; original text — President appoints "in consultation with" CJI. Three Art. 124(3) routes: bench (sitting HC judge), bar (10-yr advocate), jurist (never used). Art. 217 = HC analogue.
  • Evolution (4 phases): 1950s-80s executive primacy → 1st Judges Case (S.P. Gupta, 1981): consultation ≠ concurrence → 2nd Judges Case (1993): consultation = concurrence, birth of collegium → 3rd Judges Case (1998, presidential ref.): institutionalized CJI + 4 senior judges → NJAC/4th Judges Case (2015): 99th Amdt. struck 4:1, basic structure/judicial independence.
  • 2026 context: SC strength raised 34→38 (Judges Amendment Act 2026); Justice Ujjal Bhuyan urged using the unused "jurist" route; Rajasthan HC roster-abuse row; Justice Yashwant Varma cash case — resigned Apr 2026 before impeachment (no judge ever removed via completed impeachment).
  • Debates (continues next class): transparency vs. confidentiality (Ruma Pal: collegium is "best-kept secret"); judicial independence vs. accountability.
