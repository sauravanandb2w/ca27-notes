1. Topic 1: Right to protest — constitutional basis & core debates

0:00Opening context cited in class:

  • Recent student protests
  • Farmer protests
  • Ethnic-group blockades
  • A plea for a comprehensive national policy/guidelines on crowd control and public assembly

Constitutional basis

  • Right to peaceful protest is not an explicit fundamental right.
    • It is a derived, composite right
    • Built from Article 19(1)(a): freedom of speech and expression
    • Built from Article 19(1)(b): right to assemble peaceably without arms
    • Built from Article 19(1)(c): right to form associations, unions, cooperatives
  • Freedom of speech has both instrumental value and intrinsic value.
    • Instrumental value: a means to hold government accountable, to inform public debate
    • Intrinsic value: self-expression is valuable in itself, independent of its effects
  • The "golden triangle" of the Constitution: Articles 14, 19, and 21 read together.
  • Article 19(1)(d) — freedom of movement — creates direct tension with the right to protest.
    • Protesters' assembly rights can obstruct other citizens' movement
    • This tension recurs across nearly every debate in this topic
  • Restrictions permitted under Article 19(2) and 19(3): sovereignty and integrity of India, security of the state, public order, decency, morality.
    • Restrictions must be minimum
    • Restrictions must be proportional

Formula to remember

Maximum rights plus minimum restrictions. State action/police action must be minimum and must pass the test of proportionality.

Test of proportionality — three prongs

  • Legitimate state interest: e.g. public order, security of the state.
  • Rational nexus: a real link must exist between the measure adopted (e.g. internet shutdown, pellet guns) and the objective to be achieved (e.g. tranquility, public order).
    • A complete nationwide internet shutdown to manage a local protest would be disproportionate
  • Balancing: the rights of protesters must be balanced against the rights of commuters/other citizens — the "harmonious balancing of constitutional rights."

Core debate 1: spatial limitation vs. visibility of dissent

  • Government proposals sometimes suggest relocating protest sites (e.g. away from Jantar Mantar to outskirts).
    • Advantage claimed: prevents public-order issues and public inconvenience
  • Protesters' view: dissent needs visibility.
    • A protest far from the seat of power may fail to achieve its objective
    • Example cited: the farmers' protest at border areas had lower visibility than the recent, highly visible student protests near the seat of power
    • The three farm laws were eventually withdrawn, but the lecturer notes this was not solely due to the protest's visibility/pressure
  • Commuters' view: a centrally located protest site (e.g. Jantar Mantar) penalizes innocent citizens and creates public inconvenience.
  • Tension: proximity to the seat of power is important for democratic effectiveness, but it also maximizes disruption to daily life.

Core debate 2: state deterrence vs. proportionate response

  • State's justification for strict measures: preventing public disorder, preventing infiltration by criminal elements into demonstrations.
  • This can lead to aggressive executive crackdowns, considered disproportionate.
    • Example cited: use of pellet guns
  • Aggressive surveillance technologies used by police:
    • Facial Recognition Technology (FRT)
    • Drone camera filming
  • Consequence: a chilling effect.
    • People become hesitant to join demonstrations
    • This defeats the very purpose of the right to protest

Core debate 3: public order vs. public convenience

  • Article 19(2) permits restrictions for public order — this is constitutional.
  • Restricting protest merely for public inconvenience (e.g. a traffic jam) is extra-constitutional — not sanctioned by the Constitution.
  • Public order = affects the community/society at large (e.g. a protest turning violent, leading to rioting).
  • Public inconvenience = a lesser, localized effect (e.g. a traffic diversion) — this should not by itself justify aggressive state action.
Ram Manohar Lohia v. State of Bihar (1965/66 AIR 740): Supreme Court articulated a "concentric circles" model of state interest.
  • Innermost circle: security of the state
  • Next circle: public order (affects peace/tranquility of the community at large)
  • Next circle: law and order (a narrower, more local problem — not a valid basis for invoking Section 163 BNSS/old Section 144 CrPC)
  • Outermost circle: public inconvenience (the lowest-level concern — action based purely on this is disproportionate)
Cite the Lohia case's concentric-circles framework whenever a question asks about the distinction between public order, law and order, and public inconvenience — it directly substantiates why state action must be graded to the actual level of threat.

Core debate 4: democracy vs. mobocracy

  • Ideal democratic model: grievances are addressed through institutional/constitutional routes.
    • Peaceful protest is itself one such constitutional mechanism
    • Courts and elections are other such routes
  • Risk: protest can turn into "mobocracy" through sheer number of people.
    • Crowds may attempt to negotiate/bargain through intimidation rather than democratic process
    • Can escalate into mob lynching — citizens taking law into their own hands
  • Ambedkar's view (cited in this context): before independence, no institutional avenues existed, so civil disobedience was necessary.
    • After independence, constitutional methods are available and should be preferred
  • Rule of law vs. rule by law:
    • The British used law as a tool to enforce colonial control — that is rule by law
    • Democracy requires rule of law — no one, including government, is above the law
    • State risk: using laws like the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act to treat protest actions (e.g. a road/economic blockade) as terrorist activity — this itself becomes "rule by law"
  • Popular sovereignty (people's collective will expressed democratically) is distinct from mere crowd sentiment (which can lead to violence/anarchy without following democratic routes).

Legal framework — laws that can restrict protest

  • Section 163, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS) — replaces the old Section 144 CrPC.
    • Executive magistrate may impose prohibitory orders
    • Can prevent assembly of five or more persons
  • Prevention of Damage to Public Property Act — can be invoked against protesters who destroy public/state assets.

2. Topic 1: Significance, case law, reforms & live answer-writing

Significance of the right to protest

  • Upholds popular sovereignty: people are the source of power; citizens do not surrender their voice after elections.
  • Continuous citizen oversight: elections offer feedback only once every five years; protest is a continuous, active feedback mechanism.
  • Democratic accountability: keeps the executive/government responsive between elections.
  • Historical continuity in India: independence itself was won through civil disobedience and mass demonstrations, not a post-independence invention.
  • Drives legislative reform: examples cited — the Right to Information Act (RTI), and amendments to the Public Examinations (Prevention of Unfair Means) Act following student protests over exam-paper leaks.
  • Protects marginalized-section interests: majoritarian democracy can otherwise neglect minority/marginalized concerns.
    • Example cited: Dr. Ambedkar's Mahad Satyagraha
    • Other examples: Forest Rights Act-related tribal-rights protests
  • Fosters social fraternity: gives voice to dissenters, prevents alienation from the state, builds constitutional faith.

Arguments against / concerns

  • Infringes on other citizens' right to life/movement (mobility), causes disturbance to nearby residential communities.
  • Disrupts essential/public utility services, including medical services and sanitation — a compromise on public health.
  • Economic and industrial dislocation from disrupted transport/logistics — affects movement of people and goods.
  • Overall threats to public security: large demonstrations can turn violent, risk of infiltration by criminal elements or communal polarization.

Constraints and challenges

  • Absence of a comprehensive national crowd-control policy: no graded-response guidelines exist.
    • Leads to arbitrary executive action
  • The "mighty state" problem (a concern the Supreme Court itself has flagged): an overwhelmingly powerful state risks becoming "rule by law."
    • Linked to misuse of laws like preventive detention
  • Mass surveillance and profiling via technologies like FRT.
    • Violates right to privacy
    • Disproportionate, produces a chilling effect
  • Digital censorship: internet shutdown orders, site blocking.
  • Disinformation and fake news during protests.
    • Misinformation: not intentional (e.g. forwarding an unverified WhatsApp message)
    • Disinformation: a deliberate act, sometimes intended to create internal security challenges
  • Rise of digital vigilantism / "cancel culture": online mob action (hate speech, doxxing) replacing the democratic route with a digital one.
  • Destruction of public property during protests.

Key Supreme Court judgments

Himat Lal K. Shah v. Commissioner of Police, Ahmedabad (1972 INSC 220 / AIR 1973 SC 87):
  • Held: freedom of assembly is an essential element of a democratic system
  • Held: public streets are natural places for expression of opinion
  • Government cannot abridge the right of assembly by prohibiting it on every public street, but a citizen cannot protest "wherever they please" either — reasonable regulation (e.g. traffic management) is permitted
Anita Thakur & Ors. v. State of Jammu & Kashmir (2016):
  • Held: police use of force must be the minimum necessary
  • Held: force must satisfy the principles of reasonableness and proportionality
  • Where an assembly is peaceful, use of police force is not warranted at all
Amit Sahni v. Commissioner of Police (2020) 10 SCC 573 — the Shaheen Bagh case:
  • Arose from over 100 days of anti-CAA protest blocking the Okhla underpass (Dec 2019 – Mar 2020)
  • Held: protesters cannot occupy public spaces indefinitely
  • Criticism noted in class: this arguably creates an extra-constitutional restriction not directly grounded in Article 19(2)/(3)
In Re: Ramlila Maidan Incident (2012) 5 SCC 1:
  • Arose from a 2011 midnight crackdown on a Baba Ramdev-led anti-corruption gathering
  • Held: Section 144 CrPC (now Section 163 BNSS) orders can be justified only when the threat to peace is real and imminent, not a mere likely possibility
  • Court apportioned negligence 3:1 between the state and the organizing foundation for compensation purposes
Maniyar Iliyaz v. P. Ayyappan (2026):
  • Supreme Court held the right to walk on safe, demarcated footpaths is a fundamental right
  • Grounded in Article 19(1)(d) (movement), read with 19(1)(a)/(b)/(c) and Article 21
  • Arose from a road-accident compensation case, not directly a protest case, but cited in class as a fresh 2026 illustration of Article 21's expanding scope

Quotes to cite

Dissent is the safety valve of democracy — if dissent is not allowed, the pressure cooker may burst. A free society must make space for it.
— Justice D.Y. Chandrachud, then a sitting Supreme Court judge (later CJI, 2022–2024); first made this remark in February 2020 amid the anti-CAA protests (see Supplementary research)
There may be times when we are powerless to prevent injustice, but there must never be a time when we fail to protest.
— Elie Wiesel, Holocaust survivor and 1986 Nobel Peace Prize laureate

Scholarly perspectives on protest and democracy

  • Ambedkar's "Grammar of Anarchy" — three warnings (speech to the Constituent Assembly, 25 November 1949):
    • First warning: hold fast to constitutional methods; abandon "grammar of anarchy" methods like civil disobedience and non-cooperation, since constitutional avenues now exist
    • Second warning: do not lay liberties at the feet of a great man, or trust him with power that could subvert institutions — a caution against hero-worship (bhakti in politics is "a sure road to degradation")
    • Third warning: political democracy cannot survive without social democracy — formal one-person-one-vote equality is not enough while the underlying society remains unequal
  • Charles Tilly (sociologist): popular movements are not symptoms of democratic failure, but products of democracy's evolution — they drive institutional opening-up, which in turn mobilizes further reform.
  • Dipesh Chakrabarty (the auto-caption's garbled "deep chakraati" — resolved via Supplementary research; not Partha Chatterjee).
    • Argues in "In the Name of Politics: Sovereignty, Democracy, and the Multitude in India" (EPW 2005; Public Culture 2007) that large gatherings of ordinary people are not an emergency response to institutional failure
    • They are integral to the logic of Indian democracy
    • They are the principal avenue for subaltern sections to make claims on the state
  • Patrick Heller (sociologist, comparative work on India/Brazil/South Africa): crowds and mass mobilization are not pathologies of democracy, but one of its principal mechanisms in unequal societies, where weak bargaining power leaves subaltern groups no option but collective mobilization.

Case study: Delhi Police Standing Order 10

  • A graded-response system for crowd control.
  • Introduced to replace arbitrary/discretionary permit decisions.
  • Follows a tiered demarcation of venue.
    • Example cited: below ~1,000 participants may be allocated Jantar Mantar; above that, protesters may be directed to Ramlila Maidan

Suggested reforms

  • Institutionalized preemptive mediation/grievance-redressal cells at the lowest/local level, to engage early and let people vent grievances.
  • Designated civic assembly parks at the state level, equipped with basic civic amenities (e.g. toilets) — reduces public disorder from ad hoc protest sites.
  • Strict SOPs for biometric/digital surveillance technology: mandatory data purging after the event/investigation concludes.
  • Uniform and visible policing: officers should not operate in plain clothes; body-worn cameras recommended — ensures executive accountability.
  • Safeguards on internet shutdowns/digital blocking: to prevent disproportionate action while still curbing disinformation risk.
  • Independent assembly monitors: neutral observers such as human-rights or legal-aid groups to document police-protester interactions.
  • Above all: sustained institutional dialogue between state and protesters.

3. Topic 2: Section 17A of the Prevention of Corruption Act

1:11:48Suggested introduction framing: "The Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 serves as the primary statutory shield safeguarding India's democratic and administrative framework from the corrosive effects of corruption."

1:13:08Context: a split verdict by the Supreme Court in Centre for Public Interest Litigation v. Union of India (2026), on the constitutional validity of Section 17A of the PCA, revived a long-running debate on balancing administrative protection with accountability.

PCA 1988 — objective and three foundational pillars

  • Objective: ensure probity in public life.
  • Enacted to consolidate the law relating to prevention of corruption and to provide punishment for offences committed by public servants while performing public duties.
  • Three pillars define a corrupt transaction:
    • Undue advantage: any gratification other than legal remuneration — covers non-monetary satisfaction/favor, not just money.
    • Public duty: a duty in the discharge of which the state, the public, or the community at large has an interest — not limited narrowly to one's job description.
    • Public servant: includes any government or local-authority official, a judge, or any person holding public office who is required to perform a public duty — can include a vice-chancellor or other office-bearers, not only classic government employees.

Section 17A and Section 19 — what they require

Section 17A (introduced by a 2018 amendment): investigating officers/agencies must seek prior approval from the appropriate government before conducting any inquiry or investigation into a public servant — this is "permission to investigate."
Section 19: investigative agencies require prior permission from the appropriate government before formally prosecuting a public servant in a court of law — this is "permission to prosecute."

Evolution of the "prior sanction" debate

  • Till 1997: a single consolidated executive "Single Directive" required agencies like the CBI to get government approval before investigating senior officers.
  • Vineet Narain v. Union of India (1997) — the Jain Hawala case:
    • Supreme Court struck down the Single Directive
    • Held: no such executive directive can negate the rule of law
    • Associated with the principle that no one stands above the law, however high their office
  • Parliament's response: enacted Section 6A of the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, requiring CBI to get prior sanction before investigating officers of the rank of Joint Secretary or above.
  • Dr. Subramanian Swamy v. Director, CBI (2014):
    • Supreme Court struck down Section 6A
    • Held it discriminatory and violative of Article 14 — it treated senior officers differently from junior ones
    • A later 2023 Constitution Bench held this invalidity applied retrospectively, from the date Section 6A was inserted
  • 2018: Parliament enacted Section 17A of the PCA, again requiring prior sanction — but this time extended to all public servants, not just Joint Secretary-and-above, in an apparent attempt to avoid the Article 14 problem that sank Section 6A.
  • This is the provision challenged in the 2026 CPIL case.

Diagram: each redesign of the "prior sanction" shield gets struck down or challenged on the same underlying ground — protecting the corrupt along with the honest.

The full sequence — Single Directive (pre-1997) → Vineet Narain (1997) → Section 6A DSPE Act → Subramanian Swamy (2014) → Section 17A PCA (2018) → CPIL (2026) — is exactly the kind of "evolution of a provision" timeline UPSC likes to test. Memorize the sequence, not just the individual cases.

4. Topic 2: CPIL verdict, data & live answer-writing exercise

Centre for Public Interest Litigation v. Union of India (13 January 2026) — the split verdict

  • A two-judge bench delivered a split verdict on Section 17A's constitutional validity.
  • Both judges agreed there is some problem with Section 17A as it stands — the disagreement was about the remedy.
  • Justice B.V. Nagarathna: struck down Section 17A entirely.
    • Held it an impermissible fetter on the rule of law, discriminatory and violative of Article 14
    • Reasoning: the vice lies in the very requirement of prior sanction, not in who grants it
    • Identified a structural conflict of interest — the government both oversees the accused officials and decides whether to approve their investigation
    • Described it as protecting the corrupt — "old wine in a new bottle," no real change from Section 6A
  • Justice K.V. Viswanathan: upheld Section 17A, but only if "read down."
    • Reasoning: the provision prevents a "play-safe syndrome" among honest bureaucrats, avoiding policy paralysis
    • Suggested the approval process be filtered through an independent body — specifically the Lokpal — rather than the concerned government/ministry
    • This tracks the Law Commission's 254th Report recommendation of independent screening
  • Outcome: the conflicting opinions were referred to the Chief Justice of India for constitution of an appropriate larger bench to resolve the split.

Key data and framing

Corruption Perceptions Index (CPI) 2025 (Transparency International): India ranked 91st of 182 countries, score 39/100 — an improvement from 96th in 2024
Central Vigilance Commission (CVC) annual report figures cited in class: more than 7,000 corruption cases investigated by CBI remain pending, including 379 cases pending for over 20 years
  • "Sanskritization"/normalization of corruption (framing cited in class, attributed to Professor Kaushik Basu): society gradually accepts a certain baseline level of corruption rather than pursuing punishment.
  • Gary Becker's "economics of crime" framework: if the expected cost of corrupt behavior (probability of punishment × severity, discounted over a 20-30 year pendency) is low, the deterrent effect collapses.

Arguments supporting Section 17A

  • Prevents the "play-safe syndrome" — without protection, bureaucrats may avoid signing off on any file/decision, fearing future inquiry.
  • Protects honest officers, allowing them to function as the "steel frame" of administration.
  • Filters out frivolous/vexatious complaints against honest officers.
  • Supreme Court's own reasoning (cited in class): if honest public servants are not assured that their official decisions will not be subjected to frivolous complaints, "it is the nation that will suffer."

Arguments against Section 17A

  • Violates Article 14 by creating an unequal protection regime between decision-makers/higher officials and lower-grade employees/clerks.
  • Filtering the initial inquiry itself can protect the corrupt, not just the honest.
  • Inherent conflict of interest: the government is often the largest stakeholder in protecting its own department/ministry from scrutiny.
  • Operational impact: investigations need a surprise element.
    • Seeking prior sanction first gives the public servant advance notice
    • This creates an opportunity to destroy evidence/records before the investigation begins

Committees, global comparisons & way forward

  • Vohra Committee (1993) — the transcript's garbled "NNA committee," resolved via Supplementary research.
    • Chaired by then-Home Secretary N.N. Vohra, formed after the 1993 Bombay bomb blasts
    • Documented the criminal nexus among politicians, bureaucrats, police, and criminal syndicates
    • Its findings fed into the broader case for strengthening bribery/criminal-misconduct law, including provisions later reflected in the PCA framework
  • Law Commission's 254th Report: recommended prior sanction be routed through an independent body such as the Lokpal.
  • United Nations Convention Against Corruption (ratified by India): frames corruption as a human-rights issue.
  • In 2026, India hosted a meeting of the GLOBE Network (Global Operational Network of Anti-Corruption Law Enforcement Authorities) — signals India's commitment to cross-border anti-corruption enforcement.
  • Way forward suggested in class:
    • Independent screening model (e.g. via the Lokpal), addressing the conflict-of-interest problem while retaining some filter against frivolous complaints
    • Judicial reforms: fast-track courts, time-bound trials, to clear the large pending-case backlog
    • Systemic reforms: whistleblower protection, technology adoption (removing intermediaries/"middlemen") to reduce opportunities for corruption at the source

5. Topic 3: Judicial appointments — the collegium system

Note: this topic begins around 1:47:10 and the lecture recording ends at 2:06:17 with the instructor stating the remainder will continue in the next class. Notes below cover only what was actually taught in this session.

1:49:42Suggested introduction framing: "The appointment of the higher judiciary lies at the core of constitutional democracy, balancing judicial independence with democratic accountability."

Constitutional dimension

  • Article 124 establishes the Supreme Court of India, its structure, and judge strength (as prescribed by Parliament).
  • Original constitutional text: judges appointed by the President "in consultation with" the Chief Justice of India — a middle course, not full executive primacy nor full judicial primacy.
  • Through subsequent judicial interpretation, "consultation" was transformed into "concurrence" — giving rise to a self-appointing collegium system.
  • Article 124(3) provides three routes by which a person may become a Supreme Court judge:
    • Bench route: a sitting High Court judge elevated to the Supreme Court
    • Bar route: a practicing advocate with at least 10 years' experience
    • Jurist route: a "distinguished jurist" in the opinion of the President — this route has never actually been used in India's history; a similar route existed for High Courts but was removed by a later constitutional amendment
  • Article 217 provides an analogous framework for High Court judges.

Recent context making this topical

  • Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Act, 2026: raised the Supreme Court's sanctioned strength from 34 to 38, including the CJI (Cabinet approval 5 May 2026; the previous increase was 2019, from 31 to 34 including the CJI).

Evolution of the collegium system — four phases

  • Phase 1 (1950s–1980s): executive primacy.
    • "Consultation" with the CJI did not mean "concurrence" — the executive retained the final say
    • Consistent with a period of very strong, "towering leader"-led executives with large parliamentary majorities
  • Phase 2 — First Judges Case (S.P. Gupta v. Union of India, 1981):
    • Supreme Court held "consultation" does not mean "concurrence"
    • Continued executive primacy in appointments
  • Phase 3 — Second Judges Case (1993):
    • Coincides with a period of declining executive dominance and the rise of coalition politics (from the late 1980s)
    • Supreme Court reversed course: held "consultation" now means "concurrence"
    • Gave birth to the collegium system, shifting primacy to the judiciary
  • Phase 4 — Third Judges Case (1998, presidential reference):
    • President sought clarification on exactly who must be consulted under "concurrence"
    • Supreme Court institutionalized the collegium: CJI plus the four senior-most judges must be consulted
  • NJAC episode / Fourth Judges Case (2015):
    • Parliament had enacted the National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) — appointments via a body of three judicial members and three executive/other members
    • Supreme Court struck down the NJAC (99th Constitutional Amendment), by a 4:1 majority
    • Held it violated the basic structure doctrine, specifically judicial independence (already recognized as basic structure since the Second Judges Case)
    • Reinstated the collegium system's primacy
This is a classic "evolution" answer structure UPSC often asks for directly (not just "arguments for/against" but "how did we get here"). Memorize the four-phase sequence with its case names and years in order: 1980s executive primacy → 1981 First Judges Case → 1993 Second Judges Case → 1998 Third Judges Case → 2015 NJAC/Fourth Judges Case.

Two debates introduced (to be continued next class)

  • Transparency vs. confidentiality:
    • Pro-transparency view: citizens have a right to know who their judges are and why they were selected, and by what criteria
    • Former Justice Ruma Pal's characterization cited in class: the collegium system is "the best-kept secret in the country"
    • Pro-confidentiality view: secrecy shields candidates from political pressure and media/lobbying influence, and protects the reputation of bench-route candidates who are considered but not ultimately selected
  • Judicial independence vs. accountability:
    • Independence argument: the selection process must be insulated from political/executive interference
    • Accountability argument: a non-transparent, non-accountable appointment process itself undermines constitutional accountability and the rule of law

Articles 14, 19, and 21 read together as a mutually reinforcing set of fundamental rights.
Concentric circles of state interest
Ram Manohar Lohia framework: security of state (innermost) → public order → law and order → public inconvenience (outermost) — state action must be proportional to which circle is actually threatened.
Mighty state
Supreme Court's own framing for an overwhelmingly powerful state whose centralized power risks tipping into "rule by law" rather than "rule of law."

Ambedkar's phrase (1949) for methods like civil disobedience and non-cooperation, which he argued should be abandoned once constitutional methods become available.

Successor to Section 144 CrPC; lets an executive magistrate impose prohibitory orders preventing assembly of five or more persons, justified only where the threat to peace is real and imminent (Ramlila Maidan, 2012).

Revision kit (ARCS)

A — Active Recall: self-test before re-reading

1. Why is the right to protest called a "derived, composite right" rather than an explicit fundamental right?

There is no single article that directly names a "right to protest." It is built up from three separate Article 19(1) freedoms read together: 19(1)(a) freedom of speech and expression, 19(1)(b) right to assemble peaceably without arms, and 19(1)(c) right to form associations/unions. Courts have recognized peaceful assembly as fundamental (e.g. Himat Lal K. Shah, 1972) even though the Constitution never uses the word "protest."

2. What is the difference between "public order" and "public inconvenience," and why does it matter constitutionally?

Public order affects the community/society at large (e.g. a protest turning violent, risking rioting) and is a valid ground for restriction under Article 19(2)/(3). Public inconvenience (e.g. a traffic jam from a protest) is a much narrower, localized effect. Restricting protest based only on public inconvenience is extra-constitutional — not sanctioned by the Constitution — per the Ram Manohar Lohia "concentric circles" framework.

3. What did the Supreme Court hold in the 2026 CPIL v. Union of India split verdict on Section 17A of the PCA?

Justice B.V. Nagarathna struck down Section 17A entirely, holding it discriminatory, violative of Article 14, and structurally protective of corrupt officials due to a built-in government conflict of interest. Justice K.V. Viswanathan upheld it but only if "read down" so that the prior-sanction screening is done by an independent body like the Lokpal, rather than the government itself. The split was referred to the CJI for a larger bench to resolve.

4. Trace the four-phase evolution of the judicial-appointments collegium system.

Phase 1 (1950s-80s): executive primacy, "consultation" did not mean "concurrence." Phase 2 (First Judges Case, S.P. Gupta, 1981): Supreme Court confirmed consultation does not equal concurrence, continuing executive primacy. Phase 3 (Second Judges Case, 1993): Supreme Court reversed this, holding consultation now means concurrence — birth of the collegium. Phase 4 (Third Judges Case, 1998 presidential reference): institutionalized exactly who must be consulted (CJI plus four senior-most judges). The NJAC (2015, Fourth Judges Case) attempted to add executive representation but was struck down for violating the basic structure (judicial independence).

5. What is the "chilling effect" and where does it recur across this class's topics?

The chilling effect is the discouragement of legitimate activity (protest, dissent, whistleblowing) caused by fear of disproportionate state response or surveillance. It recurs in the right-to-protest discussion (aggressive FRT/drone surveillance discouraging participation in demonstrations) and echoes structurally in the Section 17A debate (fear of frivolous corruption complaints causing bureaucratic "play-safe syndrome" and policy paralysis) — both are cases where a security/accountability tool, if disproportionate, ends up suppressing the very activity a healthy democracy needs.

Himat Lal K. Shah v. Commissioner of Police, Ahmedabad — verified

Decided 15 September 1972 (reported at 1973 SCC context; also cited as 1972 INSC 220). Confirmed: the Court held freedom of assembly is essential to democracy, that public streets are natural places for expression of opinion, and drew a line between reasonable regulation (e.g. traffic management, requiring prior permission in some contexts) and an outright ban on assembly. Source: Indian Kanoon, full judgment; CaseMine commentary.

Dr. Ram Manohar Lohia v. State of Bihar — verified, citation corrected

Decided 7 September 1965, reported as 1966 AIR 740 (the lecture's "1966" reference matches the reported year). Confirmed: the case turned on the distinction between "public order" (broader, society-wide) and "law and order" (narrower), in the context of a preventive-detention order that cited the wrong ground. The "concentric circles" framing taught in class is a widely used pedagogical gloss on this and related public-order jurisprudence, not a verbatim phrase quoted from the judgment itself — safe to use analytically, but do not present it as a direct quotation from the Court. Source: Indian Kanoon, full judgment.

Amit Sahni v. Commissioner of Police (Shaheen Bagh) — verified

Citation: (2020) 10 SCC 573, decided 7 October 2020 by Justices Sanjay Kishan Kaul, Aniruddha Bose, and Krishna Murari. Confirmed: arose from over 100 days of anti-CAA protest blocking the Okhla underpass; the Court held protests must occur in designated areas and cannot continue indefinitely, and that no fundamental right is absolute. Source: Indian Kanoon, full judgment; Columbia Global Freedom of Expression case summary.

In Re: Ramlila Maidan Incident — verified

Decided 23 February 2012, reported as (2012) 5 SCC 1, arising from a June 2011 midnight police crackdown on a Baba Ramdev-led anti-corruption gathering. Confirmed: the Court held Section 144 CrPC orders are justified only where the threat to peace is real and imminent, not a mere likely possibility, and apportioned negligence 3:1 between the state and the organizing foundation. Source: Indian Kanoon, full judgment; LawFoyer case summary.

Anita Thakur v. State of Jammu & Kashmir — verified

Decided 12 August 2016. Confirmed: the Court held that where an assembly is peaceful, use of police force is not warranted at all, and that force used to disperse an unlawful assembly must remain reasonable and confined to what is necessary. Arose from a migrant protest march that escalated into police-crowd confrontation. Source: Full judgment text (rightofassembly.info); CaseMine commentary.

Maniyar Iliyaz v. P. Ayyappan (2026) — verified, case name corrected

Vineet Narain v. Union of India — verified

Decided 18 December 1997 (also referenced as (1998) 1 SCC 226), commonly called the Jain Hawala case.
