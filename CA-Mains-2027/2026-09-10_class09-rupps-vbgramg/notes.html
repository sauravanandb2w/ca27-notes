1. Topic 1: RUPPs — concepts, benefits & the registration process

0:33Class opens directly with the topic of registered unrecognised political parties (RUPPs).

2:02Instructor's framing: election-related questions are a recurring current-affairs theme in UPSC Mains — whether on simultaneous elections, election petitions, or right to vote — so this topic follows the same current-affairs pattern.

Suggested answer structure for any RUPP-type question

  • Introduction and context
  • Key benefits (recognized party vs. RUPP comparison)
  • Constitutional and legal framework
  • Data
  • Debate
  • Significance
  • Challenges/concerns
  • Important cases
  • Suggestions and way forward

Introduction framing suggested in class

  • Right to form associations is a fundamental right under Article 19(1)(c) of the Constitution.
    • Its unchecked application in the political arena has led to a proliferation of RUPPs
    • Many RUPPs exist only on paper
  • A better way to start an answer: link the constitutional dimension directly to the specific RUPP problem, rather than stating the right in isolation.

Context cited (media investigation)

Media investigation finding: six RUPPs, all based in Gujarat, received donations of around Rs 1,700 crore in FY 2023-24
Last year, the Election Commission of India delisted around 800 RUPPs

Key terms

Political party (definition used in class):
  • A voluntary association of people with a common ideology, formed to secure government power
  • An organized group of people who profess to share the same political views
  • Acts as a political unit to control the government
  • The mechanism through which it pursues this objective is contesting elections
  • Registration is mandatory for a voluntary association to contest elections in India.
    • Section 29A of the Representation of the People Act, 1951 governs this registration process
    • Lays down the procedure, conditions, and documentation required
  • Registration process:
    • Register the voluntary association with the Election Commission within 30 days of formation
    • Issue a public notice in newspapers (two national and two local dailies) so the public is aware a new political party has formed
    • If any objection is raised (e.g. a name clash with an existing party), the Election Commission scrutinizes it
    • Registration is finally granted after scrutiny clears any objections
  • India's political landscape:
    • Contrasted with the USA, where there are broadly only two major parties (Democrats and Republicans)
    • India has more than 2,800 registered political parties
    • Entry into forming and registering a political party in India is comparatively easy

Recognized political party vs. RUPP

  • Every newly formed political party starts as a registered but unrecognized political party.
  • If it contests elections and secures a specified percentage of votes or seats, it can be declared a recognized political party (state or national level).
  • Parties that contest but do not meet that vote/seat threshold remain RUPPs.
  • A RUPP can therefore be:
    • A completely new political party
    • A party that contested elections but did not meet the recognition parameters
    • A party that never contested any election at all

Benefits comparison — recognized parties vs. RUPPs

FeatureRecognized political partyRUPP
Election symbolPermanent reserved symbol (especially for national parties)No reserved symbol; only a common symbol available once a vote-share threshold is met in a prior election
Broadcasting rightsAllotted air time/slots on national mediaNo such facility
Star campaignersUp to 40 star campaigners allowedLimited to 20 star campaigners
Allocation of landSubsidized land for party offices in capital citiesNo such facility
Electoral rollsReceives two copiesNo such entitlement
Tax exemptionEnjoys tax exemption on donationsAlso enjoys tax exemption on donations — this shared benefit is the core of the funding-misuse problem

Instructor's framing: despite the gap in privileges above, RUPPs still retain the single biggest benefit — tax exemption — which is precisely why the group matters for scrutiny.

Constitutional and legal framework

  • Article 19(1)(c): guarantees the fundamental right to form associations and unions (including cooperatives) — the constitutional route for forming political parties.
  • Section 29A, RPA 1951: governs registration of political parties — procedure, conditions, documentation.
  • Section 29C, RPA 1951: mandates annual reporting to the Election Commission of contributions above Rs 20,000.
  • Section 12, Income Tax Act, 2025: exempts contributions received by registered political parties from income tax, subject to conditions:
    • Donations above Rs 2,000 must be paid other than in cash (cheque, DD, UPI, etc.)
    • Annual audit of books/accounts is required
The Rs 20,000 disclosure threshold (Section 29C) and the Rs 2,000 cash-donation threshold (IT Act Section 12) are the two specific regulatory loopholes to name explicitly in any "how is money laundered through RUPPs" answer.

2. Topic 1: Data, debates & the deregistration limitation

Key data cited in class

Six RUPPs (Gujarat-based) received donations of around Rs 1,700 crore in FY 2023-24
This exceeds the combined donations received by even nationally recognized political parties, excluding the BJP
Of over 2,800 RUPPs, only around 750 fielded candidates in the 2024 general election
Per Association for Democratic Reforms (ADR) 2025 report: only around 26% of RUPPs made their annual financial reports available in the public domain for FY 2022-23
That means roughly 75% of RUPPs are not updating bookkeeping/financial records at all

Core debate: freedom of association vs. financial integrity

  • In favour of low entry barriers:
    • Registering political parties is a fundamental right under Article 19(1)(c)
    • This is important for political pluralism
    • A minimal entry barrier lets diverse ideological voices and new socio-political movements participate
    • This deepens electoral and political democracy overall
  • The challenge — system abuse:
    • The same low-barrier system is exploited for tax evasion
    • Also exploited for a broader lack of financial integrity

Significance / positives of the RUPP framework

  • Vehicle for political pluralism: new socio-political movements and leaders can enter public life with minimal entry restrictions.
  • Financial incentives encourage formal political giving: tax exemptions encourage donors to give through a formal, traceable channel rather than informally.
  • Time for organizational growth: a newly formed party gets time to build cadre and public support based on its ideology before contesting a major election.

Challenges — how RUPPs are misused

  • Tax evasion and money laundering mechanism (illustrative example from class):
    • A donor contributes money (e.g. Rs 10 crore) to a RUPP functioning like a shell entity
    • The donation is tax-exempt for the donor
    • The RUPP records the amount as "expenditure" in its books
      • In reality, it returns most of that amount as cash to the donor
      • The RUPP keeps a percentage cut before returning the cash
    • The returned cash, now effectively laundered, is used by the donor for other purposes
    • Alternatively, the RUPP is used for "placing and layering" — splitting large sums into many small sub-Rs-20,000 donations across different names to avoid the disclosure requirement

Diagram: the two illustrative money-laundering routes discussed in class — direct cash-return, and placing/layering via sub-threshold donations.

  • Opacity in financial disclosures: only ~26% of RUPPs publicly disclose annual financial reports (ADR 2025 data, above).
  • Burden on election management:
    • Many RUPPs field only a handful of nominal candidates purely to remain listed as a "participating" political party
    • This still requires the full administrative machinery (ballot/EVM listing, etc.) without any genuine intent to contest
  • Exploitation of regulatory loopholes:
    • The Rs 20,000 disclosure threshold under Section 29C
    • The Rs 2,000 cash-donation threshold under the Income Tax Act
    • Fielding nominal candidates to avoid delisting

The Election Commission's deregistration limitation

  • The Election Commission has the power to register a political party, but not a general power to deregister one.
  • Deregistration power exists only in three specific circumstances, per the Supreme Court in Indian National Congress (I) v. Institute of Social Welfare (2002):
    • Registration was obtained by fraud (e.g. by submitting fraudulent documents)
    • The party's constitution was amended in violation of the mandatory undertaking under Section 29A(5) to bear true faith and allegiance to the Constitution of India
    • The party has been declared an unlawful association by the government under law
  • Delisting is not deregistration:
    • Delisting is a purely administrative exercise — removing a party's name from the published list of political parties
    • It does not amount to cancellation of the party's registration itself
    • Consequence: a delisted party loses the privileges associated with active listing (as discussed above)
  • A delisted or deregistered party can seek representation before the Election Commission, or approach a court, as a remedy.
Mnemonic for the three deregistration grounds: Fraud, Non-allegiance (unconstitutional amendment to party constitution), Unlawful association (declared by government) — "FNU."

3. Topic 1: Suggestions & live answer-writing exercise

Recommendations from institutional sources

  • Law Commission's 255th Report: recommended granting the Election Commission an explicit statutory power to deregister a political party if it fails to contest elections for 10 consecutive years.
  • Election Commission of India's Memorandum on Electoral Reforms, 2016: proposed amending Section 29A of the RPA 1951 to grant the ECI explicit deregistration power.

Additional suggestions discussed in class

  • Statutory amendment to the RPA, per the ECI's own memorandum of electoral reforms (treat this as the first-preference suggestion).
  • Introduce a vote-percentage threshold (e.g. at least 1% vote share) as a precondition for claiming tax exemption — links the benefit to genuine electoral participation.
  • Inter-agency data integration between the Election Commission, Central Board of Direct Taxes, Enforcement Directorate, and Financial Intelligence Unit, to cross-check tax-exemption claims against money-laundering red flags.
  • Lower the disclosure threshold below the current Rs 20,000 level, and make political donations fully digital instead of allowing cash transactions, to preserve an audit trail.
  • Tighten registration requirements generally.
  • Bring political parties under the Right to Information Act.
    • The Central Information Commission (CIC) has already ruled that political parties are "public authorities" under the RTI Act
    • Political parties have refused to comply with this CIC ruling

Political culture framing (India-specific analytical lens)

  • Three original political-culture types discussed (attributed to a scholar not clearly named in the transcript — see Supplementary research):
    • Modern political culture: the language of the Constitution, law, parliamentary debate, and judiciary
    • Traditional political culture: religion, caste, and social hierarchy shaping political behaviour
    • Saintly political culture: the Gandhian model — political action without expectation of personal gain, in service of society
  • A fourth, modern addition discussed in class: instrumental political culture — using politics purely as an instrument to achieve some other (often illegitimate) objective.
    • Instructor's framing: RUPP misuse for tax evasion/money laundering is precisely this instrumental political culture at work

    • A conclusion should offer administrative-style way-forward suggestions, not merely restate criticism

4. Topic 2: VB-G RAM G — from MGNREGA to a new rural employment law

47:29Second topic: Viksit Bharat – Guarantee for Rozgar and Ajeevika Mission (Gramin), abbreviated VB-G RAM G, referenced informally in class as "VBGRamG."

48:24Suggested introduction framing: "While MGNREGA served as a foundational social protection framework for two decades, its limitations necessitated legislative reform. Hence government enacted the VB-G RAM G Act to align with the vision of Viksit Bharat 2047."

  • Alternative introduction approach suggested: open with VB-G RAM G itself as bringing a paradigm shift in India's rural employment landscape, then mention MGNREGA as the predecessor being replaced.
  • Currency hook: the Act was passed in 2025, but officially replaced MGNREGA from 1 July 2026 onwards.

Constitutional basis

  • Article 41 (DPSP): obligates the state to secure the right to work.
  • Article 43 (DPSP): obligates the state to secure a living wage and decent working conditions.
  • Entry 23, Concurrent List: covers social security, social insurance, employment, and unemployment — the specific legislative entry under which MGNREGA (and now VB-G RAM G) was enacted.
  • Article 21 — right to livelihood:
    • The Supreme Court held the right to life includes the right to livelihood, in Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985)
    • This gives a rural employment program a fundamental-right dimension, layered on top of its DPSP basis
  • Fundamental right vs. DPSP debate:
    • The state's economic capacity and level of development constrain how far "right to work" can be treated as an enforceable fundamental right
    • This tension was directly raised in observations during a Supreme Court hearing on an Aruna Roy-filed petition (during the MGNREGA-to-VB-G-RAM-G transition period) — see case law below
    • Present legal status: right to work remains a DPSP obligation (Article 41), not a fundamental right

Structural shifts in India's rural employment landscape

  • Significant reduction in poverty:
    • NITI Aayog estimate: multi-dimensional poverty fell from 29% (2013-14) to 11.2% (2022-23)
    • More than 24 crore people lifted out of poverty over this period
    • Attributed to government welfare measures: housing schemes (PMAY-type), toilet/sanitation missions, the National Food Security Act, and other welfare programs
  • Diversification of rural household income:
    • Previously, rural households depended mainly on agriculture or the rural employment guarantee scheme
    • Now, increased internal migration provides an alternative income source
      • The Economic Survey highlights very high internal migration in India
      • Notably, language was expected to be a migration barrier but the Survey found it is not a significant barrier in practice
    • Migration generates both financial remittances (direct income) and "social remittances" (knowhow, skill development, improved standard of living)
    • Growing gig-economy and logistics-sector penetration is shifting rural employment from farm to non-farm opportunities
    • Increased rural asset ownership (mobile phones, two-wheelers, televisions) signals rising living standards
    • Unconditional cash transfers ("freebies") are also cited as a factor reshaping the rural employment landscape
For any "changing rural employment landscape" answer, use this five-point cluster: poverty decline, migration/remittances, gig economy, rising asset ownership, and cash transfers — then connect it to why a scheme redesign (VB-G RAM G) became necessary.

5. Topic 2: Key features, fund-sharing & special provisions

Two objectives of VB-G RAM G

  • Guarantee rural wage employment and thereby income security.
  • Create durable assets / capital formation in rural areas — a clear shift in emphasis compared to MGNREGA.

Key feature-by-feature comparison with MGNREGA

FeatureMGNREGAVB-G RAM G
Days of guaranteed employment 100 days per household per year 125 days per household per year
Seasonal timing / job allocation logic No specific seasonal restriction; allocation could occur in the peak agricultural season too — creating "lean vs. peak" ambiguity Explicitly a lean-season scheme
  • States can notify up to 60 "peak agricultural season" days when the scheme's job guarantee does not apply
  • During the peak season, labourers are expected to instead access better-paying agricultural work in the open market
Thematic/priority areas No specific priority-area framework Four thematic priority areas:
  • Water security (pond regeneration, check dams, groundwater recharge, irrigation channels)
  • Rural infrastructure (roads, waste management)
  • Livelihood-related infrastructure (skill development centres, agri markets, cold storage/agri value-chain facilities)
  • Disaster mitigation (cyclone/flood shelters, forest-fire management)
Wage fund-sharing (Centre:State) 100% of wages borne by the Centre 60:40 for general-category states; 90:10 for special-category (northeastern/Himalayan) states
Fund allocation model Demand-driven, open-ended — states could exceed the initial budget if demand rose, later reflected in revised estimates Normative allocation — a fixed, formula/parameter-based budget is set upfront for each state (comparable to a Finance Commission-style allocation model)
Work-provision timeline Work to be provided within a specified period after demand (parallel unemployment-allowance safeguard existed) Work must be provided within 15 days
Unemployment allowance conditionality Available, but conditioned on the worker physically presenting themselves for work after applying — failure to present meant disentitlement Retained, but the disentitlement condition for failing to present is removed
Digital ecosystem Limited digital tracking Expanded — biometric authentication, real-time worksite monitoring, geotagging

Additional governance and social provisions

  • Gram Sabhas must conduct social audits at least once every six months.
  • Special colour-coded Gram Rozgar Guarantee cards introduced for vulnerable groups:
    • Single women
    • Persons with disabilities
    • Elderly persons above 60 years
    • Released bonded labourers
    • Transgender persons
  • Card holders can be prioritized and offered customized/tailored work sites suited to their needs.
  • A National Steering Committee monitors how the normative allocation is distributed across states.

6. Topic 2: Debates, data, case law & live answer-writing exercise

Debate 1 — continuous employment vs. farm-labour bargaining power

  • Under MGNREGA:
    • During the peak agricultural season, a labourer had a genuine choice — continue MGNREGA work or move to better-paying private farm labour
    • This choice gave unskilled workers better bargaining power and thus better wages
    • Without this option, the only fallback for many unskilled workers historically was bonded labour
  • Under VB-G RAM G:
    • The scheme deliberately withdraws work during the 60-day peak season
    • This pushes all available labour into the open peak-season market simultaneously — a labour surplus
    • Labour surplus lowers bargaining power, which lowers wages
    • Net effect: a worker who previously got a better MGNREGA-linked bargaining position during peak season may now get less

Debate 2 — open-ended (demand-driven) vs. normative allocation

  • MGNREGA's demand-driven model:
    • Not budgeted/capped — work allocated whenever villages demanded it
    • Responsive to actual rural distress — rising demand for MGNREGA work signalled a real underlying problem in the rural economy
  • VB-G RAM G's normative allocation model:
    • Funds distributed based on a fixed formula, not demand — it is budgeted
    • Advantage: poorer regions can be guaranteed more funds by design, addressing regional inequity
    • Under the old demand-driven model, a "Matthew effect" existed
      • States with greater administrative capacity captured disproportionately more allocation
      • These tended to be wealthier, more proactive states
    • Trade-off: normative allocation offers predictability over responsiveness to real-time distress signals

Debate 3 — centralization, unfunded mandate & wage suppression

  • MGNREGA: centrally mandated and centrally funded (100% of wages) — a "center-mandated, center-funded" model.
  • VB-G RAM G: centrally mandated, but only partially funded (60% wages from the Centre in general states) — an "unfunded mandate" problem (the term is linked in class to Professor Sudha's/"Sondesai's" work on this concept — see Supplementary research for the name uncertainty).
    • States must now find the remaining 40% themselves, raising state fiscal burden
    • If a state cannot pay its share promptly, the Centre may ultimately have to provide it as a grant — still creating fiscal exposure for the Centre in practice
  • Wage-suppression risk:
    • The Centre notifies a base wage rate that may be lower than a state's own notified minimum wage
    • Since the base rate is centrally set, it can suppress effective wages relative to a state's minimum wage floor
    • Suggested fix: index the base wage rate to the Consumer Price Index (CPI) so it rises with inflation

Key data cited in class

Economic Survey data: MGNREGA demand declined by around 53% between FY 2020-21 and FY 2025-26
Rural unemployment decreased from 3.3% to 2.5% between 2021 and 2023-24
MGNREGA allocation for FY 2025-26: Rs 86,000 crore (transition-year figure)
Household participation in FY 2025-26: 5.78 crore households
World Bank study ("Does India's Employment Guarantee Scheme Guarantee Employment?"): proportion of MGNREGA job applicants actually getting work — only ~22% in Bihar, ~48% in Uttar Pradesh, ~56% national average

Implementation-gap concerns (carried over as a general critique, not VB-G RAM G-specific)

  • Expenditure often does not match physical progress on the ground — an implementation gap.
  • Digital evasion: ghost beneficiaries recorded via manipulated attendance photos, or machines used in place of labourers while full wages are still claimed.
  • Contractor-Panchayat collusion: a nexus siphoning off allocated funds, indicating corruption risk even at the grassroots level.

Pros and cons of the VB-G RAM G shift

  • Pros:
    • Better fiscal discipline via a budgeted, normative allocation
    • Removal of the disentitlement clause helps vulnerable workers who could not physically present themselves for procedural reasons
    • Stronger durable-asset creation focus and technological transparency (biometric/facial recognition, DPI-based systems)
    • More statutory backing overall
  • Cons:
    • Shift from an open-ended, bottom-up, demand-driven approach to a more centralized, top-down, capped approach — a potential dilution of the practical experience of a "right to work"
    • Unfunded mandate — increased state fiscal burden
    • Lower bargaining power for workers during the peak season (discussed above)
    • Techno-solutionism risk — digital exclusion for workers who cannot be reliably authenticated digitally
    • Wage suppression risk via a centrally notified base rate lower than a state's minimum wage
    • Overall centralization of a scheme that previously had more state-level flexibility

Case law and comparative example

Aruna Roy petition — Supreme Court observations (during the MGNREGA-to-VB-G-RAM-G transition period):
  • Case concerned pending MGNREGA wage payments and compensation for delays
  • Court observed that MGNREGA was a "salutary scheme" — very effective, doing a wonderful job in rural India
  • Court held MGNREGA is neither a freebie nor an exploitation of rural workers
  • Court separately questioned whether the statutory guarantee of rural employment can be equated with the fundamental right to life and dignity under Article 21, noting the Constitution does not recognize a fundamental right to work
Tamil Nadu's expanded scheme (state-level comparative example):
  • Tamil Nadu introduced an additional 25 days of guaranteed work (referenced in class as a "150-day scheme"), on top of the base 125 days
  • Brings total guaranteed days to 150 — a further state-level expansion beyond the central VB-G RAM G floor

Suggestions / way forward

  • Provide a transition compensation grant to states facing increased fiscal burden from the 40% wage-sharing requirement — modeled on the GST compensation-grant approach.
  • Implement a worksite manual-exception protocol: if a worker is physically present but biometric/facial-recognition authentication fails, still record and pay them, to avoid unjust digital exclusion.
  • Advance seasonal work calendars: consult the Gram Sabha ahead of the peak season and release the labour calendar in advance, so workers can plan continuously between scheme work and market work.
  • Strengthen grassroots administrative capacity using the scheme's ~9% administrative-expenditure allocation, for training and technical capability building.
  • Address rural wage stagnation by raising the centrally notified base wage rate, ideally indexed to CPI.

Glossary — quick revision

RUPP
Registered Unrecognised Political Party — a party registered with the Election Commission under Section 29A but which has not met the vote/seat threshold for national or state recognition (or has never contested elections at all).
Section 29A, RPA 1951
Governs the registration of political parties — procedure, conditions, and documentation; allows registration but not deregistration.
Section 29C, RPA 1951
Mandates annual reporting to the Election Commission of political contributions above Rs 20,000 — a disclosure threshold widely seen as a loophole below that amount.
Delisting vs. deregistration
Delisting is a purely administrative removal from the published list of parties (reversible, no legal cancellation); deregistration is complete legal cancellation of registration, available only on three Supreme Court-recognized grounds.
Indian National Congress (I) v. Institute of Social Welfare (2002)
Supreme Court held the ECI cannot deregister a party except for (i) fraud in registration, (ii) a party constitution amendment violating the Section 29A(5) allegiance undertaking, or (iii) the party being declared unlawful by the government.
Instrumental political culture
A modern addition to India's traditional/modern/saintly political-culture typology — politics used purely as an instrument to achieve an unrelated (often illegitimate) objective, such as tax evasion via a RUPP.
VB-G RAM G
Viksit Bharat – Guarantee for Rozgar and Ajeevika Mission (Gramin) Act — replaced MGNREGA from 1 July 2026; 125-day rural employment guarantee with a lean-season focus, normative allocation, and four thematic priority areas.
Normative allocation
A fixed, formula/parameter-based budget set in advance for each state (Finance Commission-style), replacing MGNREGA's open-ended, demand-driven allocation model.
Unfunded mandate
A central law imposing an obligation (125 days' guaranteed employment) on states without fully funding it — the Centre bears 60% (90% for special-category states) of wages, leaving states to fund the remainder.
Matthew effect (rural-scheme context)
Under a demand-driven allocation model, states with greater pre-existing administrative capacity captured disproportionately more funding — "the rich get richer" dynamic that normative allocation is partly designed to correct.

Revision kit (ARCS)

A — Active Recall: self-test before re-reading

1. What are the three grounds on which the Election Commission can deregister a political party, per the 2002 Supreme Court ruling?

Per Indian National Congress (I) v. Institute of Social Welfare (2002): (i) registration obtained by fraud, (ii) the party's constitution amended in violation of the Section 29A(5) allegiance undertaking to the Constitution, or (iii) the party declared an unlawful association by the government. Outside these three grounds, the ECI can only "delist" a party administratively, not deregister it.

2. Why does RUPP misuse persist despite Section 29C's disclosure requirement?

Section 29C only mandates disclosure of contributions above Rs 20,000 to the Election Commission; donations at or below that threshold require no reporting. Combined with the Income Tax Act's Rs 2,000 cash-donation threshold, this creates two regulatory loopholes that let donors split large sums into smaller, undisclosed, or cash contributions — enabling tax evasion and money laundering via RUPPs.

3. What are the four thematic priority areas introduced under VB-G RAM G that did not exist under MGNREGA?

Water security (pond regeneration, check dams, groundwater recharge, irrigation channels); rural infrastructure (roads, waste management); livelihood-related infrastructure (skill centres, agri markets, cold storage/value-chain facilities); and disaster mitigation (cyclone/flood shelters, forest-fire management).

4. How does the cost-sharing pattern for wages differ between MGNREGA and VB-G RAM G?

Under MGNREGA, the Centre bore 100% of the unskilled wage bill. Under VB-G RAM G, the wage cost is now shared 60:40 (Centre:State) for general-category states, and 90:10 for special-category northeastern/Himalayan states — creating a new state fiscal burden described in class as an "unfunded mandate."

5. What did the Supreme Court observe about MGNREGA in the Aruna Roy petition, and what question did it leave open?

The Court observed that MGNREGA was a "salutary scheme" — effective and doing a wonderful job in rural India — and was neither a freebie nor an exploitation of rural workers. It separately questioned (without resolving in the workers' favour) whether the statutory guarantee of rural employment can be equated with the fundamental right to life and dignity under Article 21, noting the Constitution does not recognize a fundamental right to work.

Supplementary research — filling gaps

Indian National Congress (I) v. Institute of Social Welfare (2002) — verified, nuance added

Confirmed: decided 10 May 2002. The Supreme Court held the Election Commission has no general power to deregister a political party under the Representation of the People Act, 1951. Deregistration is permissible only where: (i) registration was obtained by fraud/forgery, (ii) the party amended its constitution in a manner inconsistent with the Section 29A(5) undertaking of allegiance to the Constitution, or (iii) the party has been declared unlawful. The lecture's framing (three grounds) matches this, though the second ground is more precisely a constitutional-amendment violation than a general "non-allegiance" declaration by the party itself. Source: Indian Kanoon, full judgment; Drishti IAS summary.

Section 29A, RPA 1951 — verified

Confirmed: allows any association or body of citizens calling itself a political party to apply for registration with the Election Commission. Requires an application within 30 days of formation, including party particulars, membership details, and office-bearer names; requires publication of the proposed party name in two national and two local daily newspapers, with a 30-day objection window before the Commission decides. Source: PIB — Political Parties Registration Tracking Management System; Indian Kanoon, Section 29A text.

Law Commission's 255th Report — verified

Confirmed: the Law Commission of India's 255th Report (2015) recommended amending the RPA 1951 to give the Election Commission explicit statutory power to deregister a political party that fails to contest assembly or parliamentary elections for 10 consecutive years. The Election Commission echoed a similar recommendation in its 2016 Memorandum of Electoral Reforms. As of 2025-26 reporting, this recommendation has not yet been enacted into law. Source: Superkalam — ECI deregistration explainer; PWOnlyIAS — Derecognition and deregistration explainer.

Six Gujarat RUPPs — Rs 1,700 crore donation figure — verified, party names identified

Confirmed via multiple 2026 reports: six Gujarat-based RUPPs — Aam Janmat Party, Bharatiya National Janata Dal, Garib Kalyan Party, New India United Party, Satyawadi Rakshak Party, and Swatantrata Abhivyakti Party — received donations of around Rs 1,700 crore in FY 2023-24, exceeding the combined Rs 1,480 crore received by five nationally recognized parties (excluding BJP) in the same period. The parties fielded only around 15 candidates combined in the 2024 general election. Aam Janmat Party alone reportedly received around Rs 620 crore. Source: National Herald; Newskarnataka.

VB-G RAM G Act — verified, key figures confirmed

Confirmed: the Viksit Bharat – Guarantee for Rozgar and Ajeevika Mission (Gramin) Act, 2025 came into force 1 July 2026, replacing MGNREGA. Statutory 125-day employment guarantee (up from 100 days). Fund-sharing pattern for wages, materials, and administrative costs: 60:40 (Centre:State) for general states, 90:10 for northeastern/Himalayan states — a change from MGNREGA's near-100%-Centre-funded wage bill. State-wise normative allocation set annually using the 16th Finance Commission's horizontal devolution formula; spending beyond this ceiling becomes a state liability. Interim base wage floor of Rs 300/day introduced. Source: Vajiram & Ravi; Outlook Business; PRS Legislative Research.

Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985) — verified

Confirmed: decided 10 July 1985 by a five-judge bench. Held that the right to life under Article 21 includes the right to livelihood, since "no person can live without the means of living." Notably, the Court still upheld the Municipality's power to evict pavement/slum dwellers on procedural grounds, softened only by directions for humane treatment — a nuance worth noting if this case is cited for more than the bare "right to livelihood" holding. Source: Case overview; Indian Kanoon, full judgment.

Aruna Roy petition on MGNREGA — verified, direction of holding confirmed

Confirmed: Supreme Court observations (2026, during the MGNREGA-to-VB-G-RAM-G transition) described MGNREGA as effective, a "good, effective welfare scheme," and rejected the framing of it as a freebie or exploitation of rural workers. The Court separately questioned whether a statutory rural-employment guarantee can be equated with the Article 21 fundamental right to life/dignity, noting the Constitution does not recognize a fundamental right to work — this is a live, not fully settled, judicial observation rather than a final declaration either way. Source: ETV Bharat; Bar and Bench.

MGNREGA demand decline, FY 2020-21 to FY 2025-26 — verified, 53% figure confirmed

Confirmed via Economic Survey 2025-26: person-days of work generated under MGNREGA fell from a pandemic peak of 3,890 million in 2020-21 to about 1,837.7 million in 2025-26 (up to 31 December 2025) — a drop of just over 53%. This resolves the instructor's in-class self-correction between "56%" and "53%" in favour of 53%. Source: The Wire, citing Economic Survey 2025-26; PIB — Economic Survey 2025-26.

World Bank MGNREGA study — verified, national & Bihar figures confirmed

Study identified: Dutta, Murgai, Ravallion & van de Walle, "Does India's Employment Guarantee Scheme Guarantee Employment?" (World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 6003, 2012; also published in EPW) and the related "Right to Work? Assessing India's Employment Guarantee Scheme in Bihar." Confirmed findings: nationally, about 44% of households wanting NREGA work did not get it — i.e. roughly 56% did, matching the class's "~56% national average" figure. In Bihar, the rationing rate was around 79% — i.e. roughly 21% got work, closely matching the class's "~22% in Bihar" figure. The Uttar Pradesh figure (class cited ~48%) could not be tied to the exact same survey round in the sources checked — treat it as directionally plausible but verify against the original paper's state-wise table before citing precisely. Source: World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 6003 (PDF).
