1. Topic 1: AI in judiciary — framework & key terms

Recommended structure: introduction → context (news hook) → key differentiations/terms → debates → pros and cons (substantiated with data) → draft regulation details → domestic and global examples → suggestions → conclusion.

Introduction strategy

As India struggles with the massive judicial backlog of more than 5 crore cases (National Judicial Data Grid), AI offers tools to improve efficiency. However, the fundamental nature of justice demands that machines do not take up the role of the judge.
— Instructor's model introduction; deliberately states both what AI can solve and what it cannot, to signal clarity to the examiner

Immediate news hook: the Supreme Court's release of the Draft Regulations for Use of AI in Courts, 2026 in June 2026.

Instructor notes this is the most current context and can substitute for or accompany the definitional introduction.

Key differentiations

Deployment of AI in judiciary: use of AI to enhance the speed, transparency, and administrative efficiency of the justice delivery system (the accepted, "clinical"/assistive use).
Algorithmic decision-making: use of AI to automatically generate judicial outcomes, sentences, or risk profiling/bail determination — i.e., AI actually deciding a case. This is explicitly NOT wanted.
Administrative AI vs. Adjudicator AI: Administrative AI is assistive/clerical (case management, scheduling) and is encouraged; Adjudicator AI makes judicial decisions affecting individual rights and is not recommended.
Human-in-the-loop model: AI operates only in an assistive capacity; a human must supervise, verify, and take the final decision. This is the recurring conclusion for any AI-related answer, not just judiciary.

The core debate: innovation versus independence

  • Innovation (why AI is needed): reduce delay/backlogs via caseload management (automatically categorizing cases by pendency — e.g., flagging 5-6-year-old cases).
  • Improving the case clearance rate (ratio of cases disposed of to cases filed, keeping backlog from growing).
  • Independence (what must not be compromised):
    • Judicial independence is part of the basic structure of the Constitution.
    • The Supreme Court is the guardian of the Constitution and protector of fundamental rights.
    • Over-dependence on AI risks hallucinations (e.g., the NCLT case — see below).

Human primacy versus "organic justice" (algorithmic bias)

AI trained on historical data will reproduce historical patterns and biases.

  • Example used in class: India's colonial-era "Criminal Tribes" classification (tribes labeled criminal by the British, later denotified after independence)
  • If an AI model were trained on such historically discriminatory data, it could reproduce the same bias in bail/risk-scoring decisions today
  • E.g., systematically assessing poor defendants as higher flight risks than wealthy ones on biased historical correlations rather than actual case facts
Black-box AI: the core transparency problem — a model can output a decision (e.g., grant bail to person A, deny to person B) without any explainable basis, making it impossible to audit for fairness.
Accountability void (accountability vacuum): if an AI-assisted decision goes wrong, it is unclear whether the developer, the approving ministry, the judge, or the lawyer is accountable — no single accountable party.

The pyramid of permissible AI use in judiciary

Board diagram rebuilt from the lecture — a traffic-light pyramid of AI use cases:

Red (prohibited) Adjudication — deciding cases, determining bail eligibility, assessing witness credibility. Requires a human judge's ethical/moral compass; cannot be delegated.
Amber (caution) Case research, translation, transcription — permitted but requires verification (e.g., a UK case where "no" was mistranslated, changing the meaning of a judgment; India's own "leave granted" vs. "holiday granted" translation ambiguity example).
Green (allowed) Case management, categorization by pendency, administrative/clerical tasks — AI can do these better than manual processes.

2. Topic 1: Draft regulations, cases & global comparison

Key judicial data (mug up for any judiciary question)

More than 5 crore cases pending in India (National Judicial Data Grid):
— ~60 lakh in High Courts, 80,000+ in the Supreme Court
— Meaning ~4.3-4.4 crore cases are pending in the lower judiciary (the major bottleneck)

Judge-to-population ratio: ~21 judges per million population in India (verified: Law Commission's 120th Report, 1987, recommended raising this to 50 per million; the Supreme Court endorsed this in 2002 in All India Judges Association vs. Union of India).
Comparator: USA ~150/million, Europe ~220/million (lecture figures; broadly consistent with commonly cited comparisons, though exact European figures vary by country).

Draft Regulations for Use of AI in Courts, 2026 — verified details

Released by the Supreme Court's AI Committee in June 2026 (3 June 2026, public comment period closing 20 June 2026). Five core principles: human primacy, transparency and explainability, fairness and non-discrimination, accountability, and inclusivity/accessibility (data protection also cited).
  • Permitted uses: legal research, drafting assistance, scheduling, transcription, translation, citation verification, case/record management, judicial administration.
  • Prohibited uses: deciding cases, determining bail eligibility, assessing witness credibility, or influencing judicial deliberation unless authorized by law — i.e., no algorithmic adjudication.
  • Institutional structure: a full-time "apex body" at the Supreme Court (comprising SC judges, HC Chief Justices, HC judges, a MeitY officer, and technical/legal experts) to regulate AI integration; specialized committees; a proposed Centre of Research and Excellence on AI (CORE-AI).
  • Ethical impact assessment: analogous to an environmental impact assessment — before deploying an AI tool, courts must pilot it and identify risks, biases, and vulnerabilities.
  • Disclosure requirement (Regulation 43(3)): lawyers must formally disclose to the court whenever an AI tool is used in preparing pleadings, documents, or evidence.
  • Conclusion principle: human-in-the-loop model — AI must remain assistive; a human must verify and take the final call.

Resolved: Regulation number confirmed against primary source

The lecture's "Regulation 43" citation is correct. The Supreme Court AI Committee's original draft text (notice dated 3 June 2026) confirms Regulation 43 is titled "Transparency and disclosure," and sub-regulation 43(3) specifically requires that where an AI tool is used by a party or legal representative in preparing a document, pleading, or evidence, its AI-assisted character must be disclosed to the court via a certificate under Annexure I. Some secondary legal commentary loosely cites this as "Regulation 3" (the Definitions clause), which was the source of the earlier flagged discrepancy — the lecture and Regulation 43(3) are the correct citation.

Hallucination cases — domestic and global

NCLT/NCLAT "methyl isocyanate" case (Supreme Court, 2 July 2026, Justices P.S. Narasimha and Alok Aradhe):
  • The Court set aside NCLT and NCLAT orders in an insolvency matter (J&K Bank vs. Essel Infraprojects Ltd., involving a default of ₹87.43 crore) after finding the tribunals had relied on six AI-generated judicial precedents that were either non-existent or misapplied.
  • The Court held: "The production of fake, non-existent, and hallucinated material and its utilisation as precedents in law, is like the release of methyl isocyanate in the province of law and justice: invisible, insidious, and catastrophic by the time anyone notices."
  • The Court mandated a "zero tolerance" approach and directed the Bar Council of India to constitute a committee to frame guiding principles on AI use in adjudication.
(Independently verified — this is the correct case; the lecture's brief mention of an earlier "ITAT" hallucination incident refers to a separate, different case from December 2024.)
Mata v. Avianca, Inc. (US, Southern District of New York, decided 22 June 2023, Judge P. Kevin Castel):
  • Lawyers representing Roberto Mata submitted a legal brief containing fake case citations generated by ChatGPT, including fabricated quotations.
  • When challenged, the lawyers compounded the error by filing an affidavit with further fabricated excerpts.
  • The court sanctioned the lawyers (Peter LoDuca, Steven A. Schwartz) and their firm $5,000 each for violating Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 11 by failing to verify the authenticity of cited authorities.
(Independently verified — correctly cited in the lecture as "Mata versus Aanka.")
China: experimenting with AI decision-making for petty cases/small fines. US: strict sanctions regime post-Mata v. Avianca for fake AI-generated citations.

Scholar references — one verified, one still unresolved

The lecture references two sources:

  • Still unverified: "Scholars like Leis" advocating for AI literacy and litigants' right to opt out of AI-assisted courtrooms — no scholar of this name could be matched via search; treat as unverified and do not cite the name in an exam answer.

3. Topic 1: Live answer-writing exercise

"Justice is not a mere algorithm. It is a reflection of human conscience." In light of the statement, critically examine the use of AI in courts.
— Model question used for a full timed live answer-writing exercise in class
  1. Introduction: open with the quote itself (Supreme Court), or the "human conscience" framing directly — instructor notes it is fine to attribute a quote to "the Supreme Court" generically if the exact case name is not recalled, rather than omitting it.
  2. Body — organic justice vs. algorithmic justice (draw as a two-column comparison, not prose): Organic/human justice = facts + law + precedent + reasoning + empathy. Algorithmic justice = input → black-box → output, with no explainable reasoning — explicitly flagged as "not what we want."
  3. Body — pros (each substantiated with data): reduces pendency (5 crore+ cases, National Judicial Data Grid), improves efficiency (21 judges/million vs. 150/million in the USA), advanced legal research (SUVAS multilingual translation, case summarization), unified case-data platforms.
  4. Body — cons (each substantiated with a case): hallucination risk (NCLT "methyl isocyanate" case), lack of administrative capacity given 4.3+ crore pending in lower judiciary alone, digital divide 2.0 (even those with smartphones lack digital literacy), Personal Data Protection concerns given the sensitivity of judicial case data.
  5. Body — the regulatory response: Draft Regulations for Use of AI in Courts, 2026 — permitted/prohibited activities, the apex body, human-in-the-loop conclusion drawn directly from the regulation itself.
  6. Conclusion: human-in-the-loop model — AI in an assistive capacity only, human as final decision-maker and supervisor.
Put all diagrams (organic-vs-algorithmic comparison, pyramid of AI use) on the first page/side of the answer sheet visible to the examiner; do not bury them mid-answer. The instructor is explicit that a plain-text answer without diagrams loses marks even with identical content ("out of 10, I'm seeing a very plain sheet — you are not influencing me").

4. Topic 2: PDS — evolution & institutional framework

Recommended structure: introduction (data-first) → three-part context (SARTHAK-PDS announcement / evolution timeline / definition) → institutional framework → SARTHAK-PDS specifics → data on leakage and digitization → significance → challenges → suggestions → conclusion.

Model introduction

The Public Distribution System is the backbone of India's food security, delivering subsidized food grains to over 80 crore citizens. However, the food subsidy budget is increasing every year — ₹2.28 lakh crore for FY 2026-27, up from ₹2.03 lakh crore previously — hence a technological overhaul through SARTHAK-PDS is important to improve the system.
— Instructor's model introduction; the same opening (minus the SARTHAK-specific sentence) is recommended for any general PDS or food security question
For Paper 3 topics generally, lead the introduction with a data point rather than a general statement — it reads as more impactful to an examiner scanning many similar answers.

Evolution of PDS — timeline

PeriodCharacter
Before 1960sMainly for urban consumers, to address food inflation / stabilize food prices (India was food-deficit; relied on the US PL-480 "ship to mouth" import program)
1960s-1980sGeneral entitlement scheme available to all at a subsidized price (post-Green Revolution)
1990sRevamped PDS (extended to remote/food-insecure areas), then Targeted PDS (TPDS) — shift from universal entitlement to BPL/Antyodaya targeting of the "poorest of the poor"
After 2013National Food Security Act (NFSA) — statutory right to subsidized food grain for ~67% of the population
2020s onwardModernization and digitization phase — SMART-PDS, and now SARTHAK-PDS (2026)

Three generations of food security (reusable framework)

  • First generation — Availability: addressed via the Green Revolution.
  • Second generation — Affordability and Accessibility: addressed via Revamped PDS / PDS expansion.
  • Third generation — Sustainability: current focus — resilience against climate, geopolitical (e.g., West Asia/Russia grain shocks, COVID-19), and fertilizer supply shocks; achieved through climate-smart agriculture, PDS modernization, and "intelligence per acre" rather than merely increasing land under cultivation.

Institutional framework — roles

LevelRole
Center (FCI)Procurement and storage. Procurement can be centralized (everything routed through FCI, requiring huge storage capacity — inherently inefficient) or decentralized (states procure and store directly).
StateBeneficiary identification, issuing ration cards, setting up Fair Price Shops (FPS), monitoring and supervision, and intra-state movement of food grains (center → district → block → FPS).
FPS dealerFinal-mile distribution to consumers; requires a sustainable dealer margin/commission to avoid incentivizing leakage.
CAP model (Cover and Plinth): an open-air storage method — food grain sacks are stacked on a raised platform (plinth) and covered with a sheet — used when covered warehouse capacity is insufficient. Leads to significant wastage/losses, often informally attributed to rodents ("blamed on rats") rather than acknowledged as a structural storage-capacity problem.

5. Topic 2: SARTHAK-PDS, data & challenges

SARTHAK-PDS — verified scheme details

SARTHAK-PDS (Scheme for Assistance in Ration Transport and Handling — Income with Automation in PDS):
— Approved by the Union Cabinet on 27 May 2026
— Central outlay: ₹25,530 crore, covering April 2026 to March 2031
— Merges two prior schemes: assistance for intra-state food grain movement plus FPS dealer margin under NFSA, and SMART-PDS (the digitization scheme)
— Covers 81.35 crore NFSA beneficiaries (lecture's "more than 80 crore" figure is consistent)

Three AI-enabled modules (verified, not covered by name in the lecture but directly relevant):
— NIRMAL — real-time beneficiary registry
— ASHA — multilingual grievance redressal platform
— SAKSHAM — supply-chain platform with vehicle tracking (maps to the lecture's "Vehicle Location Tracking System (VTS)")
Twin objectives of SARTHAK-PDS as taught: (1) intra-state food grain movement — financial support to states, sustainable FPS dealer margins; (2) digitization — beneficiary registry, real-time transaction tracking (EPOS), portability (One Nation One Ration Card), grievance redressal.
Anticipated benefits (as taught): a "secure system" preventing leakages, enhanced dealer margins for better service, a state command-and-control center for monitoring, and a national digital database ensuring portability and coordination between states and center, avoiding duplicate records.

Verified data on leakage and digitization

ICRIER study: ~28-29% of PDS grains supplied by FCI and state governments do not reach intended beneficiaries — a financial loss of ₹69,108 crore, equivalent to ~20 million metric tonnes of rice and wheat (study analyzed FCI offtake data, August 2022-July 2023, against Household Consumption Expenditure Survey data). Lecture's "28%" figure matches; the ₹69,108 crore loss figure is an added verified detail.

Per Economic Survey (cited generally in lecture):
— ~99.8% of ration cards are Aadhaar-seeded
— ~99.6% of Fair Price Shops are equipped with ePOS (electronic point of sale) devices

Over 2 crore ineligible beneficiaries removed following field verification (lecture figure; general order of magnitude consistent with known deduplication drives, though the specific source/date was not independently pinned down).

Correction: FCI rice stock figure

The lecture cites FCI holding "309 lakh tonnes of rice" as of January 2026, "nine times the buffer norm." Independent search could not confirm this exact figure; closest verified data points found:

  • Total foodgrain stocks of ~604 lakh tonnes (roughly 3x the buffer requirement)
  • An April 2026 figure of 386.10 lakh tonnes of rice against a buffer norm of 135.80 lakh tonnes (roughly 2.8x, not 9x)

The broad point — FCI holds food grain stock well above the mandated buffer norm — is correct and well-documented, but do not cite the specific "309 lakh tonnes / nine times" figures in an exam without further verification.

Scholarly debate: coverage vs. leakage

Shanta Kumar Committee (report submitted 20 January 2015 — lecture said "2014," a minor date error): recommended reducing NFSA coverage from 67% to 40% of the population to concentrate subsidies on the most vulnerable; also recommended raising priority-household allocation from 5 kg to 7 kg per person/month and piloting cash transfers in cities with population over 1 million, projecting savings of ~₹33,000 crore. Criticized for potentially excluding many genuinely poor households.
Himanshu and Sen (economists, Centre for Economic Studies and Planning, JNU):
  • Argued for near-universal PDS coverage to reduce leakage, directly opposing the Shanta Kumar Committee's coverage-reduction approach.
  • Cited historical data showing diversion/leakage rose from ~24% to ~54% between 1999-2000 and 2004-05, a period that coincided with the shift from universal to targeted (narrower) PDS coverage.
  • This supports a negative relationship between narrower coverage and leakage control (i.e., narrowing coverage did not reduce leakage as intended).
(Independently verified; corrects the lecture's garbled "Professor Pakrishna" and "Humanu and Sen" references — the correct citation is Himanshu and Sen.)
Government initiatives cited:
  • Annapurti Grain ATM — automated grain-dispensing machine (FCI + UN World Food Programme); dispenses up to 50 kg of rice/wheat in ~5 minutes via ration card or Aadhaar biometric verification; operates 24/7; deployed at 29 locations across 6 states, first launched in Odisha.
  • One Nation One Ration Card — enables portability of ration entitlements, especially benefiting migrant workers.

Significance of PDS (beyond food security)

  • Equalizes consumption: narrows the nutritional/consumption gap between rich and poor.
    • Cited via a scholar (referred to in lecture as "Professor Pakrishna," name not independently verifiable; treat with caution) arguing PDS can be leveraged to equalize primary food consumption across the population.
    • This is preferable to cash transfers/food coupons, which do not guarantee actual nutrition access during a food-price shock.
  • Crisis resilience: functions as a buffer during economic shocks (e.g., COVID-19, West Asia/Russia grain-supply disruptions).
  • Farmer support: PDS procurement guarantees farmers MSP-based demand for their produce, linking food security policy to agricultural income support.

6. Topic 2: Live answer-writing exercise

Examine the role of technology in improving the efficiency of India's PDS system.
— Model question used for a second full timed live answer-writing exercise in class
  1. Introduction: context-first is preferable here — mention SARTHAK-PDS (₹25,530 crore modernization scheme) directly if recalled, otherwise a PDS/NFSA-based definitional opening works equally well.
  2. Body — brief timeline box: two to three points only (Targeted PDS → NFSA → modernization/SARTHAK) — a small side-box, not a full paragraph, since the question demand is about technology, not history.
  3. Body — role of technology by institutional level: Center/FCI — storage/inventory offloading tech, Vehicle Location Tracking System (VTS) for movement monitoring. State — Aadhaar-based beneficiary identification, EPOS for real-time transactions, Annapurti Grain ATMs, One Nation One Ration Card for portability. Draw a diagram of SARTHAK's flow from FCI/Center to FPS, showing where leakage/corruption is prevented at each stage.
  4. Body — challenges (still required even in a "positive" question): fiscal burden (₹2.28 lakh crore vs. SARTHAK's ₹25,530 crore outlay — frame this as: the outlay is a fraction of what could be recovered by plugging leakages) and technological/administrative capacity gaps.
  5. Conclusion/suggestions: capacity building, enhancing dealer margins, using PDS as a nutrition hub (diversifying the food basket, not just wheat/rice), and a gradual shift toward Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) as a complementary (not wholesale-replacement) technology-driven reform.
Instructor's implicit-demand tip: even in a technology-focused question, do not omit the fiscal-burden point — juxtaposing the ₹2.28 lakh crore subsidy bill against the ₹25,530 crore SARTHAK outlay makes an implicit cost-benefit argument that most students miss.

Supplementary research — filling gaps

NCLT/NCLAT hallucination case — full verified background

Decided 2 July 2026 by a Supreme Court bench of Justices P.S. Narasimha and Alok Aradhe. The underlying dispute involved insolvency proceedings initiated by Jammu & Kashmir Bank Ltd. against Essel Infraprojects Ltd. (NCLT admitted the application on 28 August 2024, recording a default of ₹87.43 crore). Senior Advocate Madhavi Divan argued that the tribunals below had relied on six judicial decisions that either did not exist or did not support the propositions attributed to them. The Supreme Court set aside the orders, directed a zero-tolerance approach to AI-hallucinated citations, and asked the Bar Council of India to constitute a committee to frame guiding principles — distinct from (and more recent than) an earlier, separate December 2024 incident involving an ITAT Bengaluru bench citing non-existent judgments, which the lecture briefly conflated in passing.

Draft Regulations for AI in Courts — Regulation numbering (resolved)

Checked directly against the Supreme Court AI Committee's original draft (notice dated 3 June 2026): Regulation 43 ("Transparency and disclosure"), sub-regulation (3), requires that where an AI tool is used by a party or legal representative in preparing a document, pleading, or evidence, its AI-assisted character must be disclosed via a certificate. The lecture's "Regulation 43" citation is therefore correct; scattered secondary commentary citing "Regulation 3" (actually the Definitions clause) was the source of the earlier flagged discrepancy.

SARTHAK-PDS — full verified scheme architecture

Approved by the Union Cabinet on 27 May 2026 with a central outlay of ₹25,530 crore over April 2026-March 2031, covering 81.35 crore NFSA beneficiaries. It merges the pre-existing "Assistance for intra-State foodgrain movement and FPS dealers' margin under NFSA" scheme with SMART-PDS. Three AI-enabled technology modules underpin it: NIRMAL (a real-time beneficiary registry), ASHA (a multilingual grievance-redressal platform), and SAKSHAM (a supply-chain platform including vehicle tracking — corresponding to the lecture's "Vehicle Location Tracking System"). The scheme uses AI, machine learning, blockchain, and other data-driven tools to strengthen NFSA implementation. None of these three module names were mentioned by name in the lecture, but they map directly onto the functions described in class (beneficiary digitization, grievance redress, supply-chain/vehicle monitoring).

ICRIER PDS leakage study — precise figures

The ICRIER working paper (analyzing FCI offtake data from August 2022-July 2023 against Household Consumption Expenditure Survey data) found ~28-29% of PDS-supplied grains do not reach intended beneficiaries, amounting to a loss of ₹69,108 crore (~20 million metric tonnes of rice and wheat). Arunachal Pradesh, Nagaland, and Gujarat were identified as the states with the highest PDS leakage. This closely matches the lecture's "28%" figure; the ₹69,108 crore rupee-value figure is a useful addition not mentioned in class.

FCI rice stock figure — could not be verified as stated

The lecture states FCI held 309 lakh tonnes of rice as of January 2026, described as nine times the buffer norm. Independent search found different figures: total foodgrain stocks of ~604 lakh tonnes (roughly 3x buffer requirement) in some reporting, and an April 2026 figure of 386.10 lakh tonnes of rice against a 135.80 lakh tonnes buffer norm (roughly 2.8x). The general point that FCI holds surplus stock well above mandated buffer norms is well-established and can be safely cited; the specific "309 lakh tonnes / nine times" figures should not be used in an exam answer without further verification against a primary FCI source.

Himanshu and Sen — corrected scholarly citation

The lecture's audio-garbled references to "Professor Pakrishna" (introducing the PDS-as-equalizer argument) and "Humanu and Sen" (documenting the negative relationship between PDS coverage and leakage) are best matched to economists Himanshu (Centre for Economic Studies and Planning, JNU) and Sen — their 2011 work argued for near-universal PDS coverage specifically because narrowing coverage (from universal to targeted, 1999-2000 to 2004-05) coincided with rising, not falling, diversion/leakage (from ~24% to ~54%), directly contradicting the Shanta Kumar Committee's later (2015) recommendation to narrow coverage further. The "Professor Pakrishna" reference (PDS as a tool to equalize primary food consumption) could not be independently matched to a specific named economist — treat this specific attribution as unverified.
