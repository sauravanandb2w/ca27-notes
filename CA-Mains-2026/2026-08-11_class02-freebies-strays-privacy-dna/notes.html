Executive summary

Second class of the CA Mains 2026 answer-writing series, covering four exam-relevant themes:

  • Topic 1 examines the "freebies vs. welfare measures" distinction — competitive populism, transactionalism, the vicious cycle of fiscal deficit, and the blurred line via unconditional cash transfers, with the 2013 Subramaniam Balaji case and Economic Survey recommendations.
  • Topic 2 covers the 2025 Supreme Court stray dog controversy — the August 11 shelter order and its August 22 reversal to the CNVR (Catch-Neuter-Vaccinate-Release) model, with terms like vacuum effect, feeder accountability, and anthropocentrism vs. ecocentrism.
  • Topic 3 covers the right to be forgotten — its distinction from the right to erasure, de-indexing vs. masking, and the Jorawar Singh Mundy precedent.
  • Topic 4 covers DNA testing in paternity disputes — the presumption of legitimacy under the Evidence Act, the shift from Section 112 to BSA Section 116, and the case-law evolution from Kamti Devi through Gautam Kundu to modern two-stage tests.

Throughout, the instructor models introduction, structuring, and conclusion technique, with two live timed answer-writing exercises.

1. Topic 1: Freebies vs. welfare measures

Recommended structure (as taught): introduction → definitions of freebies and welfare measures → legal framework → supporting arguments/concerns → data → key judgment → expert views/suggestions (including Economic Survey) → conclusion.

Model introduction

The proliferation of freebie culture, characterized by competitive populism, has emerged as a defining feature of India's political economy.
— Instructor's model introduction; can be followed by noting this transforms the constitutional obligation of welfare measures under the Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP) into "transactionalism"

2:51Central observation: the freebie culture transforms the constitutional obligation of welfare measures under the DPSP into transactionalism — vote-buying disguised as policy.

Key terms — competitive populism

Competitive populism (unnamed political scientist, transcribed "Abi")
Political leaders and parties prioritize short-term survival and measures to appease voters and secure electoral victories — eventually leading to a "crisis of governability."
Plebiscitary and personalized leadership
A related concept: leaders bypass political parties and institutions, appealing directly to voters ("vote for me") — voters respond to a leader's charisma rather than ideology, party, or manifesto.

7:34According to cited scholars, freebies focus on immediate gratification/electoral pressures, while welfare measures focus on long-term wellbeing and development.

2. Topic 1: Debates, data & recommended structure

Where the line blurs — unconditional cash transfers (UCTs)

10:31Many state schemes (women-focused unconditional cash transfers) sit ambiguously between "freebie" and "welfare measure":

  • Case for welfare measure: per economist Esther Duflo's research (referenced in the lecture as "Noble estate the flow her"/"the flow ass" — economics Nobel laureate Esther Duflo, verify spelling), when women control more income, they invest more in children's education and health — i.e., human capital formation. UCTs also increase consumption, creating a positive economic multiplier effect via cash circulation.
  • Case for freebie: unconditional and universal rather than targeted; scaling rapidly (see data below), raising fiscal sustainability concerns.

Key data points

Unconditional cash transfers to women alone: ~₹1.7 lakh crore (per a cited survey). Per the 16th Finance Commission's review: such transfers by states expanded from ₹73,000 crore (2018–19) to ₹4.14 lakh crore (2026) — described in the lecture as a "ballooning" of such transfers, contributing to sub-national fiscal stress.
The freebie culture is not a road to prosperity — it's a passport to fiscal disaster.
— Attributed in the lecture to a former Chairman of the Finance Commission (name transcribed as "Nasing" — likely N.K. Singh; verify before citing)

Further negative externalities

  • Democratic distortion: freebies challenge the principle of free and fair elections — the bedrock of democracy.
  • Environmental cost: e.g., free electricity is cited as a major driver of groundwater over-exploitation.
  • Rights-holder to beneficiary shift: transactionalism changes citizens from rights-holders (who can demand institutional accountability under the social contract) into dependent beneficiaries engaged in a transactional relationship with the state.
  • "Captive political-industrial complex": freebies involving goods (free laptops, TVs, mobile phones) create captive markets for politically-connected suppliers — a form of crony capitalism.

Key case and Economic Survey recommendations

S. Subramaniam Balaji vs. State of Tamil Nadu (2013): the Supreme Court held that election manifesto promises of freebies do not amount to a "corrupt practice" under Section 123 of the Representation of the People Act, 1951 — because that section applies to the conduct of individual candidates, not to party manifestos as policy statements. This is described in the lecture as a legal "loophole" not yet closed by legislative or further judicial clarification. (Independently verified — see supplementary research; the Court did direct the Election Commission to frame manifesto guidelines.)

17:01The Economic Survey has flagged that such schemes cause severe fiscal strain and crowd out productive investment, and recommends:

  • Schemes should not be open-ended — a sunset clause and periodic review mechanism should apply
  • Cites Mexico's Bolsa Família-style conditional cash transfer model (transfers stop once a family meets certain conditions) as international best practice, contrasted with India's typically open-ended, unconditional schemes

Model conclusion

To preserve India's economic stability and the purity of its elections, states must pivot away from dependency-inducing populist freebies and return to capability-building — i.e., genuine welfare.
— Instructor's model conclusion

Live answer-writing exercise (as taught)

The line between a legitimate constitutional welfare measure and an irrational electoral freebie is increasingly blurring in India. Critically analyze.
— Model question used for a timed (5-minute) structuring exercise in class

  2. Body — differentiate: freebies vs. welfare measures in a table format.
  3. 3. Topic 2: Stray dogs and public safety

Recommended structure: introduction → recent examples → constitutional/legal framework → data → debates/arguments for and against → challenges → judgment → views/quotes → suggestions/best practices → conclusion.

Model introduction

The intersection of urban governance, public health, and animal welfare has transformed the management of India's community animals into a constitutional and civic crisis.
— Instructor's model introduction; can be tweaked to frame a conflict between Article 21 and Article 51A(g) (fundamental duty to show compassion to living creatures), or between Article 21 and any other fundamental duty depending on the question

The 2025 Supreme Court "judicial flip"

30:25Sequence of events as taught, independently verified (see supplementary research):

  • The Supreme Court took cognizance of rising dog-bite fatalities and directed NCR local bodies to capture strays and permanently relocate them to shelters (August 2025).
  • This order triggered significant public pushback, and the Court subsequently restored the status quo — the CNVR (Catch, Neuter, Vaccinate, Release) approach, i.e., sterilize/vaccinate strays and release them back to their original territory rather than permanent confinement.

Key terms (for quotation-style answers)

Community animals
Term introduced under the Animal Birth Control (ABC) Rules: territorial animals belonging to a particular local area; the rules also permit "community feeding" of these animals.
Vacuum effect
Removing sterilized/vaccinated dogs from their territory creates an empty niche ("vacuum") that gets filled by unvaccinated or unsterilized animals migrating in — worsening, not improving, the public health problem this removal was meant to solve.
Feeder accountability
Individuals, organizations, or communities who feed community animals should assume some responsibility for their behavior — a tension point, since disputes often arise between feeders and other residents (not just between residents and dogs).
Anthropocentrism vs. ecocentrism
The ethical dilemma of whether human interest should be prioritized exclusively (anthropocentrism) or whether policy should pursue a shared, coexisting relationship between humans and other species (ecocentrism) — connects to the "One Health" approach below.
One Health approach
A framework recognizing the intersection of human, animal, and environmental health — used in the lecture to argue that this intersection is key to preventing/reducing rabies, rather than treating human and animal health as separate governance silos.
Facultatively social (Indian street dogs)
A behavioral trait of Indian street dogs: while scavengers by nature, they form social bonds with humans shaped by domestication rather than true "ferality" — a nuance used to push back against framing strays as purely wild/dangerous animals.

Key data points

India accounts for ~33–36% of global rabies deaths (per WHO, cited in lecture). Rabies case load (per the Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme, IDSP): rose from 1.5 million cases (2019) to 3.7 million cases (2024). National sterilization coverage remains at only ~30%.

Legal and constitutional framework

  • Article 21 — right to life (includes a healthy environment; prioritizes human life in this debate).
  • Article 51A(g) — fundamental duty to protect the environment and show compassion for living creatures.
  • Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Act, 1960 — central legislation prohibiting unnecessary pain/suffering to animals.
  • Animal Birth Control Rules, 2023 — mandates the CNVR approach.
  • Dog Breeding and Pet Shop Rules — mandates registration/regulation of breeders.

Governance challenges

  • Fragmented governance: human health (Health Ministry) and animal health (Animal Husbandry) are managed as separate departments; National Rabies Control Programme and the National Action Plan for Dog-Mediated Rabies Elimination are joint initiatives but face poor coordination.
  • Data mismatch: inconsistent dog-bite reporting across states/lifestyle surveys.
  • Medical supply deficit: shortage of anti-rabies immunoglobulin; patient non-compliance with the full 5-dose vaccination course.
  • "Judiciary legislates without implementation knowledge": a general critique (used illustratively in the lecture with an unrelated example of a court-ordered highway bar closure that shifted rather than solved the underlying problem) — courts issuing directions without full consideration of on-ground implementability.

Live answer-writing exercise (as taught)

The management of community animals highlights an ethical and constitutional dilemma between the human right to a safe environment and the fundamental duty to show compassion for living creatures. How can public policy reconcile these competing claims?
— Model question used for a timed (4-minute) structuring exercise in class
  1. Introduction: fact-based — India accounts for ~36% of global rabies deaths (WHO) — then link: "the increase in human-animal conflict has escalated the management of community animals into a deeply contested governance issue."
  2. Body — four dilemmas: (1) right to life vs. Article 51A(g); (2) anthropocentrism vs. ecocentrism; (3) doctrine of parens patriae — should the state act as guardian for both vulnerable humans and voiceless animals; (4) compassion vs. accountability — feeders show compassion but must also bear responsibility; also note judicial expansion (Nagaraja) as adding a further constitutional dimension.

  5. Conclusion: One Health approach; domestic best practices (Goa, Pune); separating waste-management and feeding zones; regulating the pet trade.

5. Topic 3: Right to be forgotten

Recommended structure: introduction → difference between "right to be forgotten" and "right to erasure" → constitutional/legal framework → key judgments → arguments for/against → views/suggestions/best practices → conclusion.

Model introduction

57:06General strong opening for any privacy-related question:

  • Cite the Supreme Court's landmark Puttaswamy judgment (referenced in the lecture simply as "the landmark case"), which transformed privacy from a mere common-law privilege into a constitutionally protected fundamental right under Article 21
  • Then note the digital age has forced continuous expansion of this right, of which the right to be forgotten is one example

Three essential elements of the right to privacy

  • Bodily/spatial privacy — personal autonomy over one's body (e.g., questions on DNA testing fall here).
  • Decisional privacy — autonomy of personal choice.
  • Informational privacy — control over personal information (right to be forgotten falls here).

58:48Like all rights, privacy is not absolute — any infringement must pass a three-prong test:

  • Legality — a valid law must authorize it
  • Necessity — a compelling state interest
  • Proportionality — the action must be necessary and the least intrusive means available

Key debate terms

Homo privaticus fallacy
By analogy to "homo economicus" in classical economics (the assumption that humans act rationally), the "homo privaticus" assumption is that an ideal digital user is fully informed and rational enough to manage their own privacy. This is a fallacy because there is no real informed consent online — most users click "I agree" without reading terms, amounting to a "voluntary battering" (bartering) of personal data rather than genuine consent.
Consent vs. coercion
Digital platforms often operate on a "take it or leave it" basis rather than genuine informed consent — described as coercion rather than consent, since users effectively cannot use the service at all without surrendering their data.
National security vs. privacy
The state has a legitimate interest in limiting privacy for national security, but this can lead to disproportionate data collection beyond what is required — violating the proportionality prong of the three-part test.
Article 19 vs. right to be forgotten
Direct conflict between the right to information (Article 19) / freedom of the press and an individual's claim to have information about them de-indexed or suppressed.

Right to be forgotten vs. right to erasure

DimensionRight to be forgottenRight to erasure
What happens to the dataRemains in the backend, but is de-indexed/masked so it doesn't appear in search resultsComplete deletion of the data itself, including from backend systems
Legal basis in IndiaEvolving subset of the right to privacy; recognized case-by-case by High Courts, not yet an explicit Supreme Court fundamental rightDebated during drafting of India's data protection law but ultimately not included
GDPR analogue—Article 17 of the EU GDPR ("right to erasure"), sometimes loosely called "right to be forgotten" — but is in fact closer to full deletion

1:06:42Two further technical distinctions:

  • De-indexing — disables name-based search functionality on a platform (e.g., search engines)
  • Masking — replaces a personal identifier with a neutral reference in official records (e.g., court records/registries); the underlying record still exists but the identifying detail is hidden

6. Topic 3: Case law, debates & live exercise

Key precedent

Jorawar Singh Mundy vs. Union of India (Delhi High Court, 2021): recognized the right to be forgotten as an integral facet of the right to privacy under Article 21, directing that search engines like Google must de-index or mask the identities of individuals who have been acquitted, discharged, or involved in purely private disputes. (Independently verified: the petitioner was a US citizen of Indian origin, acquitted of NDPS charges in 2011, who successfully sought removal of the judgment from Google and legal databases after it continued to harm his employment prospects abroad.) In the lecture this is referred to loosely as "the Delhi case" or transcribed imperfectly as "Laksh Vir Singh Yadav" — treat both as pointing to the same line of Delhi High Court jurisprudence and verify the exact case relied on before citing.

Arguments for and against

For recognizing the rightAgainst / concerns
Protects dignity and enables genuine rehabilitation — "the shadow of crime should not replace the shadow of dignity"May amount to arbitrary censorship, denying the public legitimate information
Prevents disproportionate, perpetual reputational harm from outdated search resultsParliament has already deliberately excluded this right from India's data protection law — recognizing it judicially risks judicial overreach into a legislative domain
Ensures "algorithmic neutrality" — since platforms like Google do not create content (unlike a newspaper), they cannot claim their own free-speech right is at stake in a de-indexing orderUndermines the principle of open justice — court records are public information, and altering search access to them affects historical/legal transparency
—Risk of selective erasure — public figures could misuse the right to hide genuine misconduct

Best practices and comparative examples

  • EU GDPR: formally recognizes the right to erasure/be forgotten.
  • Delhi High Court: has refused the right to be forgotten to public figures attempting to hide genuine misconduct — the right is not absolute or automatic.
  • France vs. Google (Court of Justice of the European Union): ruled that Google does not have to apply the right to be forgotten globally — de-indexing obligations apply only within the relevant jurisdiction (e.g., Europe), not worldwide.
Humans forget, but the internet does not forget — and does not let humans forget.
— Attributed in the lecture to a columnist referenced as "Sanjay" (surname unclear in transcript — verify before citing); recommended as a strong opening quote for this topic

Live answer-writing exercise (as taught)

"The shadow of crime should not be allowed to replace the shadow of dignity." In light of the statement, examine the judicial recognition of the right to be forgotten in India.
— Model question used for a timed (4-minute) structuring exercise in class
  1. Introduction: open with the "internet does not forget" quote, link it to the increasing demand for judicial recognition of a right to be forgotten, then briefly define the right.
  2. Body — the "shadow" framing: shadow of crime = a permanent digital record of someone's past misconduct; shadow of dignity = a person's right to a dignified life not permanently defined by their worst moment.
  3. Body — advantages: gradual judicial expansion of the right; addresses disproportionate harm; ensures algorithmic neutrality and rehabilitation.
  4. Body — the decision framework: use the decision-tree diagram above as a margin diagram — heinous offense? → selective erasure risk? → purely private matter? — to show nuanced, case-by-case judicial reasoning rather than an absolute right.
  5. Conclusion: suggestions — legislative clarity (revisit the deliberate exclusion from the data protection law), follow EU GDPR-style models, and ensure media/platforms prioritize accuracy in records.

7. Topic 4: DNA testing and paternity disputes

Recommended structure: introduction → constitutional/legal framework → core debate → evolution of case law in India → arguments for/against → global comparisons → conclusion.

Model introduction

The intersection of constitutional jurisprudence and modern scientific advancement is evident in the conflict between a child's right to biological truth and an individual's right to privacy.
— Instructor's model introduction, given in the context of the Supreme Court recently upholding a High Court order directing a DNA test to prove paternity (case referenced as "CP vs. AP," 2026 — verify exact citation before use)
The instructor explicitly warns against inventing false precision with data — don't fabricate a year or exact figure you don't actually remember; it's better to write "there has been a marked increase in X" than a specific number you're unsure of, since evaluators can tell when statistics are being manipulated for effect.

Constitutional and statutory framework

  • Article 21 — bodily autonomy and the right to privacy (compelling someone to undergo a DNA test implicates this right).
  • Presumption of legitimacy: under Section 112 of the (now-replaced) Indian Evidence Act, 1872 — carried forward with the same substantive language as Section 116 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (BSA). Any child born during a valid marriage is presumed legitimate, unless the party disputing paternity can prove "non-access" (no physical access to the spouse) during the relevant period.

Core debates

  • Scientific truth vs. legal presumption: should modern, conclusive DNA evidence automatically override an 1872-era legal presumption designed before such technology existed?
  • Routine use vs. exceptional use: should DNA testing be ordered whenever requested in a dispute, or only in exceptional circumstances (to avoid its use as a harassment or pressure tactic, e.g., in maintenance disputes)?
  • Child's right vs. father's privacy: a child's right to know their biological origin/identity vs. an individual's protection against compelled medical/bodily intrusion.

1. What is the ONE core distinction between a "freebie" and a "welfare measure"?

Freebies = short-term gratification, often untargeted, financed to win elections. Welfare measures = long-term, constitutionally obligated (DPSP), targeted at vulnerable groups, enhance human capabilities.

2. What did the Supreme Court hold in S. Subramaniam Balaji vs. State of Tamil Nadu (2013)?

Election manifesto promises of freebies do not amount to a "corrupt practice" under Section 123 of the RPA, 1951, since that section governs individual candidate conduct, not party manifestos. The Court directed the EC to frame manifesto guidelines.

3. What is the "vacuum effect" in the stray dog debate, and why does mass removal backfire?

Removing dogs from a territory leaves it empty; unvaccinated/unsterilized animals migrate in to fill the gap, worsening the public health risk rather than solving it — this is why CNVR (sterilize and release) is preferred over confinement.

4. What is the difference between "right to be forgotten" and "right to erasure"?

Right to be forgotten: data stays in the backend but is de-indexed/masked from search. Right to erasure: complete deletion of the data itself (EU GDPR Article 17 concept).

5. What is the "presumption of legitimacy" in Indian paternity law, and what must be proven to rebut it?

A child born during a valid marriage is presumed legitimate under Section 116 BSA (formerly S.112 Evidence Act) unless the party disputing paternity proves "non-access" — no physical access to the spouse — during the relevant period.

Supplementary research — filling gaps

S. Subramaniam Balaji vs. State of Tamil Nadu (2013) — verified holding

The Supreme Court held that election manifesto promises cannot be construed as a "corrupt practice" under Section 123 of the Representation of the People Act, 1951, because that provision governs individual candidate conduct, not party manifestos as policy documents. However, the Court explicitly acknowledged that "distribution of freebies of any kind, undoubtedly, influences all people. It shakes the root of free and fair elections to a large degree" — and directed the Election Commission to frame guidelines regulating manifesto content, a genuine institutional response not fully covered in the lecture's framing of this as an unaddressed "loophole."

The 2025 Supreme Court stray dog controversy — verified timeline

The crisis was triggered by the death of a young child, Chavi Sharma, from rabies on 25 July 2025. On 11 August 2025, a two-judge bench directed Delhi/NCR authorities to capture all stray dogs and permanently relocate them to shelters within eight weeks. Following significant public backlash, a three-judge bench (Justices Vikram Nath, Sandeep Mehta, and N.V. Anjaria) stayed this order on 22 August 2025, calling for a "holistic approach" balancing human safety and animal welfare, and reinstated the CNVR model — with rabid or dangerously aggressive dogs still subject to quarantine/shelter, while sterilized, vaccinated dogs are returned to their original territory. The Court's scope was expanded pan-India, directing municipal bodies nationwide to ensure only sterilized, immunized dogs are released back to their areas.

Jorawar Singh Mundy vs. Union of India — verified case background

The petitioner, a US citizen of Indian origin, was tried under the NDPS Act during a 2009 visit to India and acquitted on 30 April 2011. After returning to the US and studying law, he discovered his acquittal judgment remained fully searchable online (Google, Indian Kanoon, vLex), causing ongoing reputational harm with potential employers. In April 2021, Delhi High Court Justice Pratibha M. Singh passed an interim order recognizing the right to be forgotten as part of the fundamental right to privacy, and granted limited de-indexing relief — while emphasizing courts must weigh this against the public's right to information and judicial transparency on a case-by-case basis.

DNA/paternity case law — verified corrections

Independent search confirms the key precedent likely intended by the lecture's 1993 reference is Gautam Kundu vs. State of West Bengal, and the "2001/2005" reference likely corresponds to Kamti Devi vs. Poshi Ram (2001) — which held that even genuine DNA evidence contrary to Section 112's presumption cannot automatically override it; the presumption can only be rebutted by "strong, clear, satisfying and conclusive evidence," not mere DNA results alone, since Section 112 was enacted before DNA/RNA technology existed. Note this is a more legally conservative position than the lecture's account of a clean "two-stage test" giving DNA evidence automatic preference — in practice, Indian courts have continued to treat the Section 112/116 presumption as difficult to displace even post-DNA-testing, and a November 2025 Supreme Court ruling reaffirmed that DNA tests should not be ordered unless the Section 112-type presumption is first rebutted and paternity is directly linked to the matter in dispute.
