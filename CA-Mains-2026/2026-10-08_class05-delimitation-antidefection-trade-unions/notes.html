1. Topic 1: Delimitation — the core debate

2:26Model framing line (reusable introduction pattern): delimitation — the geographical carving of electoral constituencies — "is not merely an administrative exercise, but a deeply political one," forming the bedrock of parliamentary democracy by seeking to ensure the constitutional promise of "one person, one vote, one value."

Reusable introduction template: name the mechanism, state it is "not merely administrative/technical but deeply political," then tie it to the specific constitutional value it promises (here, "one person, one vote, one value"). This pattern generalises to any institutional-design question.

Context pegs (current affairs hooks)

  • 3:13A 131st Constitution Amendment Bill (alongside a companion delimitation bill) was introduced to expand Lok Sabha seats from the present 543 to around 850 — but the push failed because a constitutional amendment of this kind needs a two-thirds majority, which the bill could not secure.
  • 3:47Implementation of the 106th Constitution Amendment Act (the women's reservation law, "Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam") is contingent on delimitation being carried out first.
  • 4:01The Economic Advisory Council to the PM (EAC-PM) released a working paper proposing a targeted splitting of constituencies rather than a uniform, population-only split.

What delimitation legally involves

4:19Delimitation is the act of drawing political boundaries and involves three distinct steps: (1) fixing the total number of seats in the Lok Sabha; (2) calculating how many seats each state gets; and (3) drawing the boundaries of each constituency.

If a question simply asks "what is delimitation," a one-line definition plus this three-part breakdown (total seats → seats per state → constituency boundaries), boxed for visual clarity, is the recommended format — don't over-elaborate a pure definitional ask.

Constitutional and legal architecture

  • Article 81 caps Lok Sabha strength (around 550 elected members under the current formula) and requires that, as far as practicable, the ratio between seats and state population be uniform across states.
  • Article 82 requires readjustment of constituencies after every census.
  • 42nd Amendment Act (1976) froze the total number of seats and their inter-state allocation at 1971-census levels — a freeze justified at the time as protection for states that had pursued population control.
  • 84th Amendment Act (2001) extended this freeze to 2026, while permitting redrawing of constituency boundaries within a state (not inter-state reallocation) on the basis of the 2001 census.
  • 87th Amendment Act (2003) gave effect to the actual redrawing of boundaries using 2001 census data, without changing the number of seats or their state-wise distribution.
  • Article 329(a) bars courts from questioning the validity of any law relating to delimitation of constituencies.
  • Article 330A / 332A (inserted by the 106th Amendment Act, 2023) reserve seats for women in the Lok Sabha and state assemblies respectively — but this reservation is explicitly linked to the first delimitation exercise carried out after the next census following that Act's commencement.
  • Delimitation Commission Act, 2002 establishes an independent Delimitation Commission to carry out the boundary-drawing exercise; its orders have the force of law.

Why the freeze matters now

7:34Because the next delimitation will not be based on the outdated 1971 population distribution, there is pressure to increase total seats to correct under-representation — but this directly triggers a north–south political fault line, since the southern states that pursued population control stand to see their relative seat share shrink if seats are redistributed purely on current population.

The four key debates

Debate 1 — Democratic parity vs. "demographic penalty"
Representation is a core democratic value, and India's largest constituencies now have populations exceeding many small countries — one MP in some constituencies represents roughly 25–30 lakh voters, against an original design benchmark of about 7.5 lakh people per MP. This fuels demand to expand seats. But states that implemented population-control measures (mainly southern states) argue that increasing seats on a current-population basis would penalise them for complying with national policy — a "demographic penalty" for past good performance.
Debate 2 — Uniform (population-only) split vs. targeted/multi-factor splitting
One option is splitting constituencies purely by population. The EAC-PM's working paper instead proposes weighting multiple factors (economic performance/GDP contribution, linguistic and community profile, voter-turnout patterns) rather than population alone — intended to avoid a pure "demographic penalty" outcome and preserve federal trust.
Debate 3 — Under-representation vs. malapportionment
These are treated in the lecture as two distinct problems, not synonyms: under-representation is a scale problem affecting virtually all constituencies — each MP now represents far more people than originally intended, degrading an MP's ability to know, serve, and be held accountable by constituents. Malapportionment is a distributional-equality problem affecting mainly the most populous states — a state's share of Lok Sabha seats does not match its share of national population (e.g., an MP from a high-population northern state represents several times more people than an MP from a smaller southern state).
Debate 4 — Representation (Lok Sabha) vs. federal balance (Rajya Sabha)
Expanding the Lok Sabha alone, without a corresponding change to the Rajya Sabha (where seats are not population-weighted in the same way), risks a power imbalance between the two houses in joint sittings, where Lok Sabha's numerical strength would dominate even more heavily than today. The US Senate (equal representation per state, regardless of population) is cited as a contrasting model where the upper house retains real federal weight — a balance India's bicameral design does not currently replicate.

The "knowing, serving, answering" framework

A reusable three-part lens (from the under-representation debate) for why representative scale matters: (1) Knowing — an MP with an excessively large constituency cannot stay familiar with local social, economic, and welfare conditions; (2) Serving — legislative duties (in Parliament) and facilitative duties (meeting constituents, aiding access to government services) both suffer at scale; (3) Answering — accountability breaks down because constituents cannot meaningfully evaluate an MP's performance or even get face time with them.

Knowing–Serving–Answering framework: use this triad whenever a question links representative scale (delimitation, local-body ward size, etc.) to accountability failure.

2. Topic 1: Data, judgments, quotes & global models

Key data points (cite exact figures in the answer)

Lok Sabha strength fixed at 543 based on the 1971 census (population then: ~54 crore; current population: ~144 crore). The (failed) expansion plan proposed ~850 seats, of which roughly a third (~270+) would have been reserved for women under the women's reservation law. The EAC-PM working paper instead proposed expansion to around 824 seats, with about 170 of the existing constituencies split — some two-way, some three-way — rather than splitting every constituency uniformly.
Per the EAC-PM working paper (as cited in the lecture — verify exact figures against the published paper before citing in Mains): the combined Lok Sabha seat share of Telangana and Tamil Nadu is projected to stay roughly stable (~23.7% to ~23.6%) under the proposed multi-factor model, against a sharper fall (to roughly ~20%) if seats were instead reallocated purely on 2011-census population. The paper also projects a turnout increase of up to ~2.3% from the redesign.

Comparative/global models

United States — the Connecticut ("Great") Compromise
At the 1787 Constitutional Convention, large states (the Virginia Plan) wanted representation purely by population; small states (the New Jersey Plan) wanted equal representation per state. The Connecticut Compromise resolved this by giving the House of Representatives population-based seats while the Senate gives every state exactly two seats regardless of population — preserving small-state federal power in the upper chamber. Notably, the US has never increased the total number of House seats (fixed since 1929) despite large population growth, relying instead on other mechanisms (and, per the lecture, technology) to manage representative workload.
European Union — degressive proportionality
The European Parliament allocates seats using "degressive proportionality": smaller member states are deliberately given slightly more seats than strict population-proportionality would allow, so that larger states cannot simply dominate the chamber by numbers — a deliberate federal-balance correction mechanism, offered in the lecture as a possible reference model for India's own seat-allocation debate.

3. Topic 1: Model answer structure (as taught)

Distinguish between under-representation and malapportionment in India's electoral system. Evaluate the viability of expanding the Lok Sabha to 850 seats to address these issues.
— Model question used for the live structuring exercise in class
  1. Introduction: lead with the data (543 seats fixed since the 1971 census against a near-tripled population), then state the "one person, one vote, one value" constitutional promise as the value under strain.
  2. Body — define and distinguish: under-representation (a scale problem affecting nearly all constituencies — the "knowing, serving, answering" breakdown) vs. malapportionment (an equality problem concentrated in the most populous states — unequal value of votes across states).
  3. Body — why this has resurfaced now: the 1971-census freeze (42nd/84th/87th Amendments), ending in 2026, and the 131st Constitution Amendment Bill's failed attempt to expand to ~850 seats.
  4. Body — evaluate viability: positives (corrects demographic imbalance, enables fuller women's reservation roll-out, improves governance accountability, EAC-PM's projected turnout gains) weighed against concerns (southern "demographic penalty" fear, federal/Rajya Sabha imbalance, two-thirds majority and political consensus requirements, implementation timelines, gerrymandering risk).
  5. Conclusion: a workable path likely requires a multi-factor (not purely population-based) model — along EAC-PM or EU degressive-proportionality lines — built through political consensus rather than a unilateral population-only formula.
Reusable four-part skeleton for any "distinguish X and Y, then evaluate a proposed reform" question: define-and-distinguish → why-now (current-affairs trigger) → evaluate (positives vs. concerns) → conclusion favouring a calibrated/hybrid approach over an extreme position.

4. Topic 2: Anti-Defection Law — mechanics & the merger loophole

Recommended answer format for this topic (repeated instructor guidance): introduction/context → legal framework → the specific mechanism/loophole being tested → debates → judgments/quotes → comparative models → suggestions → conclusion.

Context pegs (current affairs hooks)

37:47The Tenth Schedule ("Anti-Defection Law," ADL) is framed as "one of the most critical constitutional interventions aimed at addressing the systemic malady of political opportunism." Recent triggers cited: reports of a large group of MPs merging into a newly formed party ("National Citizens Party," as referenced in the lecture), a separate group of opposition MLAs voting against their party whip on a confidence motion, and legislators from one party merging into another using the two-thirds merger exemption — all raising fresh "two-thirds merger" controversies.

Legal framework and evolution

  • 52nd Constitution Amendment Act (1985) inserted the Tenth Schedule, disqualifying legislators on grounds of defection — intended to ensure representative accountability and government stability, and to curb the large-scale floor-crossing epidemic of the late 1960s/early 1970s.
  • 91st Constitution Amendment Act (2003) omitted paragraph 3 of the Tenth Schedule, which had earlier protected a one-third "split" of a legislature party from disqualification — removing that loophole entirely — while retaining paragraph 4, which protects a two-thirds merger of a legislature party from disqualification.
  • Article 190(3)(b) requires that a Speaker cannot arbitrarily accept a legislator's resignation — the Speaker must independently satisfy themselves that the resignation is voluntary and genuine, not a device to pre-empt a disqualification proceeding.
Paragraph 3 vs. Paragraph 4: paragraph 3 (one-third split protection) was deleted by the 91st Amendment — a one-third split no longer protects defectors. Paragraph 4 (two-thirds merger protection) survives — if two-thirds of a party's legislators merge into another party, they are not disqualified.

The live "merger loophole" — conjunctive vs. disjunctive reading

Paragraph 4's protection, properly read, requires a two-step process: (1) the original political party (the organisational wing, outside the legislature) formally decides to merge with another party; and (2) at least two-thirds of that party's legislature party (the elected members, inside the House) accept the merger. A political-party-level merger happening outside the House does not, by itself, protect legislators inside the House — nor does an inside-the-House "merger" by MLAs/MPs automatically count unless the outside organisational party has also merged.

  • Conjunctive reading (both steps required together): the organisational party must formally merge, and two-thirds of the legislature party must accept that merger — this is the textually correct reading, reflected in the Supreme Court's approach in the Subhash Desai case (2023, Maharashtra political crisis) and referred for further examination to a larger bench.
  • Disjunctive reading (the loophole): if two-thirds of a party's legislators simply vote together to join another party — without any actual merger of the organisational party outside the House — this is treated as a "deemed merger," shielding them from disqualification even though no real inter-party merger occurred. This is the interpretation several Speakers have in practice applied when deciding defection petitions.
Trap: paragraph 4 is not "any two-thirds split is protected" — on a correct (conjunctive) reading, the protection requires an actual merger of the original political party, not merely two-thirds of legislators acting together. The gap between this textual requirement and how Speakers have sometimes applied it in practice is the live reform debate.

5. Topic 2: Debates, data, judgments & quotes

Core debates

Stability vs. accountability / dissent
The ADL's stated aim is government stability, but critics argue it achieves this by suppressing legitimate dissent — an MP or MLA cannot vote against the party whip on policy grounds without risking disqualification, even where independent judgment might better serve constituents.
Speaker as adjudicator vs. independent tribunal
The Speaker — typically a member of the ruling party or coalition — is the decision-making authority on disqualification petitions, raising a structural conflict-of-interest concern; some reformers argue defection disputes should instead go to an independent tribunal or the Election Commission.
Anti-defection vs. anti-opportunism in practice
The law's design assumes defections are principled splits; in practice, many episodes are framed as being driven by political opportunism (inducements, central/state government formation battles) rather than genuine ideological disagreement.
Party whip vs. individual free will
Once a party issues a whip, a legislator has effectively no free will on that vote — raising a deeper tension between party discipline and individual representative judgment.

Data

Between 1967 and 1972, India saw roughly 2,000 cases of defection among around 4,000 total state legislators nationwide — more than half of all legislators switched sides at least once in this period (cited in the lecture as drawn from work by a scholar referenced as "Vinesh Kumar"; verify exact name before citing). Per PRS Legislative Research data (as cited in the lecture), roughly 26 disqualification cases arose in Parliament between 1985 and 2009 — frequency sharply declining after the law's initial years, a pattern the lecture uses to argue the law "worked" initially but has been increasingly circumvented since.

Reform recommendations

Comparative practice

The lecture notes that the US, UK, Canada, and Australia impose no formal anti-defection restrictions on their legislators, while several Commonwealth and developing-world legislatures (Bangladesh, Kenya, South Africa are cited) maintain strict anti-defection regimes broadly similar to India's — suggesting India's approach sits closer to the stricter end of a global spectrum rather than being an outlier in isolation.

6. Topic 2: Model answer structure (as taught)

"Elected representatives are increasingly treating their mandate as a transferable asset." In light of this statement, examine the efficacy of the Tenth Schedule in preventing defection.
— Model question used for the live structuring exercise in class
  1. Introduction: the 52nd Amendment's (1985) original purpose — curbing the pre-1985 defection epidemic (~2,000 cases of defection among ~4,000 legislators, 1967–72) — framed against today's "transferable asset" critique.
  2. Body — explain the statement: what treating a mandate as a "transferable asset" means (legislators switching allegiance post-election, outside voter control) and its effect — a representation deficit and weakened democratic accountability.
  3. Body — evaluate efficacy: initial success (defection frequency fell sharply per PRS data) vs. current erosion — the paragraph 3 vs. paragraph 4 history, the "resignation and re-contest" workaround, the merger-loophole's conjunctive/disjunctive reading problem, and the Speaker's structural conflict of interest as adjudicator.
  4. Body — judicial correctives: Kihoto Hollohan (Speaker's quasi-judicial role is reviewable), the Speaker-timeline case (decide "within a reasonable period"), and Subhash Desai (scrutiny of resignations and the merger clause).
  5. Conclusion: the law was a meaningful "step in the right direction" but requires reform (Law Commission 170th Report / NCRWC-style suggestions — narrower disqualification triggers, an independent adjudicator, post-defection office bars) rather than either status-quo complacency or outright repeal.
Reusable skeleton for any "X law/institution: evaluate its efficacy" question: original purpose → initial success (data) → current erosion (mechanism-level loopholes) → judicial correctives → balanced reform-oriented conclusion.

7. Topic 3: Trade unions, labour codes & the platform economy

Recommended format (repeated instructor guidance): introduction → context/news → key terms → constitutional/legal framework → data → old-vs-new regime comparison → debates → suggestions → conclusion.

Model introduction

Trade unions do not merely play the role of protecting workers' rights — they serve as institutional safety valves that channel worker grievances through democratic dialogue, preventing systemic explosions.
— Instructor's model introduction for this topic, paraphrased

Context pegs (current affairs hooks)

  • The Union government's operationalisation of the four new Labour Codes triggered opposition protest, with major trade unions observing a "black day."
  • A nationwide strike by gig/platform workers was held against prevailing platform economic models, alongside large-scale worker protests in several industrial belts.
  • Instructor's exam-strategy note: this year's GS paper is likely to carry a question on "formal and informal associations" — such a question could be answered either through the classical trade-union lens (this section) or through the gig-worker/informal-association lens, depending on the exact wording.

Key terms

Trade union: a collective association formed by workers to protect their rights, ensure a fair living wage (not merely a minimum wage), and improve workplace conditions through collective bargaining — acting as a mechanism to balance the inherent power inequality between capital and labour.
Platform economy: an arrangement where tasks or services are mediated through a digital platform connecting service providers to consumers, typically via short-term or freelance contracts, and governed not by a human manager but by an algorithmic ("organic"/automated) management system — removing the traditional three-party structure of employer–employee–union (and the state) in favour of a platform–worker relationship with no human "in the loop."

Constitutional and legal framework

  • Article 19(1)(c): fundamental right to form associations or unions.
  • Article 43 (DPSP): directs the State to secure a living wage for workers.
  • Articles 21 and 23: right to life with dignity, and prohibition of forced labour, respectively.
  • Labour sits in the Concurrent List, meaning both Parliament and state legislatures could earlier legislate on it (producing significant inter-state variation) — now substantially unified/codified under the four Labour Codes: the Code on Wages, the Industrial Relations Code, the Code on Social Security, and the Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code.
  • Comparative note: Canada is cited as having passed a dedicated platform/gig-workers protection act.

Key data (cite exact figures in the answer)

India's overall unionisation rate (public and private combined) is only about 6.3%. The informal workforce exceeds 80% of total employment. Per Centre for Policy Research (CPR)-type data cited in the lecture, the contract-worker share of the formal workforce rose from roughly 22% (2001) to roughly 40% (2021/22) — nearly doubling over two decades that coincided with more liberal economic/labour-market policies.
Per NITI Aayog, India's gig workforce is estimated at around 12 million, projected to reach around 23 million. Average gig earnings are cited at roughly ₹75/hour. The e-Shram portal has over 30 crore registered unorganised-sector workers. Per a cited NSSO-type survey (2023), roughly 70% of food-delivery gig workers work near their hometowns and 32% were unemployed before joining a platform. Per the Fairwork India Report (2024), none of the 11 major platforms surveyed formally recognised an elected worker body (i.e., no platform recognised a functioning trade union). Per the State of Working India report, graduate unemployment is nearing 40%, against only about 1.7 million new salaried jobs created annually against roughly 5 million new graduates each year — risking a shift from "demographic dividend" to "demographic disappointment."

8. Topic 3: Debates, examples & the way forward

Core debates (framed as X vs. Y pairs)

Ease of doing business vs. ease of laboring
Rationalisation/simplification of labour law, marketed as improving the ease of doing business, is argued by critics to simultaneously favour capital at the expense of labour rights and protections ("ease of laboring").
Union as "safety valve" vs. union as "obstacle"/"labour aristocracy"
Trade unions argue they channel grievances into democratic negotiation rather than unrest; capital/entrepreneurial critics frame unions as anti-growth barriers, or as a "labour aristocracy" protecting only a small, already-privileged segment of formal-sector workers.
Algorithmic management/surveillance vs. collective bargaining
Platform work increasingly substitutes algorithmic management and digital surveillance for human-negotiated, union-mediated terms of work.
"Employment" vs. "partnership"/"entrepreneurship"
Gig workers typically seek recognition as employees (with attendant protections); platforms instead classify them as independent contractors or "partners" to avoid employer obligations.
Flexibility vs. forced participation
Gig work is marketed as offering flexible, voluntary work arrangements, but workers often have no genuinely comparable alternative employment, making the "flexibility" effectively compelled.
Convenience (for consumers) vs. concerns (for workers)
Fast, convenient delivery/ride services for consumers are built on workers bearing significant personal physical risk (e.g., time-pressured road travel).
Regulation vs. deregulation
Industry voices argue platforms create large volumes of jobs and should face lighter regulation; unions counter that regulatory silence has enabled worker exploitation to continue unchecked.
Upward mobility vs. stagnation
Platforms claim to offer upward mobility; most gig workers instead face stagnation due to a lack of skilling opportunities — though a smaller cohort of high-skill freelance/"gig" professionals (cited example: Berlin) genuinely experience high earnings and mobility, making this a bifurcated rather than uniform outcome.

Structural problems

  • Dual-class system of contractualisation: permanent and contract employees performing identical tasks are paid differently and receive unequal social-security benefits — producing what some scholars term a "disposable, non-unionisable majority" of workers.
  • Wage/"supply surplus" concealment (algorithmic wage discrimination): platforms use behavioural data (e.g., a driver's known willingness to accept certain fares) to pay the minimum amount a given worker is likely to still accept, rather than a transparent, uniform rate.
  • "AI-washing": companies attribute layoffs or adverse restructuring to "AI" or automation, when the underlying driver may be ordinary cost-cutting or work-exploitation — tracking a broader "safety valve" concern outlined above.
  • Institutional neglect: the tripartite Indian Labour Conference — the main forum for negotiating labour standards — reportedly has not been convened for roughly the last 12 years; scheme workers (e.g., Anganwadi and ASHA workers) remain classified as "honorary" rather than as workers with full labour-law protection.
  • Psychosocial and physical vulnerability: gig workers face both economic insecurity and physical risk, without the relational/organisational support structures traditional workplaces (however imperfectly) provide.

Suggestions / way forward

  • Ratification and domestic alignment with relevant ILO conventions/standards on platform and gig work.
  • "Human-in-the-loop" requirements for consequential algorithmic management decisions (pay-setting, deactivation/termination).
  • A universal minimum/living wage floor extending to gig and contract workers, not only formal-sector employees.
  • Regular convening of tripartite bodies like the Indian Labour Conference, and extension of full worker status (rather than "honorary" status) to scheme workers such as Anganwadi/ASHA workers.

Q1
The freeze on the total number of Lok Sabha seats and their inter-state allocation, originally based on the 1971 census, was extended to 2026 by which constitutional amendment?
(A) 42nd Amendment Act, 1976
(B) 84th Amendment Act, 2001
(C) 87th Amendment Act, 2003
(D) 91st Amendment Act, 2003
Answer: (B). The 42nd Amendment (1976) first froze seats at 1971-census levels; the 84th Amendment (2001) extended that freeze to 2026; the 87th Amendment (2003) allowed redrawing of boundaries using 2001 census data without changing seat numbers.
Q2
Which article of the Constitution bars courts from questioning the validity of any law relating to the delimitation of constituencies?
(A) Article 81
(B) Article 82
(C) Article 329(a)
(D) Article 170
Answer: (C). Article 329(a) bars judicial questioning of delimitation laws. Article 81 caps Lok Sabha strength; Article 82 mandates readjustment after each census; Article 170 is the analogous provision for state assemblies.
Q3
In the Meghraj Kothari v. Delimitation Commission case, the Supreme Court held that:
(A) Delimitation orders can be challenged in High Courts but not in the Supreme Court
(B) Delimitation orders have the force of law and cannot be questioned in any court
(C) The Delimitation Commission must be reconstituted after every general election
(D) Women's reservation must apply from the first delimitation after 1971
Answer: (B). The 1967 ruling held delimitation orders carry the force of law and are immune from challenge, consistent with the Article 329(a) bar.
Q4
Which paragraph of the Tenth Schedule — removed by the 91st Constitution Amendment Act, 2003 — had earlier protected a one-third "split" of a legislature party from disqualification?
(A) Paragraph 2
(B) Paragraph 3
(C) Paragraph 4
(D) Paragraph 5
Answer: (B). Paragraph 3 (one-third split protection) was omitted by the 91st Amendment, 2003. Paragraph 4 (two-thirds merger protection) was retained.
Q5
Under a correct (conjunctive) reading of paragraph 4 of the Tenth Schedule, protection from disqualification on a "merger" requires:
(A) Only that two-thirds of the legislature party agree to act together
(B) Only that the original political party announces a merger, regardless of legislators' consent
(C) Both a merger of the original political party and acceptance of that merger by two-thirds of the legislature party
(D) Approval of the merger by the Election Commission alone
Answer: (C). The conjunctive reading (applied in the Subhash Desai case) requires both the organisational party's merger and two-thirds legislature-party acceptance; a "deemed merger" based only on legislators acting together (without an actual party merger) reflects the looser, disjunctive reading that critics call a loophole.

The Tenth Schedule to the Constitution was inserted by which amendment?
(A) 44th Amendment Act, 1978
(B) 52nd Amendment Act, 1985
(C) 61st Amendment Act, 1989
(D) 91st Amendment Act, 2003
Answer: (B). The 52nd Amendment Act, 1985 inserted the Tenth Schedule (the Anti-Defection Law).
Q7
Under India's new Industrial Relations Code, a "negotiating union" that wishes to negotiate with an employer on its own (without a negotiating council) must have the support of what minimum share of workers?
(A) 33%
(B) 40%

(D) 66%
Answer: (C). A union needs 51% membership among workers to be recognised as the sole negotiating union; otherwise, a multi-union negotiating council represents workers.

Answer: (B). The earlier Industrial Disputes Act threshold was 100 workers; the new Industrial Relations Code raises it to 300, widening the set of employers who can retrench without prior government permission.

Supplementary research — filling gaps

Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992) — verified citation

This is the landmark Supreme Court Constitution Bench ruling upholding the constitutional validity of the Tenth Schedule, while reading down the finality clause (paragraph 6) to hold that a Speaker's decision on disqualification is a quasi-judicial tribunal function, subject to judicial review on limited grounds (mala fides, perversity, violation of constitutional mandates, or breach of natural justice) rather than being fully immune from court scrutiny. The lecture's reference to a "1994 case" with this holding is very likely this 1992 judgment transcribed inaccurately — verify before citing in an exam answer.

The 91st Amendment Act, 2003 — precise changes

Independent verification confirms the 91st Amendment inserted Article 361B (disqualified members barred from holding remunerative political office until re-election or the end of the original term) and omitted paragraph 3 of the Tenth Schedule (the one-third split exemption), while leaving paragraph 4 (two-thirds merger exemption) intact. It also capped the size of Councils of Ministers at 15% of a legislature's total membership, partly to reduce the incentive to defect in exchange for a ministerial berth.

Subhash Desai v. State of Maharashtra (2023) — context

Arising from the 2022 Shiv Sena/Maharashtra political split, the Supreme Court held the Governor's decision to call for a floor test was not justified on the facts, and that the Speaker had erred in recognising a faction's whip without adequately verifying the "true" political party — while leaving the deeper question of the correct interpretation of the Tenth Schedule's merger provisions (paragraph 4) for examination by a larger bench, a reference that remains significant for the conjunctive-vs-disjunctive debate discussed in this class.

EAC-PM working paper on delimitation — verify exact figures before citing

The Economic Advisory Council to the PM has published working-paper analysis proposing delimitation models that weight multiple factors beyond raw population to protect southern/smaller states' relative political weight. The specific seat totals, split counts, and percentage-share projections cited in the lecture (around 824 seats, ~170 constituencies split, ~23.6–23.7% combined Telangana–Tamil Nadu share) should be cross-checked against the EAC-PM's actual published numbers before being used verbatim in a Mains answer, since coaching-class transcription of such figures can drift from the primary source.

The four Labour Codes — names and implementation status

The four codes are: the Code on Wages, 2019; the Industrial Relations Code, 2020; the Code on Social Security, 2020; and the Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020. They consolidate 29 earlier central labour laws. Implementation was staggered across states and the centre over several years after passage; confirm the precise effective-date status closer to the exam, since the "operationalisation" current-affairs peg referenced in this lecture is a live, evolving fact.
