0. Answer review: trade unions as a safety valve (from Class 5)

Trade unions are not just mechanisms for wage bargaining — they act as a critical safety valve in a democracy. In light of this, analyse the recent wave of unorganised-worker protests.
— Class 5 practice question, reviewed at the start of this class
  1. Open with data: India's labour force exceeds 60 crore but only ~6% is unionised; 80%+ work informally without rights protection.
  2. Explain the safety valve with a two-path diagram: grievance → institutional channel (union, collective bargaining) → dialogue; vs. grievance → no channel → protests turn violent → democratic rupture. The second path is the unorganised sector's reality.
  3. Issues from the Class 5 notes (labour codes, algorithmic management, contractualisation).
  4. Best practices — domestic first (worker cooperatives such as the Kerala example), then global (Germany's IG Metall, Denmark's 3F).
  5. Suggestions — revive tripartite consultation (Indian Labour Conference); conclude with a quote.
Instructor's preference: start with data, end with a quote. Always cite a domestic best practice before a foreign one.

1. Topic 1: PESA — framework & provisions

Model introduction

Write the full form at least once: the Panchayats (Extension to the Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996 is a landmark law designed to deepen democratic decentralisation and protect tribal rights by empowering the Gram Sabha.

"Mava Nate Mava Raj" — our village, our rule.
— Gond-language slogan of tribal self-determination; usable to open or close a PESA answer

Context pegs

  • 6:36Jharkhand's PESA rules — approved by the state cabinet in December 2025 and notified in early January 2026, nearly three decades after the Act. The lecture uses this as a textbook case of delay in delivering rights, and criticises the rules' drafting as litigation-prone.
  • A tribal civil-society forum filed a contempt petition in the High Court over non-implementation.
  • Chhattisgarh — protests in the Hasdeo region against private coal mining in forest "no-go" areas.
  • Odisha — protests at Sijimali against bauxite mining in hills the community holds sacred.

Why PESA was needed

Historical exploitation (especially in the colonial period), centralised administration that overrode customs, and the absence of empowered grassroots democracy in tribal areas led the Bhuria Committee (1994–95) to recommend extending Panchayati Raj to Scheduled Areas. PESA followed in 1996.

Article 243M(4)(b): empowers Parliament to extend Part IX (Panchayats) to Fifth Schedule areas with exceptions and modifications — the constitutional basis for PESA.

Key provisions

ProvisionWhat it does
Conformity with customState laws on panchayats must be consistent with customary law, social and religious practices, and traditional management of community resources.
ReservationSeats reserved in proportion to population, but STs must have at least 50% of seats; all chairperson posts reserved for STs (the "politics of presence" idea from Class 1).
Gram Sabha's cultural roleSafeguard traditions, cultural identity, community resources and customary dispute resolution.
Minor mineralsPrior recommendation of the Gram Sabha/panchayat mandatory for prospecting licences and mining leases/auctions.
Natural resourcesOwnership of minor forest produce; management of water bodies.
Social regulationPower to enforce prohibition or regulate intoxicants; prevent land alienation and restore unlawfully alienated land.

Three underlying aims: prevent marginalisation; ensure genuine decentralisation through an empowered Gram Sabha; stop the influx of non-tribals and the resulting land alienation.

2. Topic 1: PESA — debates, challenges, judgments

Debates

Modern state vs. customary tribal law
Formal state institutions have not been harmonised with traditional structures in Fifth Schedule areas; the state often treats tribals as an obstacle to development rather than partners in it.
Development vs. autonomy
Full Gram Sabha autonomy is seen by some as an obstacle to growth, because tribal areas sit on major mineral reserves. Niyamgiri is cited both ways — as a triumph of consent, and as an example of projects being blocked.
Consultation vs. consent
Consultation = seek the Gram Sabha's opinion, then proceed anyway. Consent = the Gram Sabha's "no" is a binding veto. States generally treat it as consultation. A 2016–17 study in Jharkhand's Khunti district (as cited) found about 65% of people whose land was acquired said they were never even consulted.
Empowerment vs. entanglement
Does PESA empower, or trap communities in legal hurdles? Critics argue some rules are drafted so ambiguously that disputes go to court, producing delays that effectively bypass the Gram Sabha.
Identity vs. material rights
Politicisation of tribal identity vs. delivering material rights — livelihoods, minor forest produce, community assets.

Arguments for PESA

  • Constitutional guarantee; democratic decentralisation; empowered Gram Sabha; protection of community resources.
  • Together with the Forest Rights Act, 2006, the two pillars of a rights-based approach to tribal welfare.
  • Reduces tribal alienation — a root cause of left-wing extremism.

Challenges (how PESA is subverted)

  1. Bypassing for mining/industry — Gram Sabha decisions treated as mere consultation, or skipped.
  2. Delayed and weak rules — Jharkhand took decades; vague drafting invites litigation.
  3. Capacity deficit — many representatives have limited literacy and cannot fully use funds and powers.
  4. Parallel bodies — Joint Forest Management Committees, controlled by forest officials, bureaucratise decisions.
  5. CAMPA (Compensatory Afforestation Fund Act, 2016) — afforestation planned by the forest bureaucracy without Gram Sabha consultation or tribal representation; "land banks" of degraded forest can take tribal land; top-down.
  6. The urbanisation loophole — if a Gram Sabha opposes a project, the area is reclassified as urban (e.g., a nagar panchayat), taking it outside PESA. The lecture cites a dispute in Chhattisgarh's Surajpur district.
  7. Organisations claiming to defend tribal culture often stay silent on these issues.

Judgments

CaseWhat it stands for
Samatha v. State of Andhra Pradesh (1997)Tribal land in Scheduled Areas cannot be leased/transferred to non-tribals, including private mining companies.
BALCO Employees' Union v. Union of India (2002)Expressed reservations about Samatha and permitted BALCO's mining operations, reasoning the matter was governed by Madhya Pradesh law — a dilution.
Orissa Mining Corporation v. MoEF (Niyamgiri, 2013)Gram Sabhas must decide on religious/cultural rights affected by mining; the Gram Sabhas rejected the bauxite project — effectively treating consultation as consent.

Views and committees

  • B.D. Sharma (former SC/ST Commissioner): PESA was meant to undo historical injustice but is hindered by an outsider-biased bureaucracy.
  • Planning Commission expert group (2008): found violations by officials who view tribal autonomy as an impediment.
  • A committee (name unclear in the transcript) recommended an absolute "right to say no" to land acquisition and land-for-land compensation.

Way forward

  • Harmonise PESA, FRA and CAMPA; make Gram Sabha consent, not consultation, the standard.
  • Borrow from the Sixth Schedule: more substantive autonomy, not just representation.
  • Capacity-building for Gram Sabhas.
  • With LWE declining, robust PESA and FRA implementation is the foundation of a post-LWE strategy; otherwise alienation returns.
  • Conclude with "Jal, Jangal, Zameen" — water, forest, land.

3. Topic 1: Model answer structure

PESA 1996 was envisioned as a new social contract for tribal self-governance, yet it remains affected by governance paradoxes. Examine.
— Practice question set in class
  1. Intro: PESA as a "constitution within the constitution" protecting jal–jangal–zameen; Bhuria Committee origin.
  2. The new social contract (diagram): Gram Sabha empowerment + customary law + administrative autonomy + resource control → dignity, empowerment, sustainable development.
  3. Positives with examples — Niyamgiri and consent.
  4. Paradoxes — bureaucratic bypass, consultation without consent, parallel bodies, CAMPA, urbanisation loophole, delayed rules (4–5 points with examples).
  5. Suggestions — consent-based approach, capacity-building; cite B.D. Sharma or the 2008 expert group.
  6. Conclusion — the Gond slogan or "Jal, Jangal, Zameen".
Instructor's feedback: many students finished in four minutes — speed is good, but the answer must still show the "social contract → impact → paradox" logic, not just list provisions.

4. Topic 2: Charities, donors & NGOs — concepts & data

Model introductions (pick one)

Charities, donors and NGOs form the bedrock of a thriving civil society, bridging the gap between state capacity and grassroots development.
— Option 1
India possesses a deeply ingrained cultural ethos of everyday generosity, grounded in daan, seva and Gandhian trusteeship.
— Option 2

Context pegs

  • The FCRA Amendment Bill, 2026 — sweeping changes; put on hold after criticism (see Section 6).
  • Governance disputes in large trusts, including the Tata Trusts.
  • New funding instruments: the Social Stock Exchange and social impact bonds.

Key concepts

  • Philanthropy/charity: voluntary action and donation of resources for the public good.
  • Noblesse oblige: the moral duty of the wealthy to give.
  • CSR: companies meeting profit/size thresholds must spend at least 2% of average net profit of the preceding three years on social activities (Companies Act, 2013).
  • Social Stock Exchange: registered non-profits can raise funds by issuing Zero Coupon Zero Principal (ZCZP) instruments.
  • Article 19(1)(c): right to form associations.
  • Know the differences: donor (anyone who gives), charity (narrower, relief-oriented), NGO (broad — advocacy, rights-based, service delivery), philanthropy (strategic, long-term giving).

Data

How India Gives 2025–26 (Ashoka University, as cited): Indian households give roughly US$6 billion a year; about 68% of Indians give; only ~15% of household giving reaches formal non-profits; the largest share (~46%) goes to religious institutions.
CSR spending is about ₹25,000+ crore a year, but roughly 70% is routed through corporate-run foundations rather than independent small NGOs.
Since 2015, FCRA registrations of more than 18,000 NGOs have been cancelled (analysis of MHA data).
India Nonprofit Report 2025 (survey of ~400 NGOs): 91% run on annual budgets under ₹10 crore; over 70% report funding deficits.
A MoSPI-cited estimate: civil-society organisations generate ~27 lakh jobs plus ~34 lakh full-time volunteers.
Geographical concentration: five states (Maharashtra, Gujarat, Uttar Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka) hold ~60% of registered non-profits; aspirational districts account for only ~5%.
Reuse this data beyond NGO questions — inclusive growth, poverty, regional disparity, savings behaviour. "Aspirational districts hold only 5% of NPOs" fits any regional-disparity answer.

5. Topic 2: Debates

California consensus vs. institution-building

California consensus: a modern approach to philanthropy driven by tech billionaires, seeking rapid, measurable social change through technology and data, often bypassing the state.
DimensionWashington consensusCalifornia consensus
DomainDevelopment economics — neoliberalism, free marketsPrivate foundations, tech billionaires
MechanismTop-down conditionalitiesTop-down, outcome-tied programme funding
Theory of changeMarket liberalisationTechnological innovation and data
MeasureGDP growthMeasurable outcomes / metrics
View of the stateRoll it backBypass it

Contrast with the traditional model: years of grassroots institution-building and social capital, driven by volunteers. Related consensuses to know: Washington (1990s), Beijing (state-led), London.

Problems with the California consensus: an accountability deficit (foundations answer to no one), a coordination deficit (many donors, little coordination), and a democratic legitimacy deficit (e.g., a foundation deciding which seed or medicine is "better" without stakeholder consent).

Other debates

State control vs. autonomy
How much regulation is needed before it cripples non-profits — the debate underlying FCRA.
Programmatic vs. flexible funding
Programmatic money is tied to a specific short-term project; NGOs lose flexibility and must chase a new donor for each programme. Flexible (core) funding supports the whole organisation.
Traditional vs. new-age funding
Traditional: foundations backing grassroots NGOs and volunteers. New-age: crowdfunding, social impact bonds, social stock exchanges, micro-donations at app checkout.
Charity vs. for-profit pivot
Organisations starting as non-profits and converting to for-profit structures when charity models can't fund ambitions — the lecture cites the dispute between Elon Musk and OpenAI.
Charity vs. workers' rights
Charities that champion the vulnerable may not protect their own staff and volunteers; hence arguments to treat them as an "industry" under labour law.
Ethics of aid — "perform your pain"
Beneficiaries, mostly women, are pushed to publicly perform their suffering in appeal videos to raise money — commodification, a gender bias, and added trauma.

Impact vs. effect

Anurag Behar (CEO, Azim Premji Foundation) distinguishes measuring impact from measuring effect. "Impact" implies a single intervention caused the change, and pushes NGOs to produce impressive reports over real work. "Effect" asks what an intervention contributed, acknowledging that social change is collective (SHGs, anganwadi and ASHA workers, the state all contribute).

6. Topic 2: FCRA Amendment Bill 2026

The Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act regulates the acceptance and use of foreign contributions by individuals, associations and companies. The bill's stated aim: protect national security and stop foreign funds being misused for unlawful activities.

Key changes proposed

ProvisionWhat it does
Deemed cessationRegistration automatically ends if renewal isn't applied for, is denied, or isn't granted before expiry.
Designated authorityA government-notified authority manages the foreign contributions and assets created from them once registration ends.
Centralised enforcementInvestigations need prior approval of the central government (to avoid fragmented probes).
Wider prohibitionsExtended to anyone producing or broadcasting news and current-affairs content.
Expanded liabilityBeyond office-bearers to trustees, partners and governing-body members.

For

  • National security; preventing asset-stripping (the designated authority keeps assets in public use); strict compliance timelines.

Against

  • No prior judicial appeal — registration ends first, appeal comes after → operational paralysis; against natural justice.
  • Possible violation of fundamental rights — association, religious freedom, property.
  • Procedural paralysis — cessation can follow from bureaucratic delay in processing renewal, not misconduct.
  • Chilling effect on civil society and free speech.

Quotes and best practices

If a free society cannot help the many who are poor, it cannot save the few who are rich.
— John F. Kennedy, inaugural address (1961)
  • A sociologist (name unclear) argues the California consensus has shifted activism from passion to technocracy, starving grassroots organisations.
  • Social Stock Exchange; retail crowdfunding — Keralites crowdfunded tens of crores to pay "blood money" for a compatriot on death row abroad.
  • Goonj — collects reusable goods (not just money) for rural communities.

7. Topic 2: Model answer structure

Civil society organisations are often described as the third pillar of development. Discuss how stringent foreign funding regulations impact welfare services for marginalised people in India.
— Practice question set in class
  1. Intro (keep short): a three-pillar diagram — government, market, civil society — holding up "development"; CSOs ensure last-mile delivery, grassroots development, accountability and advocacy, community trust and mobilisation. One or two NGO examples.
  2. Why regulation: 18,000+ cancellations since 2015 indicate misconduct, tax evasion and irregularities → stringent rules like the FCRA Amendment Bill 2026.
  3. Impact on the marginalised: aspirational districts already have only ~5% of NPOs; schemes delivered through NGOs suffer; group-wise effects — women (shelter homes, trafficking survivors), children in conflict with law, SC/STs, migrants (wage rights), undertrials, RTI and environmental advocacy.
  4. Balance: scrutiny is legitimate for national security.
  5. Way forward: light-touch regulation, new models (Social Stock Exchange), plus regulating new risks (impact-chasing, "perform your pain", diversion of crowdfunded money).
Take 30 seconds to sketch a structure before writing. Notes knowledge often fails to reach the paper because students don't re-tailor it to the question's demand (here: marginalised people).

8. Topic 3: Section 17A, PCA — evolution & split verdict

Model introduction

The Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 is the primary statutory shield safeguarding India's democratic and administrative framework from corruption.

Context

The Supreme Court's split verdict (January 2026, Centre for Public Interest Litigation v. Union of India) on Section 17A highlights the permanent tension between empowering anti-corruption agencies and protecting honest civil servants.

Key provisions

  • The PCA consolidates anti-corruption law and defines public servant broadly — government employees, MPs/MLAs, and judges.
  • Section 17A (inserted 2018): investigators need prior approval of the competent government before any inquiry or investigation into an offence relating to recommendations made or decisions taken in discharge of official duties.
  • Reading between the lines: "recommendations and decisions" are made by senior officials, while juniors implement — so the protection mainly covers the top, raising an Article 14 concern.
  • Rationale: tackle policy paralysis caused by the "play-it-safe syndrome" of officials afraid to sign files.
  • Section 19: sanction to prosecute after investigation — the final safeguard against trials for bona fide actions. (17A = before investigation; 19 = before prosecution.)

9. Topic 3: Data, debates, views, way forward

Corruption Perceptions Index: India ranked around 91st out of ~182 countries (as cited — verify the latest edition).
CVC annual report: 7,000+ corruption cases investigated by the CBI pending in courts, including 379 pending for over 20 years.
CBI registered ~502 PCA cases in 2024 involving ~859 public servants, of whom ~221 were gazetted officers (as cited).

Debates

  • Protecting the honest vs. protecting the corrupt.
  • Executive control vs. independent scrutiny (political executive vs. Lokpal).
  • Accountability vs. administrative paralysis.
  • Judicial interpretation vs. judicial legislation — substituting the Lokpal for the government arguably goes beyond reading down.

For and against Section 17A

  • For: gatekeeping filters frivolous complaints — the problem is who gatekeeps, not gatekeeping itself.
  • Against: Article 14 violation; conflict of interest; destroys the element of surprise — an official tipped off by a sanction request can destroy evidence.

Views and quotes

If honest public servants are not given a basic assurance that their decisions will not face frivolous complaints, it is the nation that will suffer.
— Justice K.V. Viswanathan (paraphrased)
Section 17A, in substance, protects the corrupt rather than the honest.
— Justice B.V. Nagarathna (paraphrased)
Be you ever so high, the law is above you.
— Thomas Fuller, quoted by Lord Denning; widely cited by Indian courts
  • An economist's phrase (attributed in the lecture to Kaushik Basu — verify): the "Sanskritisation of corruption" — its social acceptance.
  • Santhanam Committee (1964) — strengthening anti-corruption law; led to the CVC.
  • Vohra Committee (1993) — the politician–bureaucrat–criminal nexus (the Adarsh Housing scam as an example of political–bureaucratic nexus).
  • Law Commission, 254th Report — approval for launching investigation should lie with independent bodies like the Lokpal.
  • India hosted a steering committee meeting of the GlobE Network (Global Operational Network of Anti-Corruption Law Enforcement Authorities); India is party to the UN Convention against Corruption.
  • Illustrations from the news: a ₹500 bribery case closed after 36 years; a Bihar case where seized bribe cash was claimed to have been eaten by rats.

Way forward

  • Independent screening via Lokpal/Lokayukta (Justice Viswanathan; 254th Report).
  • Deterrence through speed — fast-track courts for swift disposal.
  • Transparent procurement (blockchain, digital systems); whistleblower protection.
The pursuit of anti-corruption measures must not result in policy paralysis. Analyse in the context of Section 17A of the PCA and the recent judicial verdict.
— Practice question; skeleton reviewed at the start of Class 8: quote → PCA → 17A → evolution diagram → balance → concerns → Lokpal-based filter → conclusion (GlobE / "steel frame")

10. Practice MCQs (self-authored, Prelims-style)

Original questions written from the facts in this class.

Q1
Which constitutional provision enables Parliament to extend Part IX to Fifth Schedule areas, forming the basis of PESA?
(A) Article 244(2)
(B) Article 243M(4)(b)
(C) Article 243ZC
(D) Article 275(1)
Answer: (B). Article 243M(4)(b). Article 243ZC is the analogous provision for municipalities.
Q2
Under PESA, the share of seats reserved for Scheduled Tribes in panchayats in Scheduled Areas must be:
(A) Exactly in proportion to population, with no minimum
(B) Not less than one-half of total seats
(C) One-third of total seats
(D) Decided by the Governor
Answer: (B). At least 50%, and all chairperson posts reserved for STs.
Q3
In which case did the Supreme Court hold that tribal land in Scheduled Areas cannot be leased to non-tribal private mining companies?
(A) Samatha v. State of Andhra Pradesh
(B) BALCO Employees' Union v. Union of India
(C) Vineet Narain v. Union of India
(D) Subramanian Swamy v. Director, CBI
Answer: (A). Samatha (1997); BALCO (2002) later diluted it.
Q4
Zero Coupon Zero Principal (ZCZP) instruments are associated with:
(A) RBI's sovereign gold bonds
(B) Fundraising by non-profits on the Social Stock Exchange
(C) CSR expenditure accounting
(D) FCRA registration renewal
Answer: (B). Registered NPOs list ZCZP instruments on the SSE segment; donors get no interest or principal back.
Q5
Section 17A of the Prevention of Corruption Act requires prior approval:
(A) Before prosecuting any public servant
(B) Before inquiry/investigation into offences relating to recommendations made or decisions taken in official duties
(C) Only for officers of Joint Secretary rank and above
(D) Only for MPs and MLAs
Answer: (B). Sanction to prosecute is Section 19. The rank-based rule (C) was Section 6A of the DSPE Act, struck down in 2014.
Q6
Section 6A of the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act was struck down in Subramanian Swamy v. Director, CBI (2014) mainly because it:
(A) Violated federalism
(B) Created an unreasonable classification by rank, violating Article 14
(C) Conflicted with the Lokpal Act
(D) Exceeded Parliament's legislative competence
Answer: (B). Protecting only Joint Secretary-and-above officers was held to violate equality.
Q7
The committee that recommended extending Panchayati Raj to Scheduled Areas, leading to PESA, was headed by:
(A) L.M. Singhvi
(B) Dilip Singh Bhuria
(C) Balwant Rai Mehta
(D) U.N. Dhebar
Answer: (B). The Bhuria Committee (1994–95).
Q8
The Santhanam Committee (1964) is best known for recommending the creation of:
(A) The Lokpal
(B) The Central Vigilance Commission
(C) The Enforcement Directorate
(D) The CBI's Economic Offences Wing
Answer: (B). The CVC was set up in 1964 on its recommendation.

Glossary — quick revision

Panchayats (Extension to the Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996 — Gram Sabha empowerment in Fifth Schedule areas.
Consultation vs. consent
Seeking the Gram Sabha's view vs. treating its "no" as a binding veto.
Urbanisation loophole
Reclassifying a Scheduled Area as urban to escape PESA after Gram Sabha opposition.
Post-LWE strategy
Using PESA and FRA implementation to prevent renewed tribal alienation after LWE declines.
Noblesse oblige
The moral duty of the wealthy to give.
California consensus
Tech-billionaire philanthropy seeking rapid, measurable, tech-driven change, often bypassing the state.
Programmatic funding
Money tied to a specific short-term project rather than the organisation.
Impact vs. effect
Claiming sole causation vs. acknowledging a contribution to collective change (Anurag Behar).
"Perform your pain"
Beneficiaries forced to publicly display suffering to attract aid.
Deemed cessation (FCRA Bill)
Automatic end of registration if renewal is not obtained in time.
Designated authority (FCRA Bill)
Government body that takes over foreign-funded assets after cessation.
Section 17A vs. 19 PCA
Approval to investigate vs. sanction to prosecute.
Single Directive
Executive instructions requiring govt approval for CBI probes; struck down in Vineet Narain (1997).
Play-it-safe syndrome
Officials avoiding decisions for fear of investigation — causing policy paralysis.
Element of surprise
Lost when a sanction request alerts the suspect official.

Revision kit (ARCS method)

A — Active Recall

1. Name four ways PESA is bypassed in practice.

Treating consent as consultation; parallel bodies (JFMCs); CAMPA land banks/top-down afforestation; the urbanisation loophole; delayed/vague rules.

2. What are the three deficits of the California consensus?

Accountability, coordination, democratic legitimacy.

3. Give the five main changes in the FCRA Amendment Bill 2026.

Deemed cessation; designated authority for assets; centralised enforcement; prohibition extended to news/current-affairs producers; expanded liability of key functionaries.

4. Trace prior approval for corruption probes from the Single Directive to Section 17A.

Single Directive → Vineet Narain (1997) strikes it down → Sec 6A DSPE (2003, JS and above) → Subramanian Swamy (2014) strikes 6A down → Sec 17A PCA (2018) → split verdict (Jan 2026).

5. What did each judge hold in the 17A split verdict?

Nagarathna: unconstitutional (Art 14, conflict of interest, "old wine in new bottle"). Viswanathan: valid but approval to come from independent bodies like the Lokpal/Lokayukta.

The Jharkhand cabinet approved the rules on 23 December 2025; they were formally notified on 2 January 2026, covering roughly 16,000 villages and 2,074 panchayats in the state's Fifth Schedule areas — making Jharkhand one of the last major Fifth Schedule states to operationalise PESA.

Section 17A split verdict — verified

Delivered in January 2026 in Centre for Public Interest Litigation v. Union of India. Justice B.V. Nagarathna held 17A unconstitutional; Justice K.V. Viswanathan upheld it but read it down so that sanction decisions are taken by independent bodies such as the Lokpal or Lokayukta. The matter goes to a larger bench.

FCRA Amendment Bill 2026 — verified

PRS's legislative brief confirms the deemed-cessation and designated-authority provisions. The designated authority (under MHA) has civil-court powers; foreign contributions and assets provisionally vest in it on cancellation/cessation and permanently vest if fresh registration isn't obtained; religious character of places of worship must be maintained. Check the bill's status before the exam — the lecture said it was on hold.
