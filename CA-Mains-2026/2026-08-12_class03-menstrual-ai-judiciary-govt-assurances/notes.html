0. Opening fragment: reproductive autonomy (recap from previous exercise)

0:03–3:55The class opens mid-discussion, reviewing a student's answer structure on reproductive autonomy — likely continuing the DNA/paternity and bodily-autonomy theme flagged as "next class" at the end of Class 2. The instructor models a three-phase framing:

  1. Phase 1 — social factor: focus on the institution of marriage and the Indian Divorce Act framework rather than the individual.
  2. Phase 2 — bodily/decisional autonomy: focus shifts to the individual's right to make independent reproductive choices, free from unwanted state interference — part of the "decisional privacy" dimension of Article 21.
  3. Phase 3 — balancing framework: weighing traditional social-factor concerns against individual autonomy via a proportionality-style test.
  • This dimension can be misused as a tool for harassment or coercion (e.g., in maintenance or custody disputes).
  • Closes with international best-practice suggestions (e.g., Australia, European countries) on reproductive health education.

Legal/statutory framework cited

  • Assisted Reproductive Technology (Regulation) Act, 2021 (ART Act): Section 21(g) restricts ART services to women above 21 and below 50 years, and men above 21 and below 55 years.
  • Medical Termination of Pregnancy (Amendment) Act, 2021 (MTP Act): raises the upper gestation limit from 20 to 24 weeks for special categories of women (rape/incest survivors, minors, differently-abled women, and other vulnerable categories notified by the government). Beyond 24 weeks, termination is permitted only on the opinion of a State-level Medical Board, mainly in cases of substantial foetal abnormality — there is no absolute upper cap for this category.
  • Pre-Conception and Pre-Natal Diagnostic Techniques (PCPNDT) Act, 1994/2003: prohibits sex-determination and sex-selective abortion; cited as a socially protective, complementary statute to the ART/MTP framework.
The lecture frames this as a "rigid statutory threshold vs. reality of reproductive choices" debate — a reusable framing device for any question that pits a bright-line statutory age/time limit against individualized circumstances (late-discovered pregnancies, medical emergencies, genuine fertility-treatment needs delayed by life circumstances).

Formal vs. substantive equality

24:32Central conceptual move: treating men and women identically (formal equality) is not enough when biological realities differ — the constitutional goal is substantive equality, which focuses on fair outcomes, not just equal treatment on paper.

Sandra Fredman's four dimensions of substantive equality

DimensionMeaningApplied to menstrual policy
1. RedistributionRedress material/economic disadvantageFree/subsidized sanitary products; safe, private sanitation infrastructure in schools and workplaces
2. RecognitionAddress stigma, stereotyping, and prejudiceNormalizing menstruation in public discourse; challenging the idea that it is "dirty" or shameful
3. ParticipationEnsure full participation in decision-making and public lifeEnsuring menstruation-related stigma does not push women out of school, work, or leadership roles
4. TransformationStructural/institutional change, not just individual accommodationUniform, comprehensive workplace/school menstrual health policy rather than ad hoc leave
Attribute this framework by name — "Sandra Fredman's four-dimensional model of substantive equality (redistribution, recognition, participation, transformation)" — rather than the lecture's garbled rendering ("distribution... participation... transformation"); this is a genuinely citable, real academic framework used in UK and Indian equality jurisprudence.

Key line: dignity is not just an idea

Dignity is not just an idea — it means living without humiliation. Any woman forced to endure a preventable indignity is having her right to life, under Article 21, violated.
— Instructor's formulation, reusable for any Article 21/dignity-linked question

2. Topic 1: Debates, data & live exercise

The "benevolent sexism" critique

Benevolent sexism (key debate term)
The critique that framing menstrual leave purely around female "weakness" — rather than genuine biological accommodation — risks reinforcing patronizing stereotypes ("we help you because you are weak") instead of practicing genuine equity. The instructor stresses this is a real tension, not a reason to reject leave policies outright, but a reason to frame them carefully (accommodation of biological reality, not condescension).
Statistical/employment discrimination risk
A parallel debate (echoing the Indian Supreme Court's own stated concern in 2023): employers may view women as a "financial burden" under mandatory leave policies, leading to hiring discrimination, denial of promotions, or preference for short-term contracts — precisely the risk the Court flagged in declining to mandate a nationwide policy.
Multiplier effect
Reducing school/work dropout linked to menstrual stigma has compounding benefits: lower dropout → better job opportunities → higher retention → greater economic contribution — framed as a long-term multiplier rather than a short-term cost.

Key data points

Dasra (2014, with USAID and Kiawah Trust): 70% of mothers consider menstruation "dirty," perpetuating a culture of shame.
~23 million girls drop out of school annually in India due to inadequate menstrual hygiene management infrastructure (a subset framed loosely in the lecture as "one in five girls").
WHO-cited estimate: ~60% of menstruating individuals in India experience dysmenorrhea (painful periods) but do not disclose it due to stigma/lack of awareness.

Global and domestic comparisons

Supreme Court of India — Shailendra Mani Tripathi vs. Union of India (2023):
  • The Court declined to mandate a nationwide menstrual leave policy via PIL.
  • Reasoning: a compulsory legal mandate could backfire — discouraging employers from hiring women, or denying them leadership roles/promotions on the assumption of frequent absence.
  • Instead directed the Centre to frame a model policy after consulting states and stakeholders, favoring voluntary/state-level action over a binding nationwide mandate.

Suggestions and conclusion

  • Whole-of-society approach: not just government leave policy, but school curricula, male awareness campaigns, and workplace flexibility (e.g., work-from-home options) together.
  • Use CSR (Corporate Social Responsibility) funding to build sanitation infrastructure in schools/workplaces.
  • Avoid an either/or framing between "menstrual leave" and "broader welfare spending" — the instructor explicitly rejects the idea that funding menstrual leave is a wasteful "freebie" instead of a productivity investment (a deliberate callback to the previous class's freebies-vs-welfare framework).
Access to dignity cannot be reduced to a purely technical application of statutory conditions divorced from the factual, lived context of the person seeking it.
— Instructor's model conclusion line, reusable across reproductive-rights and dignity-linked questions generally

3. Topic 2: AI in the judiciary

Recommended structure: introduction → why AI is being introduced (data-first) → key regulatory development → arguments for → arguments/concerns against → the "human in the loop" principle → global/domestic best practices → conclusion.

Model introduction

India's judicial system, burdened by a massive backlog, is increasingly turning to artificial intelligence for efficiency — yet this technological embrace must remain rooted in human empathy and constitutionality.
— Instructor's model introduction; can open with the backlog statistic instead of this line if a data-first introduction is preferred

Key data points (the case for AI adoption)

India's judicial backlog: roughly 4.5–5 crore cases pending across all courts (District Courts alone: ~4.6 crore).
Judge-to-population ratio: ~15–22 judges per million (estimates vary by methodology/year) against the Law Commission's recommended 50 per million.
eCourts Mission Mode Project spending — Phase I (2011–15): ₹935 crore sanctioned / ~₹639 crore spent; Phase II (2015–23): ₹1,670 crore; Phase III (2023–27): ₹7,210 crore sanctioned.

The key regulatory development

Draft Regulations for Use of Artificial Intelligence in Courts, 2026: released by the Supreme Court of India's AI Committee on 3 June 2026 for public consultation (deadline later extended to 15 July 2026).
  • Permitted: AI may assist with legal research, drafting, scheduling, transcription, translation of judgments, citation verification, and case/record management.
  • Barred: AI cannot be used to decide cases, determine bail eligibility, assess witness credibility, or otherwise influence judicial deliberation.
  • Disclosure: lawyers must disclose AI use when filing pleadings/documents/evidence.
  • Oversight: every AI system deployed in courts is subject to annual technical, legal, and ethical audits, overseen by a proposed full-time apex body of Supreme Court/High Court judges, government representatives, and AI experts.

Why this matters right now: real hallucination incidents

  • December 2024: the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal's Bengaluru bench issued an order citing three Supreme Court judgments and one Madras High Court ruling that did not exist — traced to unverified ChatGPT use by a tax department representative.
  • October 2025: the Bombay High Court quashed a ₹27.91 crore income-tax assessment after finding the National Faceless Assessment Centre had relied on three non-existent judicial decisions.
  • September 2025: a Delhi High Court petition was withdrawn after opposing counsel exposed fabricated citations, including invented paragraphs falsely attributed to the real case Raj Narain vs. Indira Nehru Gandhi.
  • 27 February 2026: a Supreme Court bench (Justices P.S. Narasimha and Alok Aradhe) held that relying on non-existent/fake AI-generated judgments is professional misconduct with disciplinary consequences, not merely an innocent error.
This "hallucination is a feature, not a bug" framing (an AI model will confidently generate a plausible-sounding but fake citation because it is designed to produce fluent text, not verified fact) is a strong, quotable line for any AI-and-law question — attribute it generically to AI-ethics literature rather than any single named source unless you can verify a specific citation.

4. Topic 2: Debates, safeguards & live exercise

Core debates

DebatePro-AI positionConcern
Innovation vs. independenceAI automates administrative/scheduling tasks, freeing judicial timeRisk of judicial independence eroding if AI subtly shapes outcomes over time
Efficiency vs. algorithmic justiceAI can flag case patterns, assist risk-scoring for administrative triage"Black box" problem — no explanation for how an AI reached a risk score; undermines reasoned decision-making, a core judicial value
Access vs. digital divideAI-enabled translation/simplification can widen access to justice, especially in a linguistically diverse countryThose without digital literacy/access may be further disadvantaged
Technology import vs. self-relianceFrontier AI tools accelerate case management immediatelyNearly all frontier AI is imported — raises data sovereignty and long-term dependency concerns

Key safeguard principles (from the draft regulations, as discussed)

  • Human in the loop: "India has consciously approached technology as an aid to human reasoning, rather than as a substitute for independent judicial thought" — technology must augment, not replace, human discernment.
  • Absolute accountability: any AI-assisted decision's accountability rests exclusively with the responsible human (judge/court staff), not the technology or its developers.
  • Ethical impact assessment: before adoption, an AI system must undergo an assessment evaluating bias risk and vulnerabilities.
  • Right to opt out: litigants should retain a right to opt out of AI-mediated processes where feasible — AI-driven systems "must not reduce people's problems to ticket numbers."
The law, after all, is not an algorithm — it is a reflection of human conscience, shaped by empathy, moral reasoning, and an understanding of context that machines cannot replicate.
— Instructor's model line, strong for an introduction or conclusion on this topic (note: transcript rendered this as "the law is not alum" / "not me altogether" — corrected here to the clearly intended sense)

Global/domestic best practices (contextual, not exhaustively verified from the lecture)

  • Courts internationally increasingly restrict AI to administrative/support functions (scheduling, translation, transcription, legal research) while explicitly excluding core adjudicative decision-making — mirroring India's draft regulations.
  • Mandatory AI-use disclosure by lawyers (as in India's draft rules) is emerging as a common safeguard against uncritical reliance on AI-generated legal research.

5. Topic 3: Committee on Government Assurances

Recommended structure: introduction → origin and function → classification system → data on pendency → issues/debates → suggestions → conclusion.

Model introduction

Parliamentary committees function as the workshops of democracy — the Committee on Government Assurances exists precisely to ensure that promises made on the floor of the House do not simply evaporate once the debate ends.
— Instructor's model introduction

Origin and function

  • Formed in 1953 (Lok Sabha); a corresponding Rajya Sabha committee also exists. Function: to scrutinize assurances, promises, and undertakings given by Ministers on the floor of the House during discussion of bills, resolutions, motions, or questions, and to report on the extent to which they have been implemented.
  • An assurance is deemed pending if not fulfilled within three months of being made.
  • A ministerial statement made on the floor of the House carries a quasi-binding character — not legally enforceable like a statute, but enforced through the institutional/political mechanism of committee scrutiny and follow-up.

Classification system

1:03:18Pending assurances are classified by the Ministry of Parliamentary Affairs into categories (commonly labelled A/B/C in practice) based on which government body/level is responsible for implementation. Tracking cycle:

  • Classification
  • Communication to the ministry concerned
  • Examination of the ministry's action report
  • Final report to the House

Key data points

Approximately 60–66% of assurances made in Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha were reported pending as of March 2024 (year-over-year figures are broadly similar going back to 2022–23), indicating a persistent, not one-off, implementation gap.

6. Topic 3: Debates & live exercise

Core debates

  • "Specious excuses": scholars' criticism that ministries often repeatedly request extensions ("we are working on it") without genuine progress, effectively evading the promise rather than fulfilling or formally withdrawing it.
  • No penalty mechanism: the committee can summon ministers/officials and seek explanations, but has no direct power to penalize non-compliance — its force is reputational/institutional rather than coercive.
  • Institutional memory function: beyond accountability, the committee also serves as the institutional memory of Parliament, tracking which promises relate to which actions over time — described by the instructor as transforming parliamentary debate "from mere political theatre into binding governance commitments."

Suggestions

  • A dedicated online assurance-monitoring/tracking system to allow real-time public visibility into the status of pending assurances (building on existing digital initiatives of the Ministry of Parliamentary Affairs).
  • Time-bound targets and stronger committee powers to summon officials and demand specific, dated action plans rather than open-ended "under consideration" responses.
A parliamentary process meant to act as a watchdog for people's rights becomes hollow if the promises it tracks are perpetually deferred rather than delivered.
— Instructor's model conclusion line

A — Active Recall: self-test (no peeking until you answer)

1. What are Sandra Fredman's four dimensions of substantive equality?

Redistribution (redress material disadvantage), recognition (address stigma/stereotyping), participation (ensure voice in decision-making), transformation (structural/institutional change, not just individual accommodation).

2. Why did the Supreme Court decline to mandate a nationwide menstrual leave policy in 2023?

In Shailendra Mani Tripathi vs. Union of India, the Court feared a mandatory policy could backfire — employers might view women as costlier to hire, denying them leadership roles or promotions. It directed the Centre to frame a model policy via consultation instead of a binding mandate.

3. What can AI NOT be used for under India's Draft AI Regulations for Courts, 2026?

AI cannot decide cases, determine bail eligibility, assess witness credibility, or otherwise influence judicial deliberation. It can assist with research, drafting, scheduling, transcription, translation, citation verification, and case/record management.

5. When is an assurance made in Parliament classified as "pending," and who tracks it?

An assurance is pending if not fulfilled within 3 months of being made. The Ministry of Parliamentary Affairs classifies and tracks it; the Committee on Government Assurances (formed 1953) scrutinizes implementation and can summon ministries/officials.

Draft AI Regulations for Courts, 2026 — verified timeline and scope
