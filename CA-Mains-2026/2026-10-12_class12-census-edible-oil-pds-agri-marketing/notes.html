0. Answer review: behavioural health — answer the demand

The question asked for a multi-pronged strategy. Many answers spent two pages explaining the statement and gave the strategy a few lines — scoring ~1 mark against ~4–5 for an answer that proved the need briefly with data and then focused on the strategy.

  1. Intro: Omran's epidemiological transition (nature-made → man-made diseases).
  2. Define behavioural health threats in a box (lifestyle disease, digital addiction, academic burnout, UPF/HFSS).
  3. Why consumer change alone fails — a short diagram: corporate profit motive → addictive design (digital or UPF) → dopamine loop → distorted consumer choice → need for state action. Back it with data (60% of deaths from NCDs; ~10% of adults with mental disorders).
  4. Multi-pronged strategy — the main body. Two ways to structure it: a PICKAET list (policy, institutions, collaboration, knowledge, awareness, economy, technology), or concentric circles — state (soda taxes in Mexico/Chile/Saudi; Australia's age-gating; Indian states' proposals) → community (peer-buddy models) → media and civil society → individual awareness.
  5. Cite what has worked elsewhere — examples, not your own invented ideas, earn marks.

1. Topic 1: Census 2027 — framework & data

Model introduction

The census is the bedrock of democratic governance — the "mother of all counts" that informs India's developmental trajectory, welfare and representation. Or simply: after a 15-year gap, India has begun Census 2027.

Context and phases

  • Phase 1 — house-listing and housing census (HLO): April–September 2026, staggered by state; counts buildings and households (~33 questions).
  • Phase 2 — population enumeration (PE): February 2027 (September 2026 for snow-bound areas); detailed demographic and socio-economic data, including religion and caste.
  • India's 8th census since independence (16th overall); constitutionally mandated.

Extended de facto method

  • People are counted where they are found during the enumeration window, combined with the usual-residence (de jure) logic: residents present during the ~20-day period before the visit are counted at that household; usual members who are away are not double-counted.
  • For census purposes, a household is defined by a common kitchen — one municipal house with two families on separate kitchens counts as two census houses.
  • NRIs are counted only if present during the period.

Legal and administrative framework

  • Entry 69, Union List — census is a Union subject.
  • Census Act, 1948 — every resident is legally bound to answer census questions.
  • Conducted by the Office of the Registrar General and Census Commissioner, India (Ministry of Home Affairs).
  • First fully digital census: enumerators use smartphones; self-enumeration online generates an SE ID shared with the enumerator.

Data

Budget: ₹11,718 crore (Cabinet, December 2025).
Workforce: ~30–34 lakh enumerators and supervisors, mostly school teachers; enumerators receive an honorarium (lecture: ~₹25,000).
SECC 2011 recorded ~46 lakh distinct caste names — a data-integrity warning for the caste count.
UNFPA: India's population ~146 crore (2025).

Why the census is needed

  • Outdated data — a 15-year gap.
  • Welfare delivery — many are excluded from schemes based on 2011 figures.
  • Delimitation — "one person, one vote, one value"; women's reservation.
  • Social justice — data to address inequality and reservation.
  • Fiscal federalism and urban planning.

2. Topic 1: Digital census, caste census, challenges

Digital census — for and against

ForAgainst
Efficiency; real-time monitoring via the Census Management and Monitoring System portal; faster processing and releaseTechnology constraints: enumerators' own phones, a crash-prone app (reported low ratings), poor rural connectivity
Self-enumeration protects privacyDigital literacy gaps — self-enumeration works for few; in practice someone else often fills it in
Better accuracy and coverage (remote areas)Data errors; respondent fatigue (33 questions) and deliberately wrong answers from fear of losing benefits
Counts PVTGs separately for the first time; tries to capture internal migrants and transgender personsData security; data sanitisation — pressure to record, say, toilets that don't exist

Caste census

  • For: "visibility is the first step toward meaningful inclusion"; enables sub-categorisation to prevent elite capture; better welfare targeting; evidence-based policy.
  • Against: caste count vs. a casteless society — counting reinforces ascriptive identities; administrative difficulty (46 lakh names, aliases); politicisation and identity politics.

Challenges

  • Trust deficit — fear of penalisation; migrants avoiding enumerators.
  • Administrative — 30+ lakh staff, mostly teachers; last-minute dropouts leave untrained staff; overburdened teachers (elections too) → schools suffer.
  • Enumerator safety — harassment, gated societies denying entry ("stranger anxiety"), reported assaults.
  • Coverage gaps — migrants; methodological — the rural/urban binary undercounts peri-urban areas.

Quotes

The census is not just a government job; it is a responsibility of all of us.
— Prime Minister (paraphrased)
The census is a selfie of the nation — a self-portrait of the people that only their government can paint.
— Sociologist Satish Deshpande (paraphrased)
It is only if we count caste today that we can hope for a future where it will not count.
— Satish Deshpande (paraphrased)
  • Angus Deaton: good data is fundamental to good economics.
  • A former UPSC chairperson (name unclear in captions): "reservation for all means reservation for none".

Models and way forward

  • A state built a composite backwardness index to measure marginalisation (state unclear in captions).
  • Austria moved from questionnaires to a register-based census drawing on tax, school, birth/death and building registers — cheaper, can be run any time, avoids respondent-trust problems. India could use its digital public infrastructure similarly.
  • A state conducted its own disability census; the Centre set up a high-level committee on demographic change and illegal migration — which needs baseline data.
  • Way forward: enumerators in pairs in sensitive areas, security for women enumerators, pre-identified vulnerable areas; workload relief for teachers (e.g., temporary staff); drop-down standardisation; independent oversight against data sanitisation; mass-media campaigns for trust.
The credibility of public policy is directly proportional to the integrity of the data it is based upon. In light of Census 2027, discuss the challenges in conducting massive enumeration in India.
— Practice question. Structure: quote/data → why data integrity matters (briefly — the implicit demand) → challenges with examples (SECC errors, enumerator violence) → suggestions (Austria's register model, DPI).

3. Topic 2: Edible oil security

Model introduction (GS-3: start with data)

India's edible oil sector is a critical vulnerability — the country imports more than 50% of its consumption.

Context

  • High price volatility; India's "two oil problem" (crude oil and edible oil, both import-dependent) exposed by the West Asia conflict; the PM publicly urged lower consumption.
  • Indonesia's B50 biodiesel mandate (50% blending) tightened palm-oil supply from India's main source.

Data

Imports ~16 million tonnes a year; ~₹87,000 crore in the first half of 2026 (as cited).
Economic Survey: import dependence over 50%, though the import share fell from ~63% (2014-15) to ~56% (2023-24).
Sources: palm oil — Indonesia, Malaysia; soybean — Brazil, US; sunflower — Russia, Ukraine.
Domestic oils: mustard, groundnut, cottonseed, rice bran.
Oil palm yields up to ~4 tonnes of oil per hectare — about 10× mustard — but has a 4–6 year gestation.
Per-capita consumption rising in both rural and urban areas.

Issues

  • Food and nutrition security — fats are part of tackling hidden hunger; changing diets and processed food raise demand.
  • Import dependence → forex drain, imported inflation, current-account pressure; geopolitical supply risks.
  • Food vs. fuel — the global biodiesel push diverts supply; supply disruptions plus logistics → high landed cost → imported inflation.
  • Low productivity — yields below global benchmarks; pests (weed attacks on mustard).
  • Oil palm concerns — long gestation, high investment and water needs, exotic species, biodiversity and deforestation risks (e.g., the Northeast).
  • Policy distortion — farmers respond to assured price (MSP) and assured market (procurement), which favour rice and wheat — M.S. Swaminathan's "two pillars".
  • Domestic refining — refineries depend on imported crude oils; when finished/refined oil is imported instead, capacity utilisation falls and refineries close. High domestic prices also make oil-meal by-products (animal feed) uncompetitive.
  • SAFTA loophole — duty-free refined oil routed via Nepal, undermining rules of origin.

Views and models

  • An economist (name unclear in captions) suggests importing cheaper US corn for biofuel targets so domestic food land isn't diverted — maize acreage is rising because of ethanol demand.
  • Economic Survey 2025-26: an emerging tension between Atmanirbharta in energy and in food.
  • Madhya Pradesh's Bhavantar Bhugtan Yojana — compensates soybean farmers for prices below MSP via DBT.
  • A proposed regulated corporate leasing model: corporate investment + farmer land + FPO mediation (bargaining power) + government oversight — suited to long-gestation crops like oil palm.

Schemes

  • National Mission on Edible Oils – Oilseeds — raise oilseed production, using SHGs and Krishi Sakhis as community service providers.
  • National Mission on Edible Oils – Oil Palm — add 6.5 lakh hectares and raise crude palm oil output toward 28 lakh tonnes; viability gap payments to farmers linked to international crude palm oil prices (farmers sell fresh fruit bunches to processors).

Way forward

  • Incentivise crop diversification (assured procurement, Bhavantar-style payments); calibrate ethanol pricing so oilseed land isn't cannibalised; enforce rules of origin; promote non-conventional oils (rice bran); import cheap feedstock for biofuels.
Edible oils are increasingly functioning as energy-linked strategic inputs rather than pure food commodities. Examine in the context of the food-versus-fuel debate and its impact on India.
— Practice question. Points: biofuel mandates (B50), climate/NDC targets, cannibalisation of oilseed area, price linkage with crude oil, supply-chain weaponisation → impacts (food inflation, forex, refinery margins, the two-oil problem, India's "2M" agricultural risks — markets and monsoon).

4. Topic 3: PDS & SARTHAK-PDS

Model introduction

The PDS is the backbone of India's food security, delivering subsidised rice and wheat to over 80 crore people — a critical safety net, but with a food-subsidy bill of about ₹2.03 lakh crore (FY 2025-26).

Definition (three elements)

A state intervention to (1) ensure food security, (2) target the poor through subsidised grain, (3) distribute through fair price shops (FPS).

Functioning (draw as a box diagram)

  • Centre: procurement and storage (FCI); transport to state depots.
  • States: beneficiary identification, ration cards, last-mile distribution via FPS.
  • Technology: Aadhaar-based biometric authentication; ePoS devices; One Nation One Ration Card portability.

SARTHAK-PDS

A ₹25,530 crore umbrella scheme (approved May 2026, to March 2031) merging central assistance for intra-state transport, handling and FPS dealer margins with SMART-PDS (digital architecture). It addresses three problems:

  • Logistics — movement from FCI → state → district → FPS, where leakages occur.
  • Beneficiaries — ration-card databases, Aadhaar seeding, portability for migrants.
  • Leakages and grievances — trackable complaints.

Together: foodgrain logistics + FPS viability + beneficiary support — "intelligent governance".

Data

SARTHAK-PDS outlay ₹25,530 crore; ~80 crore beneficiaries.
FCI stocks (1 Jan 2026, as cited): ~309 lakh tonnes rice (plus unmilled paddy) and ~274 lakh tonnes wheat — far above buffer norms (lecture: rice 76, wheat 138 lakh tonnes).
ICRIER: ~28% of PDS grain doesn't reach intended beneficiaries — a loss estimated at ~₹69,000 crore, far more than SARTHAK's outlay.
Economic Survey: near-universal Aadhaar seeding (~99.8%) and ePoS (~99.6% of ~5.43 lakh FPS); over 2 crore ineligible beneficiaries removed after verification.

Significance of PDS

  • Food security; equalising consumption (if it distributes nutritious food, not only cereals); crisis resilience (free grain through COVID and after); farmer support via assured procurement.

Significance of SARTHAK-PDS

  • Supply-chain modernisation (digital tracking, AI); leakage control; FPS viability; support for intra-state movement; cooperative federalism; optimised stock management (offload excess inventory to markets or exports).

Challenges

  • Fiscal burden — ₹2 lakh crore+ subsidy, worsened by open-ended procurement and excess stocks stored under tarpaulin ("cover and plinth") and lost to weather and rodents.
  • Administrative capacity at state, district and block levels.
  • Beneficiary exclusion — Aadhaar biometric failures for the elderly and disabled (links to the Class 8 digital-access ruling).
  • Storage deficit; leakages (28%); nutritional skew (cereals only → hidden hunger).
  • WTO concerns — procurement subsidies fall in the Amber Box; complaints that India exceeds its limits.

Views

  • Shanta Kumar Committee (2015, as cited): scale down PDS coverage, gradually shift to DBT; (lecture) convert ~20% of FPS into nutrition hubs.
  • A scholar (as cited): PDS can be leveraged to equalise basic food consumption across the population.
  • Himanshu and Abhijit Sen: found a negative relationship between PDS coverage and leakage — wider coverage, lower leakage.

Best practices and way forward

  • Grain ATMs ("Annapurti"); digital integration of ration cards.
  • Capacity building; FCI to offload surplus (also cools market prices); raise FPS dealer margins (lecture: ~₹90 per quintal); expedite the vehicle location tracking system.
Examine the role of technology in eliminating leakages and corruption in India's PDS.
— Practice question (instructor's most likely pick). Structure: data (5.4 lakh FPS, 80 crore people, ICRIER 28%) → leakage diagram at each stage (FCI → state → district → FPS: diversion, inventory loss, corruption) → technology (JAM/DBT, Aadhaar, ePoS, ONORC, VTS, grain ATMs, SARTHAK) → challenges → way forward.

5. Topic 4: Agricultural marketing & e-NAM

Model introductions

Despite India being the 7th-largest exporter of agricultural produce, with annual shipments of about ₹5 lakh crore, its domestic marketing ecosystem remains highly fragmented.
— Option 1 (both figures cited by the Union agriculture minister)
India has moved from food deficit to food surplus, but fragmented marketing infrastructure still causes post-harvest losses of 15–25%.
— Option 2

Context

  • e-NAM completed 10 years.
  • Price crashes and farmer protests (e.g., onions); ad hoc export bans (rice, sugar); trade-deal pressure to open agriculture and dairy to US products.

Frameworks (draw)

  • Marketing functions: producer → [exchange (buying/selling) + physical (storage, processing) + facilitating (standardisation, grading, price discovery)] → consumer.
  • Regulation at three levels: markets (APMC yards), functions (price discovery, grading, payment), functionaries (commission agents, traders).

e-NAM

  • A pan-India electronic trading portal (2016) creating a unified national market; APMCs remain the backend.
  • Benefits: better price discovery, transparency, standardised quality assessment, unified operations (single licence) — more buyers → more competition → better prices.
  • Centre funds APMC hardware and software — toward "One Nation, One Market" (like GST for goods).
  • Gramin Agricultural Markets (GrAMs) — rural haats as direct marketing points.
  • Negotiable warehouse receipts — store, pledge for credit, sell when prices improve.

Data

FICCI (as cited): 67–82% of fruit is sold through intermediaries.
Post-harvest losses ~₹90,000 crore (NITI); fruits and vegetables 30–35%; only ~10% of output processed; cold-chain capacity meets <15% of need.
Exports ~₹5 lakh crore, with West Asia ~20% — concentration risk.
Quick commerce/e-commerce growing 30%+.
e-NAM (PIB, March 2026): 1,600+ mandis in 23 states/UTs; ~1.8 crore farmers; ~4,724 FPOs registered; rising trade value.
Economic Survey 2023-24 (as cited): ~54% of farmers preferred e-NAM; higher prices and lower transaction costs; 80%+ in Gujarat and Telangana reported better prices.

Benefits

  • Price discovery; lower frictional costs (delays, inefficiency, mandi fees); scale and trade volume; integration of FPOs and traders.

6. Topic 4: Challenges, models, policy framework

Challenges

  • Weak value-chain links to organised retail, exports and branding.
  • Post-harvest losses → distress sales.
  • Crop bias — production-driven, not market-led; lack of a crop-neutral agricultural policy.
  • Middleman exploitation; policy ad hocism — sudden stock limits under the Essential Commodities Act deter warehouse investment → price volatility and discounted sales.
  • Fragmented smallholdings; export concentration; non-tariff barriers (SPS standards).
  • Lack of consensus — agriculture is a state subject; the repealed farm laws show the cost.

Views

  • A policymaker: "Grow global farming is not a slogan; it is a strategy for prosperity."
  • NITI document: the next productivity leap will come from "intelligence per acre", not more inputs — from input-intensive to knowledge-intensive farming.
  • A scholar: India's horticulture "has come of age" but is held back by fragmented supply chains, brand invisibility and limited consumer trust.
  • An economist (name unclear): an optimal export tax to raise revenue without crashing global prices.

Models

  • Sahyadri Farms (Maharashtra) — an FPO with ~50,000 members, now a major branded player.
  • Kala Namak rice (UP) — GI tag + FPOs + QR-code traceability + a dedicated export development foundation: legal identity, scale, trust and institutional support together.
  • A private agritech platform combining physical and digital market infrastructure.
  • Netherlands — small, yet the world's second-largest agri-food exporter via high-value, tech-driven, climate-resilient glasshouse farming.
  • Peru's quinoa — farmers rode a global health-food trend; India could do the same with millets.
  • A state platform for MSP purchase through a digital channel, reducing mandi congestion and risk.
  • Bharat-VISTAAR (as cited) — AI-driven multilingual advisory and market-access platform; the Digital Agriculture Mission (DPI for agriculture); the Agriculture Infrastructure Fund for post-harvest infrastructure.

Recommendations

  • National Commission on Farmers (Swaminathan) — a market within ~5 km of farms.
  • More FPO integration; incremental liberalisation (e.g., free fruits and vegetables from APMC rules first).

Draft National Policy Framework on Agricultural Marketing (2024)

  • An empowered committee of state agriculture marketing ministers on the GST-council model, to build consensus toward a unified market (single licence, single fee).
  • An "ease of doing agri-trade" index ranking states on market modernisation.
  • A price insurance scheme (like PMFBY) assuring farmers a known income.
  • Critics (farm groups, Punjab) say it echoes the repealed farm laws.
Transforming agriculture from volume to value is one of the most critical imperatives for rural prosperity and macroeconomic stability.
— Suggested conclusion
Public intervention in agricultural marketing must gradually evolve from physical procurement toward enabling markets. Critically analyse, highlighting the role of digital platforms in this transition.
— Practice question (homework)
