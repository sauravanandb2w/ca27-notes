0. Answer review: unpaid domestic work (from Class 8)

  1. Open with Shishu Pal v. Surjeet — ₹30,000 notional income in motor-accident claims, recognising unpaid care work.
  2. Judicial architecture: that case plus the Madras High Court's "24-hour unpaid work is not less than an 8-hour paid job".
  3. Statistical architecture gap: care work counted as reproductive, not productive; scholars value it at roughly 15–17% of GDP; the Census classes homemakers as non-workers.
  4. Financial architecture gap: excluded from labour-force data; care treated as charity; no agreed valuation method (opportunity cost vs. replacement cost).
  5. Implicit demand: greedy jobs, women pushed into gig work.
  6. Diagram: care work seen as unproductive → excluded from GDP → women's financial exclusion → feminisation of poverty. Conclude with the way forward and a quote.

1. Topic 1: MGNREGA vs. VB-G RAM G — comparison

Model introduction

MGNREGA served as the foundational social-protection framework in rural India for nearly two decades. A changing rural economy necessitated a comprehensive legislative reset — the Viksit Bharat – Guarantee for Rozgar and Ajeevika Mission (Gramin) Act, 2025 (VB-G RAM G), with nationwide rollout from 1 July 2026.

Context

  • Several states (the lecture names Kerala, Telangana and Jharkhand among others) passed resolutions opposing the new Act — federal friction.

Constitutional dimension

  • Article 41 (right to work) — MGNREGA (2005) was its explicit operationalisation.
  • Article 43 — living wage and decent conditions of work.
  • Article 21 — right to livelihood (Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation, 1985). The instructor calls this a case every Mains candidate must know.

Matthew effect: those who start with an initial advantage (resources, capital, access) accumulate more advantages over time. Under demand-driven MGNREGA, states with better governance and awareness generated more demand and captured more funds; poorer states with weak capacity lost out. Normative allocation aims to correct this — like Finance Commission devolution.

2. Topic 1: Debates, data, model answer

Debates

Continuous employment vs. farm-labour availability
MGNREGA gave workers a fallback, raising their bargaining power and wages — good for labour, but small and marginal farmers (most of Indian agriculture) found cultivation unviable as wages rose. The 60-day pause makes labour available but removes the worker's choice during that window: demand for farm work no longer has to compete with MGNREGA, so wages can fall and exploitation — even neo-bondage — becomes possible. The pause also has a gender dimension.
Open-ended guarantee vs. normative allocation
MGNREGA: villages demand work → states demand funds → the Centre is legally bound to pay. VB-G RAM G: allocation by objective parameters. Gains predictability and equity; loses responsiveness to actual need — a right becomes a ration.
Centralisation vs. decentralisation
The Centre controls allocation but states must contribute 40%. A sociologist (name unclear in captions) calls this an unfunded mandate — states must fund compliance with a central mandate they did not design and may not afford.

Data

Economic Survey: person-days under MGNREGA fell about 53% from the FY21 peak (COVID lockdown and reverse migration) to FY26 — showing the scheme responded when needed. Rural unemployment fell from 3.3% (FY21) to 2.5% (FY24), suggesting more non-farm rural jobs.
Revised estimate for MGNREGA in FY 2025-26: about ₹88,000 crore. In 2024-25, about 5.78 crore households worked under the scheme.
Jean Drèze: MGNREGA's budget is barely ~0.25% of GDP.
World Bank (Spring 2025 Poverty and Equity Brief): India lifted about 171 million people out of extreme poverty over the last decade.

Why a new scheme

  • Falling dependence on MGNREGA — the model must be redesigned for new needs.
  • Implementation gaps: work not done on the ground; spending not matching physical progress; use of machines instead of unskilled labour; bypassing digital attendance; fake muster rolls.

Advantages

  • 125 days; equity through normative allocation; 9% administrative capacity; asset creation via PM Gati Shakti; DBT payments to curb leakage; removal of the disentitlement clause (earlier, a worker who applied but didn't turn up lost unemployment allowance).

Challenges

  • Shift from right to rationing; 40% state share as an unfunded mandate.
  • Loss of bargaining power and gender divide from the 60-day pause.
  • Wage suppression — the Centre isn't required to pay a state's minimum wage, which can deter participation (especially in high-minimum-wage states).
  • Digital divide — attendance and payment failures for those without connectivity or awareness.

Best practice and way forward

  • A Maharashtra initiative (name unclear in captions) helped farmers shift from traditional crops to fruit crops, reportedly raising incomes about tenfold — rural wealth generation beyond "dig a well, fill a well" works.
  • GPS-enabled work tracking and real-time monitoring; use the 9% admin budget for professional support; address stagnating rural wages.
Public debate on public policy is essential in a democracy; laws that shape livelihoods deserve the closest scrutiny.
— Opening line suggested in class (reusable for any welfare-law question)
The transition from MGNREGA to the VB-G RAM G Act 2025 reflects a paradigm shift in rural employment generation. Critically examine in light of the structural weaknesses of the previous scheme.
— Practice question
  1. Quote → MGNREGA as the Article 41 right-to-work safety net; don't call it a failure — it helped reduce poverty.
  2. The 53% demand decline → case for reform; key new features as a 1-2-3 schematic.
  3. Structural weaknesses: "illusion of asset creation", ghost workers, labour-market distortion, delayed wages, Matthew effect (define it in one line).
  4. How the new Act addresses them; then concerns.
  5. Diagram — the rural distress cycle: lack of work → distress migration → poverty → no revival of rural economy → lack of work. Rural employment schemes aim to reverse it.

3. Topic 2: Skill development — framework & data

Model introductions

India's trajectory toward Viksit Bharat 2047 is contingent on its ability to harness its demographic dividend through effective human capital formation.
— Option 1
Skilling initiatives are no longer just welfare measures; they are critical economic infrastructure bridging educational attainment and industrial requirements.
— Option 2

Context

  • NITI Aayog's 11th Governing Council meeting, chaired by the PM, emphasised building a skilled workforce aligned with long-term growth. The PM urged states to turn the demographic dividend into a development dividend (best used as a conclusion).
  • Economic Survey 2025-26: shift from supply-driven to industry-driven skilling.

Definition — broadened

Skill development has traditionally meant occupation-specific vocational and technical training. In the AI era it must also include cognitive and socio-emotional capabilities — improving employability, productivity and adaptability.

Life-cycle approach (Economic Survey) — draw as a four-step flow

  1. Early vocational education in school — career exposure.
  2. School-to-work transition — career guidance, industry partnerships.
  3. Apprenticeship — on-the-job training (e.g., PM Internship Scheme).
  4. Lifelong adult reskilling.

Framework

  • Article 41 (right to work); Article 46 (educational and economic interests of weaker sections).
  • Apprentices Act — establishments engage apprentices in a band of their workforce (lecture: ~2.5%).
  • NEP 2020 — integrate vocational learning into general education.
  • National Credit Framework (NCrF) — accumulate and transfer credits across academic and vocational routes; works with the Academic Bank of Credits to enable multiple entry/exit (certificate after 1 year, diploma after 2, degree after 3).

Data

Economic Survey: only ~4.9% of youth (15–29) received formal vocational training; ~21% received informal training — hence the need for Recognition of Prior Learning. Formal training is concentrated in IT/ITeS, not manufacturing.
Economic Survey 2023-24: only ~51% of youth deemed employable; India needs ~78 lakh non-farm jobs a year until 2030.
WEF Future of Jobs Report 2025: ~63% of Indian workers will need reskilling or upskilling by 2030.
India Skills Report (as cited): about two-thirds of unemployed youth aged 20–29 are graduates — many preparing for competitive exams (voluntary unemployment).
Median age ~28; demographic window closes around 2040.

Why skilling matters

  • Harness the demographic dividend; navigate Industry 4.0 and the green transition.
  • Bridge the job–skill mismatch and the graduate unemployment paradox.
  • Social mobility for marginalised groups via certified training and Recognition of Prior Learning.
  • Manufacturing competitiveness and the Lewisian structural transformation out of agriculture; the servicification of manufacturing (design, R&D, customer service).
  • Wage premium — studies find higher returns to vocational than general education at school level.

4. Topic 2: Debates, challenges, models

Debates

DebateMeaning
Skill shortage vs. skill gapVacancies unfilled for lack of qualified people vs. people with qualifications but without actual competence.
Throughput vs. outcomesCounting certificates issued vs. long-term job placement and retention.
Human capital vs. human capabilityThe person as an instrument of economic output vs. Amartya Sen's view that skilling should expand freedom, agency and the ability to lead a valued life.
Academic vs. vocational trackFamily preference for degrees; vocational seen as a last resort.
White-collar vs. blue-collarAI automates managerial and mid-level work; firms cut "bench" strength; blue-collar and gig demand rises.
Human capital arbitrage vs. sovereign innovationProfiting from wage differentials keeps India the "back office of the world"; Industry 4.0 demands home-grown frontier innovation and high skills.

Challenges

  1. "Last resort" social coding — stigma and low prestige of vocational training.
  2. Dead-end track — no vertical mobility back to academic routes.
  3. Ghost centres and assessment fraud — eroding employer trust.
  4. Neo-bondage risk — skilling for low-level automated work traps youth in exploitative labour (e.g., Tamil Nadu's Sumangali scheme in textile mills).
  5. Outdated curricula and equipment in ITIs; ultra-short "marathon" courses.
  6. Fragmentation across ministries; spatial mismatch between trained people and jobs.
  7. Weak foundational literacy and numeracy; teacher/trainer deficits; poor rural access; digital divide.
  8. Wollstonecraft's dilemma — equality through sameness (treat men and women alike) vs. through difference (maternity, care support), the latter risking a "care penalty" in hiring.

Views

  • Economic Survey 2025-26: the central challenge is not the absence of training but its weak translation into durable labour-market value.
  • Günther Schmid (transitional labour markets): shift policy from "making work pay" to "making transitions pay" — support people between jobs (allowances, reskilling, earn-and-learn) since job changes will be the new normal.

Best practices and schemes

  • Odisha (praised in the Economic Survey) — revived under-used ITIs with campaigns showcasing successful alumni, including women and entrepreneurs (the lecture calls it a "10-6-4-2" campaign).
  • Maharashtra skill voucher programme — choose your own course.
  • Germany/Switzerland dual system — classroom theory plus workplace apprenticeship.
  • Singapore SkillsFuture Credit — every adult gets credits for lifelong learning.
  • PM Internship Scheme — 12-month internships in top companies; ₹5,000 monthly stipend plus a one-time ₹6,000 grant.
  • Skill India Digital Hub — verification and job matching.
  • A committee proposal (name unclear) for a reimbursable industry contribution: firms pay ~2% of their wage bill into a fund and draw it back when they train apprentices.
  • Skill impact bonds: an investor (possibly CSR money) funds a training provider; an independent verifier checks placement outcomes; the government repays only for verified outcomes — outcome-based, like programmatic funding in Class 7.
Examine the structural barriers to integrating vocational education into mainstream schooling. How can the earn-and-learn model envisaged under the National Credit Framework facilitate the school-to-work transition?

5. Topic 3: Exam integrity — NEET & the Public Examinations Act

Model introduction

For students, examinations are the definitive gateway to socio-economic mobility; recent crises around NEET-UG and CBSE's on-screen marking point to a credibility crisis.

Context

  • NEET-UG 2026 (held 3 May 2026) was cancelled over paper-leak allegations, with a CBI probe and a re-exam; about 22–23 lakh candidates were affected.
  • CBSE's move to digital on-screen marking (OSM) for Class 12 — scanned answer sheets evaluated on screen — and its glitches.

Three vulnerabilities (LTP)

  • Logistical — paper setting, printing, transport.
  • Technological — software glitches, cyber-security, OSM failures.
  • Procurement — contracts to the lowest bidder (L1) rather than a capable vendor (link to Class 6's L1 bias).

Debates

  • Centralised vs. decentralised testing — ~23 lakh candidates for ~1.29 lakh MBBS seats in one exam: the "centralisation paradox" (a single point of failure means total failure). China mixes central and provincial roles.
  • Pen-and-paper vs. computer-based — NEET is still pen-and-paper; JEE is CBT. CBT needs infrastructure and multiple shifts (hence normalisation), and brings cyber risks.
  • Cost vs. capability in procurement.
  • Digital reform vs. preparedness — pilot first, then scale.
  • The government's reported nationwide Telegram ban after leaks spread on it — critics invoke the doctrine of proportionality.
  • Cobra effect — well-meant policy producing perverse results (the colonial bounty on cobras led people to breed them); here, crackdowns can strengthen the coaching and leak industry.

Public Examinations (Prevention of Unfair Means) Act, 2024

  • Wide definition of unfair means: paper leaks, unauthorised access, helping candidates, providing solutions, tampering with systems, fake exams and fake websites.
  • Investigation by an officer not below DSP/ACP; the Centre can transfer cases to a central agency.
  • Offences are cognisable and non-bailable; cheating attracts 3–5 years plus fine; organised crime 5–10 years and a fine of at least ₹1 crore.
  • A national technical committee on public examinations.

Data

NTA (est. 2018) has conducted 250+ exams with 6+ crore registrations (press reports).
NEET: ~23 lakh candidates for ~1.29 lakh MBBS seats.
Bihar EOU: leak mafias charge ₹30–50 lakh per candidate (as cited).
Indian Express investigation (as cited): only 2 convictions in 45 paper-leak cases over two decades.

Why it matters and challenges

  • Student welfare and mental health; institutional credibility (NTA, CBSE); deterring organised "solver gangs" operating via social media; data security in CBT; fair evaluation.
  • Challenges: digital infrastructure deficit; untrained invigilators; weak cyber hygiene on government portals; procurement traps; lack of institutional accountability (blaming "external elements"); the demand–supply mismatch fuelling desperation; equity — coaching dependence and "dummy schools"; massification of higher education without quality.

Committees and models

  • K. Radhakrishnan Committee (2024), set up after the 2024 NEET controversy: shift to computer-based testing; consider a two-stage exam; cap attempts; stronger cyber-security, audits and CCTV; collaborate with states on an Election Commission-style model; expand test centres (e.g., use Kendriya Vidyalayas). Critics note it did not propose a compensation mechanism or an independent regulator.
  • A former education secretary (lecture: "Subrahmanyam") proposed a 40-40-20 model: 40% academic record, 40% social/professional aptitude, 20% entrance exam.
  • China's gaokao: huge scale but strict safeguards — isolation of paper setters, pen-and-paper with technological safeguards, geographic decentralisation (students test in their home province).
  • China's "double reduction" policy (2021): cut homework burden and private tutoring.
Recurring breaches in centralised examinations like NEET expose deficits in India's institutional capacity. Critically analyse the systemic issues within the NTA and evaluate the Radhakrishnan Committee's recommendations.

6. Topic 4: Viksit Bharat Shiksha Adhishthan Bill 2025

Model introduction

The VBSA Bill is a significant structural overhaul of higher-education governance, seeking to dismantle the existing siloed regulatory architecture in favour of a single umbrella body. For a direct question on a law, open with the law and its objective — a 10-marker has no room for a loose intro.

Status

Introduced in the Lok Sabha in December 2025 and referred to a Joint Parliamentary Committee.

Structure

  • An apex Viksit Bharat Shiksha Adhishthan subsuming UGC, AICTE and NCTE.
  • Three councils: a regulatory council (Viniyaman Parishad — supervision), a standards council (Manak Parishad — academic benchmarks) and an accreditation council (Gunvatta Parishad — grading via third-party assessors).
  • Philosophy (from NEP): "light but tight" regulation.
  • Commission: a chair and 12 members appointed by the President (on the Centre's recommendation); council members via search-cum-selection committees.
  • Functions: strategic direction and modernisation; faceless, digital, single-window regulation replacing physical inspections; funding de-linked from the regulator (to be handled separately — details unclear).

Debates

  • Federalism vs. centralisation — a single Centre-appointed body over a Concurrent List subject.
  • Autonomy vs. bureaucratic control — "autonomy through self-disclosure" vs. "control through disclosure": a surveillance architecture with severe penal powers, including derecognition.
  • Public purpose vs. commercialisation — fear of state retreat, privatisation and massification; education debt pushing graduates into any available job (undermining human capability); risks to affirmative action; hyper-globalisation and private profiteering.

For and against

  • For: ends regulatory silos; removes inspection-raj corruption; attracts international talent; better rankings; institutions focus on teaching and research, not paperwork.
  • Against: federalism; autonomy of IITs/IIMs; penal powers; appeals go to the central government, not courts; funding ambiguity; medicine and law excluded (fragmentation persists); outsourced accreditation creates a conflict of interest (the institution pays its assessor); only symbolic state representation.

Views and way forward

  • V. Kamakoti (Director, IIT Madras): education is useless unless one learns how to live with society.
  • An IIM director (name unclear) praised the bill as enabling "Education 5.0" — a shift from technicisation to humanisation of education.
  • Way forward: shared governance with state higher-education councils; a separate Higher Education Grants Council; protect affirmative action; "consensual closure" (no institution closed without the state's consent); outcome-centric evaluation.
The VBSA Bill 2025 aims to address fragmentation in the higher education sector but risks undermining the federal structure. Examine.
