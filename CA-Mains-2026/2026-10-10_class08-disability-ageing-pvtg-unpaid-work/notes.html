1. Topic 1: Disability rights — framework & data

Model introduction

The discourse on disability is shifting from a charity-based medical model to a rights-based social model, cemented by the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (RPwD) Act, 2016.

Context pegs

  • Digital KYC — the Supreme Court directed revisions to KYC norms (liveness checks, blinking, QR scanning) that create impossible barriers for persons with disabilities, reading inclusive digital access into Article 21 (Pragya Prasun v. Union of India, 2025).
  • A wheelchair-using passenger was reportedly stopped from boarding for want of a "fit-to-fly" certificate — a lack of empathy in aviation practice.
  • Reservation for persons with disabilities in posts — an application of the "politics of presence".
  • The Supreme Court laid down guidelines against stereotyping persons with disabilities in films and on social media, after comedians' jokes about disability drew outrage.
Disabling humour vs. disability humour: disabling humour laughs at persons with disabilities and demeans them; disability humour uses humour to build awareness without hurting dignity.
Two stereotypes to avoid: (1) portraying persons with disabilities as less capable; (2) the "super-stereotype" — showing them with extraordinary powers (the blind hero who fights by sound), implying worth only through exceptional ability.

Definition and legal framework

  • Person with disability (RPwD Act): a person with long-term physical, mental, intellectual or sensory impairment which, in interaction with barriers, hinders full and effective participation in society. Benchmark disability: at least 40% of a specified disability, certified.
  • Fundamental rights (Articles 14, 15, 21) apply to all. Article 41 (DPSP) directs public assistance in case of disablement — a strong conclusion for any social-justice answer.
  • RPwD Act 2016: expanded categories from 7 (1995 Act) to 21, including acid-attack victims and mental illness; rights to equality, dignity and protection from abuse; accessible elections and public facilities; 5% reservation in government higher-education seats and 4% in government jobs; mandatory accessibility standards for transport and infrastructure.

Data

Census 2011: 2.68 crore persons with disabilities (~2.2% of population) — ~1.5 crore men and ~1.18 crore women; about 69% live in rural areas (intersectional compounding of disadvantage).
Survey estimates vary; the lecture cited a higher share from a recent survey (verify the source before quoting).
India's public spending on disability welfare is a tiny fraction of GDP compared with advanced economies (lecture's comparison — verify figures).
ILO/World Bank estimates: low- and middle-income countries lose roughly 3–7% of GDP by excluding persons with disabilities from education and employment.
Disability spending has a fiscal multiplier above 1 (lecture: ~1.4–1.6) and frees family caregivers to work.

Economic concepts

  • Earning handicap: reduced ability to earn income because of disability.
  • Conversion handicap (Amartya Sen): even at equal income, a disabled person must spend more (aids, care, transport) to reach the same standard of living — income doesn't convert into well-being equally.
  • Postcode lottery: benefits depend on which state/district you live in, given uneven schemes and infrastructure.
  • Link to SDG target 1.3 (social protection systems for all).

2. Topic 1: Disability — debates, judgments, way forward

Debates

Charity/medical model vs. rights-based social model
Medical model: disability is an individual problem needing a medical fix; persons with disabilities are objects of charity. Social model: the problem is social and physical barriers — remove them through accessibility and the person can fully participate.
Resource approach vs. capability approach
Resource approach measures advantage by income alone. Sen's capability approach measures a person's real freedom to achieve valued functionings — the right lens for disability.
Legal recognition vs. actual entitlement
The Act recognises 21 categories, but on the ground only five broad categories were identified for the 4% job reservation — leaving "invisible disabilities" recognised on paper but unsupported.
Inclusive schools vs. special schools
Rights groups favour inclusive schools; others argue some children need specialised schools. Segregation risks signalling that disabled children don't belong. Very few schools are disability-friendly, and some find ways to deny admission.

Other challenges

  • Lack of universal design in physical and digital infrastructure; piecemeal fixes; fragmented responsibility across agencies.
  • Fund under-utilisation — the lecture cites large unspent allocations under the scheme for implementing the RPwD Act (SIPDA) in a recent year (verify the figure).
  • Double marginalisation of women with disabilities; intersection with caste and rural location; political disenfranchisement.

Judgments and views

  • Reasonable accommodation is context-dependent; a one-size-fits-all 40% benchmark should not by itself bar a genuinely capable candidate (Supreme Court on disabled MBBS aspirants — Omkar Ramchandra Gond, 2024).
  • Pragya Prasun (2025): inclusive and meaningful digital access is part of the right to life.
  • Disability-rights advocates criticise portrayals of persons with disabilities as objects of pity or burdens on society.

Best practices and schemes

  • Employment quotas abroad: private companies above a size threshold must employ 2–5% persons with disabilities (cited as a global practice — country not clear in captions).
  • A single national disability authority to implement the Act.
  • Sugamya Bharat Abhiyan (Accessible India Campaign); UDID portal for online disability certification; DDRS (Deendayal Disabled Rehabilitation Scheme) funding NGOs that run special schools and rehabilitation.
  • Courts have criticised the slow rollout of District Disability Rehabilitation Centres.

Way forward

  • "Nothing about us without us" — persons with disabilities must participate in framing policy (politics of presence again).
  • A minimum universal disability pension floor.
The shift from a medical model to a social model of disability remains incomplete without financial equity. Examine in light of the implementation of the RPwD Act.

3. Topic 2: Ageing population — framework & data

Model introduction

India is on the cusp of an ageing transition — a growing elderly population driven by falling fertility (TFR below 2) and rising life expectancy (above 70).

Context pegs

  • A state announced the creation of a dedicated department for the elderly (state not clear in captions).
  • Andhra Pradesh introduced cash incentives for couples to have a second child, citing rapid ageing.

Legal framework

  • Article 41 — public assistance in old age.
  • Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007: obligation of children and relatives to maintain parents/seniors; power to revoke property transfers if the senior is neglected; penal provisions for abandonment; old-age homes.

Key terms

  • Senior citizen: aged 60 and above.
  • Ageing transition: the shift caused by declining fertility and rising life expectancy.
  • Silver economy: economic opportunities, products and services designed for the elderly.
  • Feminisation of ageing: women form a rising share of the elderly because they live longer — and face widowhood, poverty and lack of assets.
  • Longevity dividend: economic and social gains when longer-lived elderly remain healthy and productive.
  • Ageing in place: the ability to age in one's own home and community — dignity, social bonds, and in line with Indian values of filial obligation; it also offsets India's weak care infrastructure.

Data

India Ageing Report 2023 (UNFPA/IIPS): the elderly share is projected to rise from ~10.5% (2022) to ~20.8% by 2050; the 80+ population is projected to grow by ~279% between 2022 and 2050.
LASI (Longitudinal Ageing Study in India) — a survey that tracks the same people over time: about 75% of the elderly have at least one chronic disease; a large share live without regular income; around 70% live in rural areas.
About half of men over 60 continue to work, compared with roughly a fifth of women.
Ageing varies sharply by state — southern states are much older than northern ones.

4. Topic 2: Ageing — debates & way forward

  • Demographic window: India must "get rich before getting old". Advanced economies became rich (industrialised) before ageing; India's window — roughly until the 2040s — is limited, so skilling and jobs for the current working-age population are urgent.
  • Family vs. market vs. state care: family care rests on kinship obligation and emotional bonds; market care on contracts and professionalism; state care on taxation and entitlements. Changing family structures (joint → nuclear, migration, urbanisation) weaken family care, and loneliness is rising.
  • Pension burden vs. youth employment: extending working life and pensions can crowd out jobs and fiscal space for the young.
  • Gerontocracy vs. youth representation: ageing leadership may fail to represent youth priorities.
  • Cash vs. care infrastructure: pensions are too small to cover old-age health costs; should the state fund care homes or monthly transfers?
  • Hollowed-out villages: rural areas populated mostly by the elderly after youth migrate.
An ageing society is more set on preservation than renovation.
— Cited in the lecture (attribution unclear in captions — verify)

Judgments, models, schemes

  • Courts have held children legally liable to maintain their parents.
  • Bolivia's constitution guarantees rights of the elderly; Japan focuses on active ageing.
  • IGNOAPS (old-age pension); Rashtriya Vayoshri Yojana (assistive living devices); SAGE (Seniorcare Ageing Growth Engine — supports start-ups offering elder-care products); SACRED portal (connects senior citizens seeking work with private employers).
  • Policies must be gender-sensitive, not gender-neutral — recognising women's lifelong unpaid care work, which leaves them without human capital or financial assets in old age (a "longevity penalty").
Ageing in India is not gender neutral. Examine the socio-economic challenges of this and the policy measures required.

5. Topic 3: PVTGs & the Great Nicobar Project

Model introduction

Tribal communities are not a monolith but groups at varying stages of socio-economic development; at the very bottom of this spectrum lie the Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups (PVTGs).

Context pegs

  • The Great Nicobar Island project (~₹72,000 crore: transshipment port, airport, township, power plant) — reports that around 84 sq km of notified tribal reserve would be denotified, affecting the Shompen and Nicobarese.
  • Pastoral communities such as the Van Gujjars protesting dispossession of livelihoods.

Framework

  • Primitive tribal groups were identified as a separate category in 1973 (following the Dhebar Commission's earlier work) and renamed PVTGs in 2006.
  • Criteria: pre-agricultural level of technology, low literacy, economic backwardness, and stagnant or declining population.
  • Article 46 (promotion of educational and economic interests of weaker sections); Forest Rights Act, Section 3(1)(e) — habitat rights for PVTGs; the Andaman and Nicobar (Protection of Aboriginal Tribes) Regulation, 1956 — protection through exclusion of outsiders.
75 PVTGs in 18 states and 1 UT, out of 700+ Scheduled Tribes; total population about 45 lakh. The Sentinelese and some Andamanese groups number only in the tens or hundreds.
Supreme Court (2002) ordered closure of the Andaman Trunk Road through the Jarawa reserve — an order the administration has only partially implemented.

Debates

Assimilation vs. integration vs. isolation
Assimilation: the minority adopts dominant norms. Integration: participation while retaining a distinct identity (Nehru's Tribal Panchsheel). Isolation: Verrier Elwin's "national park" approach — no contact. The Sentinelese live in effective isolation.
Imposed development vs. self-determination
Should mainstream development (roads, infrastructure) be imposed, or should PVTGs choose their own path — the "right to be left alone"? The core problem is free, prior and informed consent.
Development vs. protection (Great Nicobar)
Great Nicobar overlooks the Malacca Strait chokepoint (China's "Malacca dilemma"); a deep-water port and air facilities strengthen India's strategic position. But development causes displacement, ecological loss and cultural erosion.
Fortress vs. inclusive conservation
Fortress conservation removes people to protect nature; inclusive conservation treats communities as stewards through participatory, rights-based management (e.g., community forest management).

Challenges

  • Loss of livelihood, land and traditional knowledge; inability to survive a market economy.
  • Complicity and neglect — welfare fails to reach the last mile.
  • Officials not respecting customary relationships; outsiders entering reserves; disease exposure.
  • Vulnerability to environmental shocks and disasters.
  • Education that ignores community context, eroding culture.
  • Demonisation — indigenous people portrayed as enemies of nature; human–wildlife conflict where state action follows animals, not people.

Way forward and schemes

  • A no-displacement policy for large infrastructure; implement the order on the Andaman Trunk Road.
  • Campaigns to provide basic documents (Aadhaar, bank accounts) to all tribal citizens.
  • PM-JANMAN (2023) — targeted mission for PVTG habitations (housing, roads, health, nutrition).
  • Community-based crèches and feeding centres for maternal and child health; incentives to delay marriage.
Discuss the various concerns surrounding the Great Nicobar project. How can the state balance strategic imperatives with the rights of PVTGs?

6. Topic 4: Unpaid domestic work & the care economy

Model introduction

The reality of women's labour is captured by a painful paradox: women work all the time, yet their work remains invisible.

Context — judicial valuation

  • Shishu Pal v. Surjeet (Supreme Court, June 2026): fixed ₹30,000 a month as the notional income of a homemaker in motor-accident compensation, and created a distinct compensation head, "loss of domestic care". Compensation was raised from about ₹8.4 lakh to ₹62.8 lakh.
  • Courts have held that a homemaker's contribution cannot be dismissed in maintenance proceedings merely because she had no formal income — recognising a "marriage penalty".
  • The Madras High Court (2023) ruled that a wife who toils as a homemaker is entitled to an equal share in property acquired by the husband, since her 24-hour uncompensated work at home cannot rank below his 8-hour paid job.
  • Earlier: Kirti v. Oriental Insurance (2021) on notional income for homemakers.

Terms

  • Unpaid work: activities performed without payment but vital for social reproduction.
  • Care economy: paid and unpaid care work together.
  • Statistical invisibility: contribution exists but isn't counted — the Census classes people doing only household duties as "non-workers", alongside groups like dependants and prisoners.

Framework and data

Articles 14, 15(3), 39 (equal pay, livelihood). Maternity Benefit (Amendment) Act, 2017: 26 weeks of paid maternity leave.
Time Use Survey (NSO 2024): women spend about 289 minutes a day on unpaid domestic services vs. about 88 minutes for men.
Estimates value women's unpaid care work at a substantial share of GDP (figures vary widely by method — verify the source).
Globally, women perform about 76% of unpaid care work (ILO).
Marriage reduces female labour-force participation by several percentage points (as cited).

Debates

Wages for housework vs. matrimonial property rights
Should husbands pay a monthly salary, or should wives get an equal share of marital property? Salaries risk making the wife an employee bound to the husband's wishes. Compensation after death does nothing for her agency while alive.
"Tradwife" glorification — choice vs. confinement
Social-media glorification of traditional housewives is framed as free choice, but critics see it as a performance of class status that can sanctify women's confinement to the home and hide invisible labour.
Productive vs. reproductive work
Economics counts only market activity as productive; feminists argue reproductive and care work (child-rearing, emotional labour) is what makes society function — yet it is statistically invisible.
Gendered vs. gender-neutral valuation
Valuing only women's work excludes male caregivers doing unpaid care; valuation should apply to whoever performs the work.

Why value unpaid work

  • End statistical invisibility; tackle the feminisation of poverty.
  • Close the insurance gap — better coverage for homemakers.
  • Elevate status and agency; strengthen claims for fair maintenance and property.
  • Care-economy gaps hurt children (learning, nutrition, schooling, child marriage) and mothers (stress, poor health, career exits).

Constraints

  • Paying for housework may entrench gender roles — turning an unjust division of labour into a permanent job.
  • Payment within the household doesn't guarantee control over money.
  • "Greedy jobs" (Claudia Goldin) demand constant availability, pushing one partner — usually the woman — out of paid work.

Frameworks and models

  • 3R framework (Diane Elson): Recognise the value of unpaid work, Reduce drudgery (technology, infrastructure), Redistribute it between men and women and between family and state.
  • A scholar (name unclear) on intimate labour: care work involving affection and responsibility is paradoxically stripped of financial compensation, leading to female impoverishment.
  • Couple equity (Claudia Goldin): gender equality in the labour market needs equality within couples.
  • Sweden: generous paid parental leave (from 1974) and universal public childcare lifted female labour-force participation above 80% by around 1990.
The Supreme Court has named the homemaker a nation builder, but the financial and statistical architecture that surrounds her is yet to respond. Analyse in the context of the judicial valuation of unpaid domestic work.

(B) 4% and 5%
(C) 5% and 4%

Which Directive Principle provides for public assistance in cases of unemployment, old age, sickness and disablement?
(A) Article 39A
(B) Article 41
(C) Article 43
(D) Article 47
Answer: (B). Article 41.
Q4
Which is NOT one of the criteria for identifying a PVTG?
(A) Pre-agricultural level of technology
(B) Low level of literacy
(C) Stagnant or declining population
(D) Residence in a Sixth Schedule area
Answer: (D). The four criteria are pre-agricultural technology, low literacy, economic backwardness and stagnant/declining population.
Q5
Habitat rights for PVTGs are specifically provided under:
(A) PESA, 1996
(B) Forest Rights Act, 2006
(C) Wildlife Protection Act, 1972
(D) Biological Diversity Act, 2002
Answer: (B). Section 3(1)(e) of the FRA.
Q6
The Shompen tribe, affected by the Great Nicobar project, belongs to which group?
(A) A PVTG of the Nicobar Islands
(B) A pastoral community of Uttarakhand
(C) A Scheduled Tribe of Lakshadweep
(D) A denotified tribe of Rajasthan
Answer: (A). The Shompen are a PVTG of Great Nicobar.
Q7
In 2026 the Supreme Court fixed the notional monthly income of a homemaker for motor-accident compensation at:
(A) ₹10,000
(B) ₹20,000
(C) ₹30,000
(D) ₹50,000
Answer: (C). Shishu Pal v. Surjeet (June 2026), with a new head, "loss of domestic care".
Q8
The "3R framework" for unpaid care work stands for:
(A) Record, Report, Review
(B) Recognise, Reduce, Redistribute
(C) Reward, Regulate, Reform
