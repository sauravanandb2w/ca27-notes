1. Topic 1: Parliamentary privileges — framework

Model introduction

Parliamentary privileges form the bedrock of a robust, independent and functional democracy.

Context pegs

  • Expunction of opposition leaders' speeches by the Speaker — free speech of legislators vs. procedural rules; allegations of microphones being switched off.
  • Sita Soren v. Union of India (2024) — a seven-judge bench overruled the 1998 JMM bribery case judgment.
  • "Cash for query" allegations and an ethics committee inquiry into an MP.

Definition

Parliamentary privileges: special rights, immunities and exemptions enjoyed by the Houses and their members — "special" because ordinary citizens don't have them, "privileges" because only legislators enjoy them. The purpose is not to create an elite class but to ensure free speech and independent functioning on the floor of the House.

Draw a box: collective privileges (enjoyed by the House) and individual privileges (enjoyed by members — e.g., freedom from arrest in civil cases during and 40 days before/after a session).

Constitutional and legal framework

  • Articles 105 (Parliament) and 194 (state legislatures) — freedom of speech in the House; immunity from court proceedings for anything said or any vote given (105(2)); other privileges as enjoyed before the 44th Amendment's commencement (1979), i.e., effectively British House of Commons conventions (105(3)) — still uncodified.
  • Articles 122 and 212 — courts cannot inquire into the validity of legislative proceedings on grounds of procedural irregularity.
  • Article 361A — protects publication of substantially true reports of legislative proceedings (press freedom).
  • Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 — MPs and MLAs are public servants.

Lok Sabha rules to cite as examples

RuleSubject
380Expunction of words from the record
352Restrictions on defamatory and unparliamentary words
353No defamatory or incriminating allegations without advance notice to the Speaker (used to stop an MP quoting a former army chief's unpublished memoir)
349Members may not read out certain external material

2. Topic 1: Debates, judgments, way forward

Debates aren't a heading in the answer — sort each debate into the positives or the concerns section depending on the question.

Debates

Constitutional privilege vs. procedural rules
Internal procedure (e.g., expunging whole sentences or passages under Rule 380) should not override the constitutional free speech of members under Article 105. Experts: the presiding officer's disciplinary power must not supersede an MP's fundamental right to free speech.
Legislative supremacy vs. judicial review
Courts are barred from inquiring into House proceedings, but committees are often dominated by the ruling party. Where there is substantive (not merely procedural) illegality, unconstitutionality or denial of natural justice, courts will intervene.
Codification vs. non-codification
Neither Parliament nor state legislatures have codified privileges. The Committee of Privileges (2008) advised against codification to preserve flexibility, but non-codification makes "breach of privilege" subjective and arbitrary.

Arguments for privileges

  • Free, frank and fearless expression — essential for genuine deliberation.
  • Protection from arbitrary arrest and political vendetta.
  • Institutional independence — contempt powers protect the dignity and authority of the House.
  • Summary disciplinary control — faster internal action than courts.
  • Independent voting without fear of legal consequences.

Concerns

  • Threat to fundamental rights of citizens and press freedom, given vague, boundaryless definitions (e.g., a state assembly issuing a privilege notice even against a judge who granted bail).
  • Weaponisation — disproportionate punitive action by committees; a chilling effect on journalists and critics; political censorship.
  • Unspeaking warrants — arrests for "breach of privilege" without specific reasons, so the accused can't defend; the House acts as both accuser and judge, against natural justice.
  • Pre-2024, a shield for corruption (below).

Judgments

CaseHolding
P.V. Narasimha Rao v. State (JMM bribery, 1998)Broad reading: MPs who took bribes and voted as agreed were immune; those who took bribes but didn't vote were not — a "bizarre legal paradox".
Sita Soren v. Union of India (2024, seven judges)Overruled 1998: bribery is not a legislative act; the offence is complete on accepting the bribe, irrespective of the vote. Laid down a two-fold test: the privilege must be tied to the collective functioning of the House and necessary to the legislator's essential duties (lecture framing: it must enhance the House's dignity and be a legitimate exercise of free speech).
Raja Ram Pal v. Speaker (2007)Judicial review of privilege actions is possible where there is substantive illegality or unconstitutionality.

Quotes

Only the free, frank and fearless expression of members' views enables the legislature to perform its role effectively.
— P.D.T. Achary, former Secretary-General, Lok Sabha (paraphrased)
Privileges are privileges of the people and for the people — not privileges against the people.
— Cited in the lecture (attribution unclear in captions)
While privilege is an exemption from the general law, it is strictly limited to what is absolutely necessary for the House to function.
— The principle in Erskine May, the authoritative text on British parliamentary practice (paraphrased) — usable as a conclusion

Recommendations and way forward

  • NCRWC — amend Article 105 to exclude corrupt acts from privilege.
  • Commonwealth Parliamentary Association — urged MPs to reduce deadlocks and raise political issues outside the House to preserve decorum.
  • Narrow expunction — only specific unparliamentary words, not passages.
  • Codify privileges; privileges committees to follow natural justice.
  • Tech protocols (e.g., two-factor authentication on MPs' portal access — after the cash-for-query case involved sharing login credentials).
Parliamentary privileges are designed to protect the independence of legislators. Examine in the context of the Supreme Court's 2024 verdict overruling the P.V. Narasimha Rao judgment.
— Practice question
  1. Definition; box with collective and individual privileges.
  2. How they protect independence: free speech, vote, immunity from arrest, institutional independence.
  3. Pre-2024 vs. post-2024: shield for corruption under Narasimha Rao → Sita Soren's "bribery is not a legislative necessity" and the two-fold test.
  4. Not to be a political weapon: expunctions, reported microphone switch-offs, arbitrary suspensions.
  5. Demand for codification; narrow expunction; Lok Sabha's tech controls; conclude with Erskine May's principle.

3. Topic 2: Fiscal federalism & the 16th Finance Commission

Context

The 16th Finance Commission's award period runs from 1 April 2026 to 31 March 2031; the government accepted its recommendations.

Constitutional framework

  • Article 280 — Finance Commission; recommends vertical (Centre–states) and horizontal (among states) devolution.
  • Article 275 — statutory grants-in-aid; Article 282 — discretionary grants.
Fiscal federalism: the study of financial relationships and responsibilities between levels of government — Centre, states and local bodies.

Three principles (N.K. Singh, 15th FC chair)

  • Fiscal equivalence — each public service has its own jurisdiction, and provision covers everyone who benefits (e.g., a city that supplies water also levies water charges).
  • Decentralisation — a service should be provided by the jurisdiction controlling the minimum geographic area that captures its benefits.
  • Subsidiarity — functions performed by the lowest competent authority.

Three imbalances (Vijay Kelkar)

  • Vertical — mismatch between taxation powers and spending responsibilities of the Centre and states.
  • Horizontal — unequal fiscal capacity among states.
  • Developmental — regional disparities in physical and social infrastructure (the "3/3 puzzle": the three richest states are about three times as wealthy as the three poorest).

  • Revenue-deficit grants discontinued — a move toward fiscal discipline (Article 275 statutory grants give way to more discretionary/conditional transfers).
  • Grants to local bodies: about ₹7.91 lakh crore (as cited).

4. Topic 2: Debates, issues, model answer

Debates

  • Equity vs. efficiency — for decades funds favoured poorer states to help them converge; the 16th FC tilts toward rewarding economic performance, answering complaints from Maharashtra, Gujarat and others that they were penalised.
  • North–south — southern states contribute disproportionately to central taxes yet receive less, and are penalised for better governance, human development and demographic indicators; northern states argue historical under-investment requires compensation.
  • Freebies vs. fiscal prudence.
  • Population size vs. demographic performance — both weights adjusted.
  • Revenue vs. expenditure — the Centre collects ~62% of revenue but spends ~36%; states collect ~38% and spend ~64% — the vertical imbalance.

Issues

  • The "41% illusion" — cesses and surcharges (~2.2% of GDP, ~₹9 lakh crore as cited) sit outside the divisible pool, so states' real share is well below 41%.
  • Statutory vs. discretionary grants — ending revenue-deficit grants and shifting to Article 282 transfers weakens guaranteed equity.
  • State Finance Commissions — the FC must base local-body grants on SFC reports, but SFCs are often not constituted or produce unusable data (lecture: only Chhattisgarh and Sikkim submitted usable reports in this cycle). The 16th FC suggested amending the Constitution to drop the SFC-report requirement — critics say this removes states' incentive to empower SFCs and goes against the constitutional mandate.

Views and suggestions

  • 16th FC's "grand bargain": fold cesses and surcharges into the divisible pool; states accept a smaller percentage of a larger pool, with no revenue loss to either side.
  • "Fiscal federalism endures only when efficiency disciplines equity" — a public-policy expert (as cited).
  • Counter-cyclical buffers — save during growth, deploy during slowdowns (especially important once revenue-deficit grants end).
  • More data-driven horizontal devolution.
The 16th Finance Commission has shifted the balance of fiscal federalism from equity to efficiency. Evaluate in light of its new horizontal devolution formula.
— Practice question
  1. Intro: award period, or Article 280, or fiscal federalism with N.K. Singh's three principles.
  2. A table of criteria marked equity/efficiency/need/opportunity cost (income distance — equity; population and area — need; demographic performance and GDP contribution — efficiency; forest — opportunity cost).
  3. Further shifts: revenue-deficit grants ended (prevents moral hazard, enforces discipline); move to discretionary transfers (bypasses Article 275).
  4. Counterpoints: the 41% illusion; SFC bypass; need-based support still exists — it's a balance, not a full swing.
  5. Diagram — virtuous cycle: fiscal discipline → investment in infrastructure → economic opportunity → more tax/non-tax revenue → better governance.

5. Topic 3: City governance & empowered mayors

Model introduction (from NITI Aayog's report)

Urbanisation will be central to Viksit Bharat and a $30 trillion economy by 2047: urban regions will anchor growth, drive innovation and create jobs. For this, cities must deliver reliable infrastructure, efficient services, predictable regulation and strong governance, and become aspirational cities that attract global talent.

Context

NITI Aayog's April 2026 report, Moving Towards Effective City Government: A Framework for Million-Plus Cities, proposes turning the mayor from a ceremonial head into an empowered executive. Mayoral elections in New York and London have also fuelled the debate.

Framework and present structure

  • 74th Amendment provides for municipal elections, chairpersons and five-year terms — but says nothing specific on mayors; municipal corporations are created by state laws.
  • Mayor heads the council, usually indirectly elected, with a short term (as little as one year in some cities) — largely ceremonial.
  • Standing committees handle day-to-day work.
  • Municipal commissioner — usually an IAS officer appointed by the state — is the real chief executive.
  • Ward committees for decentralisation.

Debates

  • Elected mayor vs. unelected bureaucrat — who can best drive city growth in a democracy?
  • Centralisation vs. decentralisation — states hold on to powers; decentralisation exists on paper but not in practice.

Five structural challenges (NITI Aayog)

  1. Limited devolution of functions — of the 18 functions in the 12th Schedule, cities fully control only about 4 on average ("powerless city government").
  2. Fragmented set-up and weak leadership — ceremonial mayors, executive power with bureaucrats, short terms → no long-term vision.
  3. Weak revenue base — chronic financial distress; can't invest independently in infrastructure.
  4. Fragmented service delivery — water, transport and waste handled by state-level parastatals; poor coordination, and citizens can't hold their councillors accountable.
  5. Inadequate human resources — few planners and finance experts; temporary consultants and deputations.

Data

India's ~46–47 million-plus municipal corporations hold ~30% of the urban population on ~10% of urban area, and contribute ~60% of national GDP.
AMRUT credit ratings (as cited): of 468 urban local bodies rated, only 162 were investment grade.
2017–2025: only ~25 municipal bond issues raising ~₹3,579 crore — while China issued 252 local government bonds worth ~$142 billion in August 2025 alone.

6. Topic 3: Reform agenda, models, challenges

NITI Aayog's reform agenda (four pillars)

  1. Empowered political leadership — directly elected mayors; fixed five-year term coterminous with the council; mayor legally designated executive head, with the municipal commissioner reporting to the mayor; sanctions against arbitrary dissolution and time-bound elections.
  2. Mayor-in-council — a cabinet-style system replacing standing committees: councillors hold portfolios (water, health, finance, transport); mayor appoints department heads; zonal committees for local differences.
  3. Unified service delivery — any agency delivering a public service in the city should work under the corporation, e.g., city-specific divisions of state parastatals under municipal control.
  4. Stronger municipal finances — SFCs set up at least two years before their award period; state action-taken reports on SFC recommendations; mandatory publication of budget allocations to cities. (Note the contrast with the 16th FC's proposal to bypass SFCs.)

Global and domestic models

  • New York — strong-mayor system. London — directly elected mayor controlling citywide transport and housing policy.
  • Brazil — among the most autonomous mayors, controlling local policing, education, healthcare and land use.
  • East Asian model — local governments as residual claimants who keep a share of the prosperity they generate (Indian cities generate ~60% of GDP but taxes flow to the Centre and states).
  • Kolkata — Subhas Chandra Bose (chief executive officer) and C.R. Das (mayor) show city government as a political pipeline.
  • Tamil Nadu, Himachal Pradesh and Rajasthan discontinued direct mayoral elections; Madhya Pradesh and Chhattisgarh restored them — inconsistent models.

Positives of empowered mayors

  • Direct accountability; effective city governance → virtuous cycle of investment, revenue and governance.
  • A political pipeline that prevents "political hollowing" — genuine local leaders emerge.
  • Incentivised prosperity (residual claimant logic).

Challenges

  • State reluctance; inconsistent state models; legislative stagnation (private members' bills for direct elections found no support); parastatal fragmentation; delayed elections.

Views — a three-way harm

One expert: the lack of effective, accountable local government "hurts citizens, throttles the economy and weakens the political system":

  • Hurts citizens — service-delivery failure → alienation → private substitutes (gated societies, private security), migration.
  • Throttles the economy — weak revenue → weak infrastructure and human capital → poor ease of doing business → economic underperformance.
  • Weakens politics — a patronage economy (services only through political intermediaries) → participation deficit → elite capture (contractor and crony capture).
The 74th Constitutional Amendment promised participatory urban democracy, but Indian cities are effectively run by unelected bureaucrats. Examine in the context of the demand for empowered mayors.
