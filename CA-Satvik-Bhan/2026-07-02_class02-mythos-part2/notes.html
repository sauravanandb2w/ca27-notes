Executive summary

  • AI sovereignty: sovereignty is no longer only borders — access to frontier AI tools now decides geopolitical might. The US government treated Mythos (and Fable) as national-security-sensitive and restricted access; India could not buy its way in.
  • Cyber-power inequality: haves (Global North, owners of frontier AI) vs have-nots (Global South, dependent on external vendors) — attacks at machine speed, patching at human speed.
  • AI cyber arms race (e.g. Qihoo 360) and hybrid/asymmetric warfare: non-state actors can skip expensive weapons and strike critical infrastructure and air-defence software.
  • Cost hits MSMEs hardest; they lack capability, trained staff, backups and incident-response plans.
  • Gaps: narrow vision · institutional (headless AI Safety Institute, no model-testing body, development-first approach, many agencies, little convergence) · human resource · legal/accountability · risk-classification · pre-release evaluation · R&D · outdated 2013 policy · poor patching (WannaCry, AIIMS) · weak sector coordination.
  • Steps taken: high-level inter-sectoral review (FinMin–MeitY–RBI–NPCI–CERT-In) · RBI advisories and board-level gap assessment · engagement with Project Glasswing · panel under SBI chairman · RBI AI framework (2025).
  • Way forward: start with a grand strategy; then policy/legal, sectoral, regulation/accountability, AI diplomacy, AI skilling, sovereign AI — conclusion line: "India must develop and indigenise sovereign AI capabilities."

Recap — how the Mythos notes are memorised 0:05 – 0:12

  • Lecturer's own recall method: remember a framework (heads), then the keywords he must say in class, then a few facts. The rest returns while writing — "your subconscious mind is awake in the exam".
  • Keywords he repeats: frontier AI, zero-day vulnerability, scale marker US$400 bn, and the comparison with other LLMs on four points: (1) finds software vulnerabilities with speed and scale (→ zero-day), (2) high autonomy, (3) very high situational awareness (new keyword added in this class), (4) low barrier (cite UK AI Safety Institute). Fact to carry: 10,000 vulnerabilities found.
  • Implications structure: Broad (dual use; cyber security) → Sectoral (five–six sectors) → Other (all the rest). "Other" is a safety valve: if you forget a sector, club two–three points there.
  • Implications and challenges mean the same to the examiner in a current-affairs topic like this — but practise them separately.

Master structure map

1. Implication 3 — Emergence of AI sovereignty 0:10 – 0:22

Third-level implication: international concept level (beyond cyber security and sectors).

  • Sovereignty was once defined by borders ("you cannot enter mine; I cannot enter yours"). That idea has become largely irrelevant: economic security now decides sovereignty (C. Raja Mohan and other experts).
  • Example: if China stops supply chains even while discussing de-escalation, disengagement and de-induction on the LAC, the economy hurts at once.
  • Chip anecdote (lecturer): a chip order for EV components cancelled overnight; prices up ~20% by morning. Reason: Nvidia and Anthropic placed bulk orders with chip fabs for cutting-edge GPUs, so fabs deprioritise regular chips (cars/EVs, mobiles, laptops) → prices rise. Linked to talk of an "AI bubble" bursting.
  • What happened when Anthropic launched Project Glasswing: after realising Mythos' power, the US administration (Trump) told Anthropic's chief not to sell the model to any country the US cannot approve. India's Finance Ministry (Sitharaman) called a meeting; RBI Governor Sanjay Malhotra gave immediate directions — but there was no way out: Anthropic was willing, the US government said no until the country is vetted. Note: [Fable] and Mythos are the two models named.
Write-up (lecturer's board text): "Frontier AI models have emerged as tools of geopolitics, and access to these tools decides the geopolitical might of nations. Example: restricted access of Fable and Mythos by the US government, citing national security."
AI sovereignty is a Mains super-keyword: it also fits economics (supply-chain security), IR and cyber security. Also in IR: economic security now dominates political security; geo-economics dominates geopolitics.

2. The AI race — gaps in time and India's stance 0:15 – 0:30

  • Earlier: space race (1950s–70s). Now: AI race, and inside it a second race — who builds frontier AI models. Building a ChatGPT-class model is now ordinary; frontier models that can unravel whole systems are the new frontier.
  • Six-month gaps (lecturer's claim): US ahead of other nations by ~6 months; within the US, New York is ~6 months behind Silicon Valley; within Silicon Valley, frontier-AI firms are ~6 months ahead of other IT firms. Net: frontier labs are roughly 1–2 years ahead of India (estimates in newspapers range 1.5–3 years).
  • A tech race has become a race between nations. The larger IR discourse: sovereignty is not decided by guns alone but by how good your AI models are.
  • Talent point: best coders/prompters/AI modellers will lead; but note a recent caution — a US carmaker's AI use failed and it re-hired people.

Domestic debate: what should India build?

ViewArgument
Don't build ChatGPT-like LLMsAn IIT-linked founder: India cannot win; don't waste time.
Dual approachNandan Nilekani (as cited): build small language models for specific sectors and still use frontier models — if access is cut, dependence becomes a "significant hazard"; India is 100% vulnerable in the AI race.
Government's stanceIndia knows it can't win the build race; thus emphasises AI governance — it doesn't matter who makes a technology; it matters how it is used for welfare (India AI Impact Summit, Feb 2026).

Congruence with France / EU

Macron (France) and India are both "squeezed": the US has stepped back in the West, Japan in the East. France holds the G7 presidency, India BRICS chair — a rare chance to bridge Global North and South; risk: managing Iran, UAE and Arab states inside BRICS. Use "congruence" in IR answers.

3. Implication 4 — Cyber-power inequality 0:27 – 0:33

  • Frontier AI creates haves and have-nots: haves = those with access to frontier AI tech; have-nots = dependent on external vendors (OpenAI, Claude) without access.
  • Write cyber-power inequality ("country-wise divide between haves and have-nots") — Global North (haves) vs Global South (have-nots).
  • The Global South would be exposed to machine-speed attacks with human-speed patching — "patches will take years; accept it".
  • Best example: China — denied the International Space Station → built Tiangong; denied advanced chips by Nvidia → partial H200 supplies only after the Xi–Trump meeting.

4. Chips, ASML and the India Semiconductor Mission 0:30 – 0:36

  • ASML (Netherlands): the only company with EUV (Extreme Ultraviolet) lithography — machines that imprint chip dies on silicon wafers. The smaller the chip, the greater the precision needed.
  • The US (Trump administration) directed ASML (~2 years earlier) not to supply the latest equipment to China, so China's chips lag Taiwan's.
  • India link: PM Modi met ASML's CEO in the Netherlands (the lecturer also recalls an earlier photo of the CEO with Tata in India). Reason: US tariff policy and the decline in trans-Atlantic cooperation make the Netherlands attractive. ~2.5 lakh Indians live in the Netherlands (Europe: UK first, Germany second, Netherlands third).
  • India Semiconductor Mission: ~12 plants approved so far; ISM 2.0 expected before Prelims. Chips can be made in India (plants approved, ~2 more years) but equipment must be imported — hence ASML.
  • Static hooks: India's rare-earth corridor scheme (four states) for sintered permanent magnets (Budget class); critical minerals gave 3 questions in Prelims 2025 and 1 in 2026 (rare earth elements).

5. AI cyber arms race, hybrid & asymmetric warfare 0:35 – 0:55

Implication 7 — AI cyber arms race

  • Chinese cybersecurity firm Qihoo 360 (Q360) announced tools to match Mythos-type capabilities — an autonomous software-vulnerability detection tool. (Always use the names the lecturer gave: examiners reward precision.)
  • IDSA commentary (a young researcher, June 2026) on this — good source, but no time to read.
  • Cyber conflict precedents: Georgia and Estonia, Ukraine–Russia, India–Pakistan (our backyard).

Implication 8 — Intensification of hybrid and asymmetric warfare

  • Asymmetric example — Iran vs US: the US has the strongest defence tech and ~19–20 aircraft carriers (China ~9–10, India 1–2). Iran's strength was horizontal escalation with cheap drones and missiles. For the weaker side, not losing is a win — keep the stronger power engaged for all "five rounds".
  • Non-state actors need no expensive drones: one clever engineer with Mythos-type access can hit critical infrastructure or air-defence software. Mission Sudarshan Chakra (air-defence shield) runs on software. (Rafale loss: the vendor admitted one jet was down — software dependence.)
  • If a Chinese model like Q360 succeeds, it could be given to Pakistan — no need to engage forces; instead cause internal-security crises in India.
Write-up: "There is a shift from traditional warfare (state-led; force ratio; firepower; mobility — Kargil took ~35–40 days to mobilise) to hybrid & asymmetric warfare (non-state actors; information, cyber, proxy)." Add: it is not dependent on cyber alone — it is based on data flows; add decision latency and machine-assisted command.
  • Operation Sindoor lessons (lecturer's view): India lost the information and diplomatic battle; Pakistan spread narratives abroad. Non-state actors (LeT, JeM) launched cyber attacks (e.g. on an army school in Srinagar) — neutralised, but war is all-encompassing, not just on borders.
  • Core thought for the question: this is not merely a cyber-security threat — it is an internal-security, national-security and sovereignty threat.

6. Implication 9 — Financial cost; MSMEs 0:46 – 0:55

  • Acquisition cost of access to frontier AI is high (even a ChatGPT-class subscription ~₹23,000/yr; frontier access is at another scale).
  • State, Infosys-type firms and unicorns can pay. The worst hit are MSMEs (bulk of Indian IT firms are MSMEs). Recommendation: a separate fund for them with immediate effect.
  • Government has no access yet; once it does, officials will study and then act — too late if an attacker (e.g. a militant group) has already used it.
Why MSMEs are most vulnerable (write 1–2–3): (1) lack of cyber-security capabilities, (2) shortage of trained staff, (3) weak backup discipline and incident response plans — they have prepared for fire, monsoon and internet outages, not cyber attack.

Conclusion line for "Implications": "Therefore, the Mythos-led frontier AI model era is a national security concern." (This is also the 'Discuss' question flipped — if asked "Discuss", cover the dimensions above.)

7. Merits of access to Mythos — the positive side 0:55 – 1:07

Negatives dominate (about 70–80% of the answer), but passing mention of positives is needed. Government meetings, RBI statements and MeitY's access request show the downsides are serious.

  1. Better (sovereign) cyber-security capabilities.
  2. Early access → indigenisation of frontier AI model technology.
  3. Capacity building and training of human resource (cyber professionals learning AI tools).
  4. Economic advantages: a team of 100 cyber experts at ~₹1 crore each is a big cost; with Mythos, patching/discovery time falls and need for human deployment reduces (because of speed and scale of detection) → reduced dependence on cyber-security manpower.
  5. Strong deterrence against cyber attacks by non-state actors like LeT/JeM exploiting vulnerabilities for cyber crime (e.g. ransomware).

All merits are the flip side of the demerits — hence "double-edged sword" and "dual-use technology" (like a missile: send it to Pakistan or to outer space).

Dual-use export-control regimes (lecturer unsure which two — verify)

Four regimes: NSG (nuclear), MTCR (missiles), Australia Group (chemical/biological), Wassenaar Arrangement (conventional arms and dual-use goods/technologies). [Supplement] India is a member of MTCR, Australia Group and Wassenaar; not NSG.

8. Issues & gaps — challenges for India 1:07 – 1:55

There is a thin line: issues of India's cyber-security capabilities (a 3–4 page topic) vs issues from frontier-AI/Mythos-led threats. A "Mythos" question wants the second; cover the first in 3 lines.

A. Narrow vision 1:10

  • Cyber security is seen as a support function — protecting military systems and information systems (a few computers and antivirus).
  • The focus must now shift to: (1) AI-enabled cyber security, (2) autonomous systems, (3) network-centric architecture and the all-encompassing nature of frontier-AI-led cyber security.
  • This is a conceptual flaw. Fix the vision first.

B. Institutional gaps 1:15 – 1:30

  1. AI Safety Institute recently established (lecturer: Oct 2025) and headless (no director). Compare: UK AI Safety Institute analysed Mythos; US has the Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI).
  2. No dedicated body for model testing. Even if Mythos were handed to India, no body can reverse-engineer and test it with top experts. UK and US have world-class institutions.
  3. Development-driven, not safety-driven approach: IndiaAI Mission (MeitY) is more tilted to development/better models than safety evaluation — although "safe & trusted AI" is one of the seven pillars. Understandable for a developing country, but ethics should be clear from day one. Approach style: India is light-touch regulatory (like Japan) — US believes in unconstrained innovation; EU is strict on personal data; China is state-driven.

C. Human resource challenge 1:27 – 1:32

  • Lack of AI-related skilled professionals. Need specific roles (not generic "data analyst"): exploit analysts, secure-code reviewers, reverse engineers, cloud and API security experts, legal-forensic specialists.
  • Fact: cyber-security workforce gap of ~6 lakh professionals in India (lecturer garbled: "6000 … 6 lakh" — verify).
  • Fact: CERT-In reported 29.44 lakh cyber incidents (year not clear in the audio — verify); the lecturer will graph the trend in cyber security class.

D. Legal and accountability gap 1:32 – 1:42

  • Archaic laws incapable of addressing Mythos-type threats; e.g. IT Act, 2000 (26 years old). Other jurisdictions (EU, US, NZ, Australia) have legal frameworks; India has none for this layer — if Mythos were used to attack a bank or all coaching institutes, is it a cyber crime? It is a different layer altogether.
  • Accountability: a developer building a Mythos-type model has no regime to bind them; analogy: Manhattan Project minds. In the US, too, there is no regime — the administration's experts caught Anthropic's head and told him not to send anyone.
  • No AI-vulnerability governance regime covering safe harbour — e.g. Sec. 79 IT Act (intermediary protection): Telegram's brief ban threat to force compliance with MeitY directions.
  • No mandatory disclosure for critical vendors; no liability for negligent non-patching; no penalties for malicious use of AI-generated exploit pathways.

E. Classification and pre-release evaluation gaps 1:40 – 1:52

  • Frontier AI cyber-capability classification gap: a visible risk-tiering framework (high/medium/low risk) for AI models with offensive cyber capability needs to be developed. Some argue India shouldn't classify; many countries do (China, parts of the West). Classification is the first step to regulating development and release.
  • Pre-release evaluation and controlled-access gap: e.g. US government treated Fable and Mythos as national-security-sensitive models, temporarily restricted access, and lifted the restrictions only after safeguards and work on jailbreak standards. (Jailbreak = bypassing a software's built-in restrictions, like an iPhone jailbreak/unlocking.)
  • US process: tested extensively, studied implications, checked whether it could leak / be jailbroken by non-state actors (Al-Qaeda, Taliban), ransomware experts, Russian/Chinese experts → then allowed release to approved countries. India has no such pre-release mechanism (and no access).

F. Other gaps 1:50 – 2:12

  1. Limited R&D spending (about 0.6–0.7% of GDP; the audio reads "6%") — majority from public sector.
  2. Cyber-security related challenges (inside "Other"; a separate topic in the next class):
    • Outdated cyber-security policy — National Cyber Security Policy, 2013; no AI in it. (UPSC asked a basic cyber-security question in 2013; the lecturer missed the interview call by 1 mark that year.)
    • Poor record in vulnerability patching: WannaCry ransomware (Windows) — Microsoft discovered and patched the flaw ~2 months before the attack but Indian systems did not patch and ran outdated software; it hit hospitals and critical infrastructure. Similarly AIIMS Delhi ransomware, 2022 — slow to respond/patch.
    • Limited sectoral coordination — critical-infrastructure vulnerability needs Finance, Telecom, Railways, Power and Airports together.
    • Lack of sector-specific AI-cyber playbooks and lack of mandatory drills for AI-related threats.
    • AI-related threats to name: frontier-AI-led cyber-security concerns, supply-chain security, AI-assisted ransomware scenarios.
    • Cyber hygiene (better than "cyber literacy") — next class.
  3. Resource constraints: limited budget allocation to MeitY and limited allocation by companies towards cyber security.

The lecturer's order of gaps (memorise as a chain)

Vision → Institutions → People → Law & accountability → Classification & pre-release → Technology inferiority and money. "Vision (A) · Institutions (B) · HR (C) · Legal (D) · Risk-tiering/pre-release (E) · Other (F)".

9. Steps taken by India 2:05 – 2:20

"Steps" in Mains means the steps already taken; "way forward" expands them into your strategy. Steps here are specifically for the Mythos-led threat, not generic cyber steps.
  1. High-level inter-sectoral review — Ministry of Finance (Sitharaman) called a meeting with MeitY (Ashwini Vaishnaw) and sectoral regulators; participants included RBI, NPCI, CERT-In. It was not convened by MHA. Treated as a threat to financial security and critical digital infrastructure; shows proactive response.
  2. Role of RBI: issued advisories to regulated entities (banks and NBFCs); coordinated with global central banks (US Federal Reserve, Bank of England); directed boards to approve a gap assessment and a time-bound action plan. Assessments: scan risk from frontier AI models, identify vulnerabilities, structured cyber-security framework.
  3. RBI framework (2025): a framework to promote responsible and ethical adoption of AI in the financial sector — not a direct response to this threat but came before it.
  4. Engagement with Project Glasswing: India engaged via diplomacy and governmental agencies to gain access to Claude Mythos so as to see the vulnerabilities (the lecturer says Indian cyber-security agencies got access — verify).
  5. Panel under the SBI Chairman: set up to assess risk from AI platforms like Mythos and develop mitigating measures.

The lecturer says "four steps" then lists these; the RBI 2025 framework is a sub-point. Memorise as 4: review · RBI · Glasswing · SBI panel.

10. Way forward — strategy 2:20 – 3:08

Strategy logic: vision first → then policy/law → sector steps → regulation/accountability → capacity → diplomacy → sovereign AI. Remedies must come from the gaps you listed ("treat the disease you diagnosed"). Newspapers add refinements later.

Grand strategy (opening line)

A grand strategy for India comes first (like deciding "I want to be an IAS" before taking a course and allocating resources). No siloed approach.

1. Policy and legal steps

  • Do not say "update cyber-security policy" (a different domain) — instead create/formulate a National Frontier AI Cyber-Security Strategy focusing on: AI-assisted vulnerability discovery, AI-enabled phishing, AI model access governance, and incident reporting for AI-related incidents.
  • AI model access governance: who gets access to a Mythos-type model, how it is made, risk tiers, steps.
  • Establish a National AI-Cyber Fusion Centre integrating inputs from CERT-In, NCIIPC, I4C, RBI, SEBI, DoT, cyber-security companies and major cloud providers — avoids silo-based sectoral approach. Analogy: Information Fusion Centre – Indian Ocean Region (IFC-IOR), Gurugram (maritime security).

2. Sectoral steps (critical infrastructure and others)

  • Continuous AI-assisted security audits.
  • Mandatory asset inventory — hardware, software, vendors, cloud dependencies, APIs, open-source libraries.
  • Sectoral AI-cyber drills for all sectors to test preparedness.
  • Focus on smaller entities in critical sectors (The Hindu article): Example: while deposing before the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Finance, RBI acknowledged that cooperative banks lag in security maturity and have been subject to cyber attacks. Co-op banks (farmers' money) likely don't use robust CBS-based systems like HDFC/ICICI/Axis; many attacks go unnoticed. MSMEs must be told about the threat.
  • Prepare a trusted-vendor list.

3. Regulation and accountability

  • Government laws should not weaken cyber security. Example: the Digital Competition Bill (draft) aims to increase competition by allowing side-loading (installing third-party apps outside the app store's own market); this may expose users to malicious and fake applications, since app stores (esp. Apple iOS) run strict security checks that Android side-loading lacks.
  • Fill the legal/accountability gaps (safe harbour limits, mandatory disclosure, liability for negligent non-patching, penalties for malicious AI exploit use).

4. AI diplomacy

  • Cooperate with like-minded nations on AI-cyber risk, vulnerability disclosure and incident response.
  • Use platforms: Quad, G20, BIMSTEC, ASEAN, and bilateral dialogues on cyber security.
  • Example: India–Israel cooperation (Pegasus was built by Israel's NSO; the PM visited Israel in Feb 2026 per lecturer; Iran's attack on Israeli security used orchestrated cyber elements). Sign MoUs, learn capabilities.

5. AI skilling

  • AI-enabled cyber-security capacity building; industry–academia–government collaboration (IITs, NITs teach; government gives subsidies). Engage universities, private sector; design curricula.

6. Sovereign AI

  • Develop indigenous domestic AI models with domestic AI firms to detect anomalies and isolate vulnerabilities in real time.
  • Ensure internationally that AI models above a certain threshold are subject to international notification and review requirements (IDSA commentary after Mythos). Reason: AI is trans-national; everyone should be informed, not a few friends of a company. India can be the bridge between North–South, East–West.
  • Launch an equivalent of Project Glasswing in India with secured access — involve top 10–15 AI firms (e.g. Sarvam AI, Hanooman) to build generative AI models; the government currently follows a light-touch regulatory approach. Classify, adopt a frontier AI strategy and conduct pre-release trials.

7. Whole-of-government approach (end line)

Adopt a whole-of-government approach to ensure inter-ministerial and inter-sectoral cooperation, as national security is inseparable from national digital resilience.

Conclusion keyword: AI sovereignty — "India needs to develop and indigenise sovereign AI capabilities; it should not depend on anyone."

11. Answer writing — worked question and model answer 3:08 – 3:35

Question (dictated): "Discuss the concept of zero-day vulnerability in the light of Mythos. Also discuss the challenges faced by India because of the evolving era of frontier-AI-led cyber security." (Time: 10–12 min.)

Rules from the demo

  • Intro: 20–30 words normally; here ~40–50 words because two things are asked. Body: ~200 words. Conclusion: 20–30 words with a grand idea (here: sovereign AI).
  • Do not define Mythos first. The question is zero-day in the light of Mythos — first define zero-day (~25 words), then in the next paragraph link it to Mythos using the same keyword as the question ("in the light of").
  • Examiner has little time — the keyword in the question must show in your answer.
  • Paragraph 2 must connect to paragraph 1; don't jump straight to challenges. Use plain English; play with language (not "Mythos is frontier AI; it is autonomous").
  • Body: use a diagram/box titled "Challenges for India": A. Cyber-security challenge (1,2,3), B. Sectoral challenge (1,2,3,4), then C. AI sovereignty, D. Cyber arms race, E. Challenge of developing sovereign AI; add 2 lines of example for each. Use numbered points (1, 2, 3) not loose lines.
  • Handwriting: neat, medium size, no red pen. Practise in class; handwriting plays a large role in marks.
Model answer (drafted from the lecture's framework)

Intro: Zero-day vulnerability refers to an undisclosed flaw in software or hardware, unknown to its developer, which threat actors can exploit — it is called "zero-day" because the developer has zero days to fix it once it is found.

In the light of frontier AI models like Anthropic's Mythos, with autonomous capability to detect and exploit zero-day vulnerabilities at machine speed and scale, the concept has gained prominence and presents a major challenge for India.

Challenges for India:

  1. Cyber-security challenges: (a) outdated cyber-security policy (2013) and IT Act (2000) with no AI provisions; (b) poor record of vulnerability patching — e.g. WannaCry, AIIMS 2022; (c) shortage of AI-skilled professionals (workforce gap ~6 lakh); (d) institutional gaps — headless AI Safety Institute, no model-testing body.
  2. Sectoral challenges: BFSI (legacy core-banking systems, shared infrastructure), DPI (Aadhaar, CoWIN), critical infrastructure (power grids, ports, SCADA-run wind turbines), and national security (defence and intelligence platforms).
  3. AI sovereignty: frontier tools are geopolitical instruments — US restricted access to Mythos citing national security; India depends on foreign vendors to even learn of its flaws.
  4. Cyber arms race: e.g. China's Qihoo 360 matching Mythos-type capability; non-state actors can exploit with a low barrier.
  5. Challenge of developing sovereign AI: limited R&D spend, compute and talent.

Conclusion: In the light of Mythos, India must build a National Frontier-AI Cyber-Security Strategy and develop and indigenise sovereign AI capabilities, so that it does not depend on anyone for its digital security.

Glossary

AI sovereignty
A nation's control over, and access to, frontier AI capabilities; access decides geopolitical might.
Cyber-power inequality
Divide between countries with frontier AI access (haves) and dependants (have-nots).
Situational awareness
Mythos' very high awareness of its environment/system context — added as a keyword.
Horizontal escalation
Widening a conflict geographically/across fronts (Iran's strength vs US).
Asymmetric warfare
Weaker side avoids defeat using low-cost means (drones, cyber).
Decision latency
Time between detecting a threat and deciding to act; machine-assisted command aims to cut it.
Jailbreak
Bypassing a system's built-in restrictions.
Safe harbour
Legal protection to intermediaries (Sec. 79 IT Act) from liability for user content, on condition of compliance.
Risk tiering
Classifying AI models into high/medium/low risk.
