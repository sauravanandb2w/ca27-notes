Executive summary

  • Impact framework (reusable for any topic): individual → business → nation → international. For cyber: Economic · Social (psychological) · Political · National security · Geopolitical. Tweak the order to the subject — for internal security, economic + national security + geopolitical outrank social and political.
  • Economic: individual — loss of savings (digital arrest, scam hubs, extortion, fake loan schemes); business — loss for MSMEs/corporates (boss/CEO scam), higher cost of doing business (audit, fraud monitoring, customer redressal, cyber insurance, legal compliance, employee training); BFSI — weakened financial integrity (co-operative banks, systemic risk, financial exclusion); national — budgetary expenditure (CERT-In, MHA, cyber forensics, I4C, Pratibimb).
  • Social: psychological trauma, humiliation, anxiety, stigma; secondary harm to families; digital hesitation (first-time users, elderly, disabled — Supreme Court: right to digital access under Art. 21); impact on women and children (CSEM, cyber bullying, blackmail, depression, self-harm, Blue Whale); impact on youth (instant gratification, money-mule accounts).
  • Political: hits e-governance and service delivery (hospitals, municipal, certificates, welfare portals), good-governance principles, loss of trust and credibility, space for disinformation/misinformation and for non-state actors (Manipur, North-East).
  • National security: exploitation by non-state actors, extremist organisations, hostile intelligence networks; critical-infrastructure vulnerability (hospitals, power, banking, telecom, transport, ports) — JNPT ransomware 2017, GPS spoofing in the West Asian crisis and on aircraft near the western border.
  • Geopolitical: increased dependence on the Global North (toolkits, software, capacity building), dependence on bilateral and multilateral cooperation (India–Myanmar on cyber slavery; Israel), loss of credibility of India's DPI model and leadership of the Global South (PYQ 2025: India–Africa digital partnership).
  • Challenges (full list): rising attack volume (CERT-In incidents ~13.9 lakh in 2022 → 15.9 lakh → 20.4 lakh in 2024) · digitalisation · evolving crime · geography (hostile neighbourhood, proximity to Myanmar–Thailand–Laos, vast maritime geography and undersea cables, concentration of critical infrastructure) · policy (2013 policy, no maritime cyber policy, fragmented coordination, sectoral gaps) · federal issues · weak state cyber-policing · public–private trust deficit · low R&D · human-resource gaps · citizen-side hygiene · privacy–security dilemma · geopolitical dependence · AI-led challenges.
  • Answer-writing discipline: underline keywords → build structure around the keywords → show the heading the examiner asked for → intro (15–30 words) + body (~200 words) + conclusion. Never define what the question did not ask to define.

How to write any "Impact" answer 0:00 – 0:30 · 16:41 – 17:30

  • The lecturer: "These are structures to unravel while writing; every question is a new question in the exam. Once you start writing, your brain responds automatically in the exam hall." You do not memorise whole answers; you memorise the framework, and points flow.
  • Impact in this class is the same impact you will study again under Poverty & Hunger and Globalisation in GS-1/GS-2 — the same ladder applies: individual → business/institutions → nation → international.
  • If the question is about globalisation's impact on women/society, give more weight to society, the individual and politics; if it is an internal-security topic, give priority to economic, national security and geopolitical over social and political.
  • Two-way rule: keep tracing back to the topic (cyber attack vs cyber crime) so you don't expand beyond what the question demands.
Crucial caveat — attack vs crime in "impact": if the question asks the implications of a cyber attack, you cannot use digital arrest or scam hubs as examples — those are cyber crimes. Use malware, ransomware, DDoS, supply-chain attacks. But if an attack is asked, a cyber-crime impact is also acceptable as long as the points stay the same and only the examples change. "This is the toughest but easiest part."

1. Economic impact 0:00 – 23:20

"Economic impact" = money. Order: individual → business → nation.

A. Individual level

  • Loss of savings. Examples (for crime questions): digital arrest, scam hubs, extortion, fake loan schemes. Even if an account is hacked, money must be spent to recover/secure it.

B. Business / MSMEs / corporates

  • Loss of business (MSMEs, corporates). Example: "Boss scam" / "CEO scam" — a caller says "I am your boss/CEO, transfer this money", and the employee obeys.
  • Higher cost of doing business (the key one). Analogy: when GST started (2017), small firms had to hire a chartered accountant to file input credits on the government portal; a short-term tactical loss, later a strategic gain (good GST revenue). Similarly cyber compliance is a cost. Fire safety is another increased compliance cost (₹8–10 lakh to be fully compliant).
  • Cost items for a company (write them as sub-points): cyber-security audit (the government tells firms to get one — "see how vulnerable your data is"); fraud monitoring; customer redressal (a separate vertical to answer "where did our data go?"); cyber insurance; legal compliances; employee training.
  • Lecturer's own example: coaching institutes get calls claiming "your videos are on Telegram; strike a deal with us and they stop leaking" — extortion that raises the cost of running a platform.
  • Also: AI tools help but each paid platform adds to monthly cost (he cites ₹7–8k a month for 2–3 platforms).

C. BFSI (Banking, Financial Services & Insurance) and the digital economy

  • Cyber-attack-induced losses weaken financial integrity.
  • Co-operative banking platforms — the example: attacks on co-op banks create a systemic risk for the sector. Current-affairs link: the Home Minister said co-operatives are not just for milk, fertiliser and seeds but an alternative governance/development model — keyword "Sahkarita se Samriddhi"; Bharat Taxi (co-operative taxis); the vision of a co-operative in every village (petrol pump, grain storage, PDS shop). National Cooperation Policy 2025 ★. Co-operatives are the "fourth model" — neither private (privatisation) nor government (bureaucratisation) nor PPP.
  • Financial inclusion impact: repeated attacks make people refrain from cyber-enabled payment mechanisms (UPI, QR). UPI's strength was scalability; attacks reverse the trend — as people once abandoned credit cards over fear of card cloning.
  • Impact on government — JAM trinity (Jan Dhan–Aadhaar–Mobile): a rural MGNREGA beneficiary's account is in a village branch of SBI, a co-operative bank or the Post Office Savings Bank — all very susceptible to attacks; "server down" events halt withdrawals → half of inclusion suffers.

D. National level — budgetary expenditure

  • Increase in budgetary expenditure — allocation to CERT-In (MeitY), MHA for cyber forensics, cyber reporting, and development of platforms: I4C, NATGRID, and Pratibimb.
  • While writing: the answer can show government steps side by side ("if a cyber security incident → CERT-In; cyber crime → I4C; coordination → platform"). NATGRID (MHA) — a grid of intelligence databases linking police stations; the Ministry has been urging states to use it; a keyword for Prelims. Pratibimb (Hindi for "reflection") — a platform for coordination ("samanvay" = coordination); note the name. ★ verify details.
  • Final point: social-sector expenditure may be compromised as funds shift to cyber security.
Structure to remember: Individual — Business — Nation — [BFSI inside]. If a single line like "BFSI represents a country's financial image and reputation" fits, add it; underline "loss of savings" and "financial integrity".

2. Social impact 23:20 – 45:00

Ladder: me → my family → community → larger society. Not every point must appear for every head — don't force points. Keep the writing "like a sponge", flexible to the question's need.

  1. Psychological trauma — fear (aspirants even more: lost notes bought for ₹200 on Telegram), humiliation (data theft), anxiety, social stigma; fuels distrust ("I should have gone abroad…"). Examples — attack: trojans, malware, ransomware; crime: sextortion, doxing, cyber stalking.
  2. Secondary harm to families — reputational anxiety, emotional distress, social isolation; in vulnerable cases suicide (repeated cyber bullying/stalking; teenagers harming themselves).
  3. Digital hesitation (keyword) — first-time users, vulnerable sections (elderly). Example: the Supreme Court (reported May 2026 in the lecture) held that the right to digital access is part of the fundamental right to life under Art. 21 — in the context of KYC norms where a physical process is a major hassle for persons with disabilities (e.g. a blind person using iOS accessibility). ★ verify date/case.
    • Include disabled population, and rural and small-town users → undermines financial inclusion and digital governance (e.g. fear of fingerprint mismatch in ration cards). Leads to concern for India's Digital India approach (all service delivery via digital).
    • Those who are online keep using it; those who are not get further excluded → widening of the digital divide ("already existing divide between digital haves and have-nots further accentuates; cyber-crime-induced digital hesitation").
  4. Impact on women and children — women: stalking, morphing, harassment, cyber blackmail → reduced women's participation in online spaces. Children: CSEM, cyber bullying, blackmail (also via gaming apps) → depression, self-induced isolation, self-harm, suicide; example Blue Whale game (staged challenges ending in self-harm); a child has no social safety net. (For girls it is more reputational anxiety; for boys/children more self-harm.)
  5. Impact on youth — youth (Gen Z) wants instant gratification; no course joined, "Telegram will do". Cyber crime lures youth (e.g. "give your account, ₹10,000 comes in"): becoming a victim and perpetrator; money-mule accounts ("mule = khachchar", for which youth are targeted) — fraud proceeds must be deposited into third-party accounts to look legal; being drawn into a money-mule network for quick money and getting entrapped in an organised cyber-crime network.
The lecturer's caution: do not write only your own suffering — optimise. Handwriting and answer-writing skill differ by person; know how much the question needs.

3. Political impact 44:51 – 53:50

  1. Impact on e-governance and service delivery. Cyber attacks hold up public services — hospitals, municipal systems, digital certificates, government welfare portals — so good-governance principles are compromised (service delivery first: a beneficiary whose portal was hit by malware does not get benefits).
  2. Loss of trust and credibility for the government and institutions, which opens space for public distrust and dissent against government decisions.
  3. The "phenomenon" this causes (lecturer's argument): misinformation and disinformation spread because of inherent loss of trust. If governance were optimal, fake news would find no ground. Example: after an Aadhaar-enabled payment fails and ration/housing benefits are denied, people believe a WhatsApp rumour. Information warfare starts from the lack of faith in governance. In developed nations with high trust (Denmark, Norway, Netherlands, Iceland) spread is many times lower — a matter of social capital (strength of citizens' and communities' networks).
  4. Increased role of non-state actors — where the state is incapable, others fill the gap: North-East liberation groups; Manipur — Arambai Tenggol, Kuki and Meitei groups. In Africa and Pakistan the state is "not a credible service provider" (in Pakistan the army, not the state) — "parallel government" is the idea (slightly off-track but valid).

Don't over-expand political impact — it is lower in priority for an internal-security topic.

4. National security & internal security impact 53:44 – 1:03:49

Always track back to the question's focus (cyber attacks / cyber crime). Impact on national security is the highest-priority head.
  1. Threat to national security because cyber attacks/crime are exercised by (1) non-state actors, (2) extremist organisations and (3) hostile intelligence networks.
  2. Impact on critical infrastructure — vulnerability increases in: hospitals, power, banking, telecom, transport (ports in particular). Example: 2017 global ransomware attack on the JNPT terminal (Jawaharlal Nehru Port, Mumbai — "based in Bombay") ★.
    • Also increases vulnerability of the logistics sector → delay in delivery of goods and services, port congestion, exposure of cargo and crew data, and enabling smuggling.
    • Recent example — GPS spoofing in the West Asian crisis: navigation signals used by shipping companies were targeted, ships got lost; satellite-tracked. Similarly, Indian airlines flying towards the western border (Amritsar) or Leh/Srinagar frequently report GPS spoofing by non-state actors across the border (temporary; other navigational aids exist, but it is a major concern for the transport sub-sector).

5. Geopolitical impact 1:02:36 – 1:19:10

Economic and national-security dimensions are already covered; write the example only and elaborate each critical-infrastructure head separately only if told to. Maritime security is flagged because of recent articles after the West Asian crisis.

  1. Increased dependence on the Global North for (a) cyber security toolkits, (b) cyber security software, (c) overall capacity building. Reason: India markets itself as a provider of affordable, scalable DPI (Aadhaar, CoWIN, UPI) to the Global South; but if the African nations see India's own systems attacked, they will say "you can't manage yourself — why should I buy from you?" and pay a bit more for US/UK/France/China systems.
  2. AI worsens it: nations already stronger (US, UK) gain; India's dependence on the US increases further. The lecturer: "It is not Trump 2.0's tariffs; it is your dependence on (1) access to critical minerals and (2) access to the latest AI models" that forces India to hold hands. AI will triple existing challenges — speed, scale and low entry (the Mythos-class framework) — so dependence on the West grows, compromising India's role as leader of the Global South.
  3. Increased dependence on bilateral and multilateral cooperation — e.g. India–Myanmar cooperation is essential to address scam compounds and cyber human trafficking, along with: (a) evidence sharing, (b) financial tracing (bringing money back), (c) takedown of infrastructure, (d) recovery of assets. Emerging concern: cyber slavery in Myanmar.
    • Strategic necessity: India needs Myanmar's cooperation for North-East insurgency management; the junta (which deposed the NLD/Aung San Suu Kyi) must also help stop the luring of youth. "If these compounds didn't exist, your leverage would be slightly heavier."
    • Israel: "India–Israel is like a marriage made in heaven" — surveillance, radar, resilient water practices, cyber security. Reason: Israel receives about 12% of cyber attacks worldwide (IDSA) so is thousands of years ahead; it is also ready to sell. Dehyphenation of Israel is a necessity; "you have to take sides by presuming you aren't". Lecturer's view: for Mythos-class vulnerabilities India may need the Israeli back channel since access via the US is unlikely. First cyber agreement with Israel was signed earlier and has recently been renewed ★ (dates garbled in captions).
  4. Loss of credibility. Frequent cyber attacks → vulnerability of India to cyber crime compromises India's reputation and its unique DPI model (low cost, scalable, adaptable). Then what will India sell?
    • PYQ (Mains 2025, 10 marks): "India–Africa digital partnership is achieving mutual respect…" — elaborate (keywords: mutual respect, co-development, long-term institutional partnership). Answer logic: compare with China's debt-ridden model and US's exploitative capitalist model; India is not a donor–seller; examples IIT Madras campus in Zanzibar, African states willing to enable UPI-based payments, Kenya pleased with an Aadhaar-like DPI — cheap, scalable, open source, modifiable for local needs.
    • So compromised reputation → affects India's leadership of the Global South (write: "affect India's leadership of the Global South").

6. Concluding the impact 1:19:21 – 1:22:50

  • Concluding line: "Cyber attacks and cyber crimes have not only impacted economic security and citizens' data security and national security but have also had strategic impact." Write a keyword only once — the lecturer's "repertoire is limited", so after using "economic security", "data security", "national security", the fresh keyword is strategic impact.
  • Five lines summarise the whole head: losses for government, individuals, society, nation; societal set-up affected; strong political consequences as governance is hit; loss of public trust; national security compromised along with loss of credibility internationally → strategic impact.
  • To be written in 150–200 words in the exam. This takes 1–2 months of practice; "be like a sponge — absorb all the facts, then write per the need of the exam."
  • "The introduction itself opens up the entire topic — a structure, a head, a pillar open the whole topic, and the same happens in the exam hall."

7. Challenges — how the head opens 1:22 – 1:29

Third part of the structure: "Challenges and issues in dealing with cyber security and cyber attacks" (the Hindi words chunautiyan = challenges, mudde = issues).

  • Write in brackets: "steps to be taken by the Government of India, and the way forward" — the way forward must follow the same structure.
  • Structure exception: the steps taken by India are normally written with an over-arching structure, so a verbatim one-to-one matching of challenge → step is not possible; but the way forward can be written in bracket form: "I recommend X because the government already does Y — let us expand it." If the way forward feels dead, either solve the issues or expand the steps already taken.
  • If the question is on way forward in Paper 3 (five internal-security questions), the examiner gives you that margin to expand your knowledge.
  • The lecturer would normally teach this as a table (Challenge | Issue | Steps) but avoids this pedagogy at this early stage.
  • Brain-storm first: "If this comes, everything comes." Start from the simplest fact: the increasing number of cyber attacks. Imagine you are a new DCP/SP in a Delhi zone: your first challenge is the rising crime rate in your district.

8. The challenges, one by one 1:29 – 2:36

Challenge 1 — Increasing volume of attacks 1:39 – 1:43

  • Graph: 2022 → 2023 → 2024. CERT-In-reported cyber security incidents: roughly 13.9 lakh (2022) → 15.9 lakh (2023) → 20.4 lakh (2024). "Remember ~20 lakh." (data via PIB.)
  • Interpretation: more attacks and better reporting — "a dual-edged sword": increased incidents point to better reporting and to increased vulnerability of India. Not every affected entity files an FIR; as with mobile theft, many say "good riddance"; as with rapes that are under-reported because of social pressure.
  • "Therefore it shows increasing risk as well as cracking capabilities." You may draw a bar graph or Venn — "any way of depiction".

Challenge 2 — Increasing digitalisation (of the economy)

  • Causes: increased internet penetration, smartphone penetration, urbanisation → increased vulnerability to cyber security incidents and cyber crimes.
  • Substantiate with facts: about 1.4 billion Aadhaar identities (≈95–98% of the country covered); 86% of households connected to the internet ★. "The government is creating UPI, CoWIN and Aadhaar — and you don't know the vulnerability; you are exposing your setup to the world."
  • Synonym: financialisation of the economy (treated as nearly the same concept).

Challenge 3 — Evolving nature of crimes

  • Cyber security challenge because of limited skills, techniques and technologies to address the threat.
  • Examples: digital-arrest fraud; cyber scam compounds; FaaS (Fraud as a Service — FraudGPT, WormGPT, Evil GPT: fraud tools are packaged and sold as software to potential scammers). Dark-web/Telegram availability of kits.

Challenge 4 — Geographical challenges 1:49 – 2:09

"Geography" in UPSC is all-encompassing: not just borders but social, economic, political geography, tribes, boundaries and fronts.

  1. Hostile neighbourhood converts borders into "cyber frontiers". Tip: when writing a 15-marker, break a long sentence into a point and then substantiate it; box only the main point so the examiner can find it — don't draw boxes upon boxes. Write 7–8 main points boxed, then sub-points with underlines.
    • Example — China-linked targeting of power-grid assets in Ladakh (a sensitive neighbouring territory; to erode confidence of locals) ★.
    • Example — Pahalgam attack period: cyber attacks on an Army school in Srinagar ★. These are APTs — Advanced Persistent Threats: groups that target India's infrastructure persistently.
    • APT36 — "Transparent Tribe" (Pakistan-based; from IDSA) — "not new", operating for over a decade, since India's first cyber security policy; continuously launching threats against India.
  2. Proximity to Myanmar, Thailand and Laos — which have been recruiting Indian youth into scam compounds / cyber slavery. KK Park (Myawaddy region, Myanmar–Thailand border) — "remember KK Park". Aside: a very popular library at the lecture venue shares the initials "KK" (humour).
  3. Weak cyber resilience in border areas — e.g. J&K: power-transmission structure is well-equipped and planned, but an attack on a school in Srinagar/Uri/border areas creates asymmetric vulnerability — "the enemy is very strong and you as the victim are not on the same footing".
  4. Vast maritime geography — sea lines of communication (SLOC); about 11,000 km coastline (lecturer: 11,012; commonly 11,098 km, Survey of India under Ministry of Earth Sciences ★). Samudrayaan came in Prelims this year. Undersea cables that bring internet to the country were a threat during the Black Sea crisis (Russia targeting Black Sea cables) and Iran (an asymmetric power) probing everything opposite to the US. In India, about 17 international submarine cables land at about 14 landing stations ★: Mumbai, Chennai, Kochi, Thoothukudi (Tuticorin; old name Thoothukkudi; where V.O. Chidambaranar Port — a green-hydrogen port hub — is near the Gulf of Mannar Biosphere Reserve), Thiruvananthapuram and others. Sea Lines of Communication = the shortest route; cables lie along it, also trade routes and global container/commodity flows.
    • Write: "Challenge of vast maritime geography: undersea cables are a strategic vulnerability as India hosts 17 international submarine cables at 14 landing stations." Cyber security is therefore not only computers and internet; it also covers threats to undersea cables.
    • Narco: India is source of neither; it is a transit and a consumer, between the Golden Triangle and Golden Crescent — relevant to narco-terrorism, not to cyber security.
  5. Concentration of critical infrastructure in high-value cyber targets. Data centres are located in metros — Noida, Gurugram (IT services clusters), Hyderabad (pharma), Bengaluru (start-ups); also stock exchanges, corporate headquarters, fintech platforms and financial infrastructure in urban areas. Five cities could be targeted to paralyse the entire internal-security and cyber-security mechanism.
Any single challenge can also be asked as a standalone question — "the entire challenges head can also be a question." Keep the entire head in mind and "play with the points and words".

Challenge 5 — Policy-related challenges 2:09 – 2:21

  1. Outdated cyber security policy — the National Cyber Security Policy 2013 has not been updated.
  2. Lack of a separate maritime cyber security policy.
  3. Fragmented institutional coordination — draw a box with the agencies:
    • For incidents: CERT-In (Ministry of Electronics & IT).
    • For crime: I4C and NCRB (data), both under the Ministry of Home Affairs.
    • Telecom networks: DoT (Department of Telecommunications) and TRAI.
    • National Security Council Secretariat (NSCS) under the PMO — overall security coordination and strategic direction (National Security Adviser's office; not NTRO).
    • Crime registration: BNS; State police.
    • National Critical Information Infrastructure Protection Centre (NCIIPC); private operators; SEBI (stock exchanges) and RBI (banks, esp. co-operative banks).
    Therefore there is a lack of a unified cyber command-and-control architecture (unlike Israel, which has a more unified cyber-response mechanism).
  4. Sectoral gaps — the 2013 policy asked for a unified approach with command and control, but a sector-specific approach is a must: e.g. telecom, renewable power. Example: an e-rickshaw battery management application can be switched off remotely ("all Chinese apps"); smart grids and energy management systems run on software — AI helps predict demand from rainfall and past consumption (e.g. "Delhi needs X MW next year", and asking the solar supplier to be ready with batteries) — if attacked, the entire system goes astray. If you are doing maritime cyber security, treat it separately.
    • Write: "Lack of dedicated cyber security frameworks for various sectors like telecom, health, ports."

Challenge 6 — Federal challenge 2:21 – 2:31

In internal security always write the federal challenge: the DGP–IGP conference (held annually near Pusa Road, ICAR campus; addressed by the PM) discusses upcoming concerns — cyber security among them.

  1. Lack of coordination between States and Centre. Police and public order are State List subjects; the crime must be registered by the State, then coordinated by the Centre. Example flow: CBI's Operation Chakra across many states → states' DGPs communicate to the Home Secretary → meeting at NSCS → NSA informed → decide to involve CBI. "No coordination = no reaction." Show it as a box: State government — State police — Public order in State List vs Central government — CBI (Operation Chakra) — CERT-In — NCIIPC — I4C → poor coordination to address cyber threats.
  2. Weak state-level cyber-policing capacity — "when you are an IPS officer at a state police academy, you will see the terrific difference between the SVP National Police Academy in Hyderabad and the state academy for state-service officers: DSPs, SIs, constables are barely trained. Only a district police may not be capable of investigating cyber crime (cyber forensics team, technical expertise, training in locating vulnerabilities)." Even Delhi (the pinnacle of policing) struggles: "my three phones have been lost; FIR is no way out".
  3. Poor inter-state coordination and jurisdictional challenge — cyber crime is inherently borderless: crime from a Myanmar compound; money to an account of a person living in Delhi; the victim called in Maharashtra; a crypto account created for Manipur. (Zero FIR exists — one can file anywhere — but both police must coordinate.)
  4. Conclude: structural mismatch — cyber security-related threats are borderless, but Indian policing capabilities vary from one state to another. "I don't have this problem with narco, cattle smuggling or fake currency notes (physical entities); here everything is on the internet." (BSF is exploring even crocodile/reptile ideas on the Bangladesh border for physical threats.) Cyber security is complete coordination.

9. "Other challenges" — the safety-valve head 2:30 – 2:52

"Other challenges" is a way of writing: if you've written 4–5 points and want 5 more, classify those 4–5 under Other as sub-points. The examiner finds it easier to evaluate. "Instead of showing 101 points, show 5 in main and 10 sub-points."

  1. Public–private trust deficit. A big IT company (running software, online classes) attacked: will it report to CERT-In? No — because reporting harms its reputation, brings regulatory penalties and litigation, and then the government follows up with audits and compliance (regular cyber audit, regulatory compliance). Even Infosys/TCS/Tech Mahindra-sized firms are susceptible.
    • Points: critical infrastructure is usually operated by private entities / PPP, so there is less reporting of cyber security incidents because of (1) reputational harm, (2) regulatory penalties, (3) litigation after reporting breaches. Therefore it weakens threat intelligence sharing.
  2. Privacy–security dilemma. To prevent crime in a classroom you add CCTV, but you violate privacy — you must display "under CCTV surveillance". To have cyber security, you need apps like Sanchar Saathi (Department of Telecom) — if everyone must have it pre-installed it is a perceived privacy violation. Hence "the challenge of balancing" cyber security and privacy.
    • Cyber security measures involve: (1) surveillance applications, (2) mandatory installations (example: DoT's Sanchar Saathi app; "device monitoring" — like a broken screen where a repair person asks for the unlock, or an iOS developer being asked for an iCloud password), and (3) large-scale data collection.
  3. Geopolitical challenge — dependence on the Global North and West-led cyber security companies; export or access restrictions imposed by nations (example: US Government restricting access to Mythos); dependence on other nations for cyber crime investigation and enforcement (e.g. cooperation with Myanmar to address cyber slavery); limited global cyber security cooperation.
  4. Technological and resource challenge. India's R&D spend is about 0.66% of GDP (the lecturer first says "6%" then "0.66%" — verify; advanced nations like the US and Israel spend ~3–4%+ ★). Israel is the leader in cyber security, with a unified, centralised approach; India has lack of coordination between units → "limiting an innovative cyber security approach". "Can we not make a Claude/Mythos/Fable on our own?" — a technical affair, not a mere money one.
  5. Human-resource challenge — limited role-specific cyber security capacity/capability: cyber forensic experts, malware analysts, privacy engineers, AI security researchers, cyber prosecutors. (Another framing: security experts exist but are not employable in the light of AI-related challenges.) Limited capabilities to deal with the latest crimes like scam compounds, extortion and crypto scams.
  6. National security challenge — addressing vulnerabilities in critical infrastructure, "the main battlefield of cyber conflict (or cyber warfare)": BFSI sector, maritime ports and submarine cables, energy and smart grids, industrial systems.
  7. AI-led challenges (a separate topic — a 500-page subject): AI has driven speed, scale and low entry barrier for cyber attacks and created problems of:
    1. Authenticity — e.g. deepfakes;
    2. Scale — mass delivery of personalised fraud messages;
    3. Attribution — because of AI-enabled identities and anonymised accounts;
    4. Trust problem — doctored videos of police officials;
    5. plus the latest frontier-model-led cyber security threats — e.g. Claude's Mythos.
  8. Citizen-centric challenges — limited digital awareness; limited knowledge of cyber hygiene.
Two reminders from this class: read rising incident counts as both better reporting and higher vulnerability, and learn topic-specific facts by heart. Nothing here replaces the fact base — "if an important fact like the ~20 lakh incident count stays with you, good; separate fact-books don't work well — topic-specific facts must be remembered, and for that you need relative understanding of data".

11. Worked question: cross-border cyber attacks 3:13 – 3:37

Question: Keeping in view India's internal security, analyse the impact of cross-border cyber attacks. Also discuss the challenges faced by India against these sophisticated attacks.

Step 1 — Read the question

  • Keywords: India's internal security · cross-border cyber attacks. Then two commands: analyse the impact and discuss the challenges ("sophisticated attacks" are the setting). Three parts: intro + impact + challenges (+ conclusion).
  • It is not asking "what is a cyber attack" — so don't define cyber attack (a common error). It may be necessary to define cyber espionage / cyber terrorism / cyber warfare (technical terms). Say which border and which countries first.

Step 2 — Introduction (15–30 words)

  • Lecturer's draft: "India's vast geographical borders with China, Pakistan and Myanmar, along with proximity to Thailand and Laos, make it susceptible to cross-border cyber attacks, despite cyber attacks being borderless." Write the km numbers in the exam if recalled (e.g. x km with China, y km with Pakistan, z km with Myanmar; Bangladesh ~2,200 km? — he is unsure; to be studied in Border Management).
  • Then the keyword link: "Cyber security is a national-security threat" — inside, a polygon: economic security, data security, critical-infrastructure security, BFSI security, defence security, government database security ("I just try to expand").

Step 3 — Body: Impact (restrict to ~100 words) then Challenges

  • Impact heading: "Impact of cross-border cyber attacks". Don't expand — "if you only write impact you'll fill three pages". Impact is multi-dimensional (a closing sentence).
  • Be careful with social impact: psychological trauma was a cyber-crime impact. A Pakistani malware/ransomware attack doesn't produce psychological trauma; conceptual difference between attack and crime must be processed by the brain.
  • (a) Economic impact: loss of household savings; e.g. attacks on the banking system from China; reduced financial integrity — BFSI represents the financial image and reputation of the country; attacks on DPI like Aadhaar and UPI restrict financial inclusion and associated schemes (Jan Dhan Yojana / JAM trinity); increased business compliance cost and budgetary expenditure to ensure cyber compliance (e.g. NCIIPC, I4C).
  • (b) Political impact: reduced public trust in government and e-governance services and in India's DPI architecture.
  • (c) National security: repeated cross-border attacks make critical infrastructure vulnerable and emerge as a national-security concern.
  • (d) Geopolitical impact: reduced credibility as a leader of the Asian world and increased dependence on the West for access to cyber security capabilities/technologies.

Step 4 — Challenges: choose the five that fit "cross-border"

  1. Increasing digitalisation makes India more vulnerable (if the volume list is excluded, "that goes").
  2. Policy and institutional challenges: limited Centre–State coordination; limited state cyber-security capability (police and public order are State List subjects); outdated 2013 policy; lack of sectoral frameworks.
  3. Geographical challenge — since the question is about cross-border.
  4. Human resource challenge — lack of forces/capable mechanism to address cyber security.
  5. Technological and resource challenge.
  6. AI-related challenges — increased Pak–China cooperation may provide Pakistan access to frontier AI models — example Qihoo 360 (Q360) as a competitor to Mythos.

What to drop: evolution of cyber crime (Pakistan hasn't evolved that far — "100 years behind us"); geopolitical and citizen-centric points can be omitted; the "increasing number of attacks" point can be assumed (no cross-border data exists, but post-Operation Sindoor numbers are surely larger). If the question asked the whole of cyber attacks, the answer would change.

Step 5 — Conclusion (the lecturer's drill)

  • Way-forward lines are always asked: "In the light of recent AI-enabled cyber security challenges, India needs a new cyber security policy along with international, geopolitical collaborations to address terrorism and cyber security simultaneously."
  • "One arrow, many targets": the enemy is a terrorist and a cyber-terrorist. India must say it within the country and loudly to the world that Pakistan promotes proxy terror, proxy non-state actors, cyber terrorism and terrorism. India wants a UN convention on terrorism signed (a comprehensive convention) — terror attack, state-led or proxy-led, should be castigated and nations penalised; the same dialogue is needed on cyber security at the international level. Conclusion needs thinking beyond the notes and slowly acquired knowledge.
Mistakes the lecturer pointed out in the class's drafts: (1) defining cyber attack; (2) not naming which borders; (3) writing "cyber security is not a technical issue" without the polygon; (4) writing impact and challenges at equal length — impact capped at ~100 words; (5) using cyber-crime impact for an attack question. Don't read your own answer as an examiner would — the examiner sees only the keywords and the headings.

Borders as lines across which cyber attacks flow.

Faking navigation signals so that ships/aircraft get wrong positions.
Social capital
Strength of trust and networks within a society; higher in Nordic countries, reducing misinformation spread.

Weak-resilience areas facing a stronger adversary.
Trust deficit
Reluctance of private firms to report incidents to government.

Single architecture for cyber response, as in Israel.

AI both strengthens defence and empowers attackers.
