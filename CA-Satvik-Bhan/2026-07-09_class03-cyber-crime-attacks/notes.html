Executive summary

  • Frame every cyber answer in 6 parts: Introduction (definitions) → Impact → Challenges/issues + gaps in India's approach → India's strategy/steps → Way forward. Keywords: impact, elements, measures taken, types of cyber crime, threats, cyber warfare.
  • Cyber security = protection of cyber space (computer systems, networks, data, digital services, critical infrastructure) from unauthorised access, disruption, manipulation, espionage, fraud and destruction.
  • It is an overlapping issue, not a technical one: internal security, economic security (supply chains, BFSI), privacy, public order and national security/sovereignty. Link to AIIMS 2022 ransomware, co-op banks, telecom, transport, ports.
  • Functions (IBM list): protect sensitive data · prevent threats · business continuity · secure digital infrastructure · maintain the CIA triad · secure communication · regulatory compliance (GDPR, DPDP Act 2023) · identify & mitigate risk · educate users (cyber hygiene) · incident response.
  • 7 elements/pillars: network · information · endpoint · cloud · application · operational · disaster recovery & business continuity.
  • Cyber attack ≠ cyber crime. Almost every cyber attack is a cyber crime, but not vice versa. Attack = technical act on a system; crime = unlawful act (law decides) where the computer is a target or a tool. Government calls attacks "cyber security incidents", reported to CERT-In (MeitY); crimes go to police/I4C (MHA) and NCRB keeps data.
  • New trend: FaaS (Fraud-as-a-Service), WormGPT-style tools, low entry barrier because of AI. Cyber crime is now multidimensional: fraud + human trafficking + organised crime + terror/insurgent funding (scam compounds in Myanmar/Cambodia/Laos; cyber slavery).
  • Current-affairs hooks: Telegram blocked under Sec 69A after NEET 2026 leak, Operation Chakra (CBI), Online Gaming Act 2025, WazirX hack, Hezbollah pager attack, Iran-linked DDoS on GCC states.

What UPSC has asked — and the answer structure 0:08 – 0:23

Always keep the PYQ booklet in hand when studying a topic: first learn what type of questions come. The lecturer opens the PYQs on cyber security and notes the keywords:

  • Cyber attack + its impact ("what shall be the impact").
  • Elements of cyber security — a new term; just list the elements for now.
  • Measures taken by India (= "steps taken"; = "cyber security strategy"). If the verb is "critically analyse", add pros and cons (positives first, then negatives).
  • Different types of cyber crime — a fruitful area (phishing; digital arrest was expected and a full-page news article appeared).
  • Threats of cyber attack and cyber warfare. Social-networking-site meanings (2013) and the B.N. Srikrishna report — not relevant now.
Structure rule: every topic must be organised around a structure; before reading anything ask "where will I fit this?"
  1. Introduction — details of cyber attack and cyber crime (the two are different); cyber security as a concept; AI and cyber security (because AI is a current-affairs topic and cannot be taught in isolation — look at its effect in diplomacy, security, governance, society, geography).
  2. Impact (UPSC also says implications) — PYQ.
  3. Challenges / issues in dealing with cyber attacks and gaps in India's approach (what are the problems).
  4. Response — cyber security strategy of India (in brackets: "steps taken by the Government of India". Both mean the same whether UPSC says "steps" or "strategies").
  5. Way forward — usually asked as "suggest the strategies / what steps do you recommend".

Current-affairs framing: current affairs must be placed into a core structure; reading them "the way you read news" does not prepare you. Also: AI-enabled misuse is just one strand — you can ask ChatGPT/Claude how to launch an attack, and "FaaS" kits are sold on Telegram.

Examples of how the question arrives: "Critically analyse the steps taken by the Government of India…", "Discuss the evolving pattern of cyber crimes and suggest strategies…". Interview tip: structure also helps you talk in the interview.
Latest cyber-crime tool: WormGPT — the "other brother of ChatGPT" that explains how to carry out a cyber attack (the lecturer says "don't use it"). Reported by Times of India; FaaS = Fraud as a Service. Low entry barrier is AI's biggest problem (to be studied separately).

1. Cyber security as a concept 0:23 – 0:28

  • Definition: cyber security is protection of cyber space.
  • What is in cyber space: (1) computer systems, (2) networks, (3) data (data breach is the concern), (4) digital services (e.g. UPI), (5) critical infrastructure — "an important keyword; always show it separately to the examiner".
  • Protection from what: (1) unauthorised access, (2) disruption, (3) manipulation, (4) espionage (snooping/surveillance — see cyber espionage, e.g. Israel against Hezbollah, Hamas, Iran), (5) fraud and (6) destruction of systems.
  • Cyber crime is a small subset of cyber security. If you say "cyber attack", cyber crime is left out; if you say "cyber crime", cyber attack is left out — so define cyber security broadly.
  • Example: if malware/virus/trojan affects my UPI data, my services are affected as well. Whenever you discuss internal security, show critical infrastructure separately.

2. Not a technical issue — an overlapping issue 0:28 – 0:40

  • Twenty years ago people said cyber security is only for those who own a computer or laptop. That is also the government's earlier mistake: the Cyber Security Policy came in 2013. (Context: the pattern of Civil Services Mains was changing and cyber security was becoming a topic.)
  • Do not define cyber security every time. Tell the examiner at the start: "cyber security is not a technical issue; it is an overlapping issue." Show it with a polygon (dots) diagram — the lecturer uses polygons; a Venn diagram is also acceptable.
  • Internal security (centre of the diagram) — also expect the IR keyword used by the Prime Minister: India's ties with Japan, New Zealand, Indonesia, Australia are to be seen from this perspective. Hint: a similar keyword was asked in last year's Mains.
  • Economic security — ensuring the financial sector is not hit by cyber attacks; supply-chain resilience; security of BFSI. If an import system or a government website is attacked, economic security is compromised.
  • Privacy protection.
  • Public order — e.g. deepfake videos of politicians released; political statements circulated.
  • National security / sovereignty — "if I do not protect my country's cyber network I compromise my national security."

Why internal security? Cyber attacks can disrupt essential services:

  • Health: ransomware attack on AIIMS (2022) — a very popular example.
  • Banking: frequent attacks on co-operative banks.
  • Telecom, transport and (most important) ports — the port is the government's vision; maritime security was asked in Mains last year (Internal Security).

It also enables cyber terrorism, propaganda and radicalisation; allows theft of personal data, identity theft, fraud and online blackmail; and — since Operation Sindoor — is part of information warfare, asymmetric warfare, hybrid warfare, disinformation (spreading fake news at national/international level) and grey-zone warfare by non-state actors.

Finally it weakens public interest/trust in governance: everyone uses UPI, Aadhaar, DigiLocker, CoWIN, hospital appointment platforms (AIIMS, Safdarjung). If people lose faith, they stop using Aadhaar/DigiLocker. Recent scam — FASTag: fake sites rank first on search (search-engine optimisation by criminals) and charge for a fake FASTag recharge; the user pays at the toll twice. Use such scams as examples inside the main structure ("these are just a few words in your Mains answer"). Do not confuse the fake-recharge example with an actual NHAI/government system.

Templates, not rules: you can define, give broad implications, and justify why it is an internal-security issue. If you forget "privacy", it is fine — the examiner looks for keywords.

3. Functions of cyber security 0:39 – 0:51

Taken from IBM's website (the lecturer's source). These are the ingredients/pillars of a cyber security policy.

  1. Protect sensitive data.
  2. Prevent cyber threats.
  3. Ensure business continuity.
  4. Secure digital infrastructure — servers, databases, cloud environments.
  5. Maintain the CIA triad (see below) — "any company or government agency providing cyber security will always maintain the CIA".
  6. Enable secure communication.
  7. Support regulatory compliance — EU's GDPR (2018); in India the DPDP Act, 2023 (Digital Personal Data Protection Act). DPDP Act is also expected in Mains this year because the government recently approved the new rules for implementation; to be studied in Prelims later.
  8. Identify and mitigate risk.
  9. Educate and train users — cyber hygiene (keyword: if you don't write it, you don't get marks).
  10. Facilitate incident response — develop and execute plans to quickly detect, contain and recover from cyber security incidents.

The CIA triad (tripod / "triad of cyber security")

  • C — Confidentiality: information is accessible only to authorised parties.
  • I — Integrity: protection of data from being altered or corrupted.
  • A — Availability: resources are available when needed.
  • Link to Mythos (Class 1): "patching software" is a keyword from the CIA mindset.

Cyber hygiene — what it means

  • India has no training for users on cyber hygiene. Hygiene = what you do like washing hands/brushing teeth; for cyber space: don't log in on unauthorised websites; don't leave private-data logins open; two-factor authentication; change passwords (about 85% of a class of 100 won't have done even these); antivirus; use biometrics with privacy in mind; regular backups — the most important part.
  • Government link: whenever it faces an imminent threat, it ensures that data is located/served within the territory of India (data localisation) so that a backup exists after an attack.
Question to write and attempt after class: "In the light of increasing cyber security challenges, discuss and devise a cyber security policy and also analyse India's cyber security approach." — the 6–7 heads above are the ingredients of the policy.

5. Definition: cyber attack, and types of attacks 1:05 – 1:40

Make a heading "A. Some definitions for the exam" in the introduction. First definition: cyber attack.

Cyber attack: an attack on data, networks, applications, computer systems, critical infrastructure and services (e.g. health, UPI) with the intent of stealing information, disrupting services, causing financial loss, manipulating systems — and thereby threatening national security / internal security / sovereignty (these terms are interchangeable for the answer).

Broad objective: violation of one or more pillars of the CIA triad. Example — a virus corrupts data → threatens integrity; if the phone can't be accessed → threatens availability; confidentiality stays intact if the contact list was not stolen. Whenever you target a computer system, it is a cyber attack.

Official terminology (important)

  • The Government of India does not use "cyber attacks" — it uses "cyber security incidents". The IT Act, 2000 gives the frame; there is no formal definition of "cyber attack" in government documents (definitions below are from IBM, Kaspersky, US telecom etc.).
  • If a cyber attack happens → report to CERT-In (Computer Emergency Response Team – India) under MeitY (Minister: Ashwini Vaishnaw). CERT-In is the body formed to report cyber security incidents — data released through PIB on "increasing number of cyber security incidents".
  • If it is also a cyber crime → report to the nearest police station / Ministry of Home Affairs (MHA); an FIR is lodged. Example: my app is attacked and data stolen → I report the incident to CERT-In and file a police complaint.
  • Do not confuse with cyber bullying, cyber extortion, digital arrest or FASTag scams — those are cyber crimes. "Cyber security incident" ≈ cyber attack; remember "CSI → CERT-In" (CSI = Cyber Security Incidents) and: Prelims angle — the ministry is Electronics & IT (a recent Prelims asked the ministry behind Rashtriya Gram Swaraj Abhiyan — knowledge of ministries is needed).

Types of cyber attack (names only — depth is not required)

(i) Malware — malicious software

  • Includes virus, trojan, spyware, bots.
  • Example — "Android God Mode" malware: impersonated bank services (e.g. SBI YONO), replicating the SBI YONO and Jeevan Pramaan apps and other customer-support apps via a fake app. Just remember "God Mode".
  • The lecturer's note: "Don't confuse with COVID virus — pathogenic viruses will be studied in Prelims (Health)" and mentions two viruses currently in the news — Ebola (study locations, transmission, vaccine/drug availability, origin, human-to-human transmission) and Hanta virus. ★ verify current status.

(ii) Ransomware — where ransom is demanded

  • Attacker takes control of a system and demands money (like kidnapping for ransom) from an individual/company/organisation.
  • Modern ransomware = double extortion (keyword): 1) Exfiltration — copy the data out and demand a ransom to release it; 2) threaten to publish the data if the ransom is not paid, damaging reputation. (Infiltration = entering the system; exfiltration = taking data out.)
  • Lecturer's framing: nature of crime is evolving — write examples of single → double extortion ransomware, evolution of FaaS, and the emergence of AI-based, replicating phishing tools and deepfakes.

(iii) Social engineering

  • Phishing — impersonate trusted entities to steal credentials (OTPs, bank details) or introduce malware. Example: caller says "I am from HDFC Bank, you received ₹20,000; send OTP/QR".
  • Vishing = voice-based; Smishing = SMS-based. "Just keywords, touch and go".
  • Spear phishing — targets a head of an organisation or a specific individual (someone with resources / prominence); e.g. repeated calls to a UPSC-qualified candidate.

(iv) DDoS — Distributed Denial-of-Service

  • Launch several bots on a system so the site suffers heavy simultaneous traffic; users cannot log in. Keywords: attempts to make a website, application and network unavailable.
  • Current example: in the Iran war, Iran carried out many such attacks on the Gulf Cooperation Council states (Saudi Arabia, UAE, Oman, Bahrain, Kuwait, Qatar). ★ verify.

(v) Man-in-the-Middle (MITM)

  • An attacker secretly intercepts and alters communication between two parties. Methods: rogue Wi-Fi, DNS spoofing, fake certificates, malicious proxies.

(vi) Exploiting zero-day vulnerabilities

  • Software bug/error whose existence is unknown, so there are zero days of response. In brackets add: "Mythos' capability to attack and exploit zero-day vulnerabilities" (Class 1 link).

(vii) Supply-chain attacks

  • An organisation is indirectly targeted through (1) a trusted vendor, (2) a software provider, (3) a cloud service (e.g. attack on iCloud affects a teacher who teaches from a laptop and phone), (4) hardware components.
  • Hardware example: Israel's attack on Hezbollah pagers (a glitch/identified fault, then use triggered blasts; thousands of casualties). So far software was discussed; hardware is also an example.
  • Even an attack on Windows/macOS/iOS counts under supply-chain attack from the perspective of dependent users.
  • Prelims differ in depth: such questions will come as "match the following"; for Mains only the structure matters.

(viii) Other attacks

  • Account takeover, website defacement (changing a site's appearance with software), server compromise. Definitions differ across sources (Kaspersky, IBM, telecom firms); India has no single definition.
After listing, write one line: "However, cyber attacks need to be differentiated from cyber crimes."

6. Cyber attack vs cyber crime 1:39 – 1:49

  • Rule of thumb: almost all cyber attacks are cyber crimes, but not vice versa. Ask: is it a crime under the IT Act, 2000 and under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS) (earlier IPC)? Then report to CERT-In (if an attack) and file an FIR (MHA involved). Exceptions are very few.
  • Exception example — ethical hacking: hiring a firm (e.g. Anthropic's CEO) to use Mythos on your software to find zero-day vulnerabilities and patch them. It is a "cyber attack" technique but not a crime — you paid for it.
  • Cyber crime that is not an attack: sharing a paid class with 10 others (piracy); digital arrest phone calls ("I am from ED/CBI; your passport found with ₹2 crore drugs; pay immediately"); cyber bullying (writing bad things about a person); cyber stalking; deepfakes (AI edits of a photo) — "not an attack and not wholly a cyber attack". The law/government decides what is a cyber crime.
  • Cyber attack = technical exercise (you attack a computer system) — "not something a Tom, Dick or Harry can do… but AI lowers the entry barrier". Cyber crime = any crime committed through a network/phone/laptop; usually at individual level (though can be at large scale). Cyber attacks usually hit a system, harming many people.
  • Lecturer's view: AI-enabled cyber attack is more dangerous than AI-enabled cyber crime — because attacks need technical capability and AI removes that barrier; crimes like OTP fraud are already common.
  • Server example: if a server is compromised through an attack → cyber attack. If someone steals the physical server → simple theft (not cyber attack).

7. Cyber crimes — the detailed list 1:46 – 2:20

Cyber crime (definition): unlawful acts — cyber attack is technical; cyber crime is law — where a computer, mobile, network, internet or digital identity is either the target or the tool, or the place of commission of the offence.
  • Target — someone's computer/phone is attacked. Tool — a crime committed using a phone/computer, e.g. online betting (Dream11-type IPL betting), crypto trading that is illegal, etc.; no malware or bots, but the computer is the instrument.
  • Includes: all cyber attacks (malware, ransomware, DDoS, phishing, social engineering) plus identity theft (e.g. someone steals your Aadhaar OTP), online financial fraud, cyber stalking.

Government classification (MHA / I4C) — remember if you can

  • I4C = Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre, under the Ministry of Home Affairs (to be studied in steps taken).
  • Data comes from NCRB (National Crime Records Bureau; a new DG was appointed recently; office in Delhi near the airport — lecturer's recollection).
  • I4C's four categories: (1) Cryptocurrency crime (example: WazirX hack — ~US$230 million, ~₹2,300 crore ★), (2) Cyber terrorism, (3) Hacking, (4) Online financial fraud (also ransomware).

Detailed list of cyber crimes (a PYQ — write as 1, 2, 3, 4)

PYQs: only study those likely to recur; five-year-old PYQs are "out of fashion". The lecturer will help pick PYQs later.

  1. Cyber attacks (in brackets: the list above — malware, ransomware, DDoS, phishing, social engineering).
  2. Online financial fraud — includes smishing, phishing, vishing; KYC update fraud; job fraud and work-from-home scams; loan app fraud (fintech apps; "the biggest fraud"); matrimonial and romance fraud (dating apps, honey trapping). Causes: rise of internet, urbanisation, rising disposable income and associated loneliness in urban areas (India reportedly second most lonely country ★).
  3. Digital arrest — see next section.
  4. Cyber blackmail, online extortion, sextortion (threat of publishing content for money).
  5. Cyber stalking, cyber bullying, online harassment, and doxing — doxing = publishing personal information (phone numbers, address, workplace details); asked in an earlier exam.
  6. CSEM — Child Sexual Exploitative Material / Child Sexual Abuse and Exploitative Material. Linked to NCPCR (National Commission for Protection of Child Rights; Paper 2 question asked about its role). The Supreme Court used the term CSEM in a judgment (lecturer can't recall the case).
  7. Fake news, misinformation, disinformation (mis = unintended; dis = deliberate; Pakistan does it "to the highest order") and deepfake-enabled offences.
  8. Piracy, digital counterfeiting and intellectual-property offences (watching paid lectures on Telegram).
  9. Online betting, gaming and illegal lotteries (step: Online Gaming Act 2025 — the government recently issued the online-gaming rules; gaming companies have a Supreme Court case ★).
  10. Crypto-related cyber crime and virtual digital asset fraud (WazirX example).
  11. Cyber-enabled trafficking, forced cyber crime and scam compounds (Myanmar, Cambodia, Laos).

Telegram block — a current-affairs case study

  • Government gave 15 days to Telegram after paper leaks (NEET 2024; 2026) because Telegram hosts CSAM, leaked papers and tool kits ("FaaS fees ~₹2,000 for WormGPT" per Times of India). Delhi High Court: government action is proportionate to the objective (lecturer will discuss in Polity). ★ verify details.
  • Earlier use of Sec 69A, IT Act was mostly account/post blocking: Facebook (Meta) and Twitter dominated; WhatsApp very few; Telegram ~2–3%. Three levels: post → account → entire platform. First time a whole platform is blocked.
  • "Next year (after Prelims): implementation of the IT Act — Sec 79(3)(b) and Sec 69A — is a very interesting discourse."

Online betting — the lecturer's policy view

  • Banning does not eliminate demand: people use VPNs, dark web, Telegram, crypto. Western countries (US) have legalised betting for revenue; India needs a stronger regulatory channel so the state earns revenue — analogous to liquor prohibition (Gujarat, Bihar, Kerala except five-star hotels) where bans create bootlegging networks (Bihar → UP; Delhi's poor excise policy → Haryana, as the lecturer observed). Ethical dilemma to quote.

8. Digital arrest, scam compounds & cyber slavery 2:01 – 2:27

Digital arrest

Digital arrest: not a formal legal term, but a common modus operandi where fraudsters impersonate police, CBI, ED, customs, TRAI (Telecom Regulatory Authority of India) and court officials and falsely claim the victim is involved in money laundering, drug trafficking or illegal parcels, then force the victim to stay on a video call under psychological control and extract money.
  • Why it works: an ordinary person is scared of police; preparation for the exam also helps remove fear. The lecturer's family anecdote: a student received a call from a "Excise Inspector" in uniform (SSC-selected officers wear uniform); a cyber complaint was filed; victims in some cases lost lakhs/crores. "Don't pay."
  • Impact angle: beyond money — psychological trauma (educated people fooled; fear of reputation — "people will say he teaches current affairs and got fooled"). An AI clone of me would hurt less than being fooled — this is where psychological impact becomes a head of "Impact".
  • Government response: CBI's Operation Chakra — "just know the name Operation Chakra of CBI" (started across 100+ locations/states; the transcript says "101 states", garbled ★).
  • A full-page article on scam hubs appeared in The Hindu (Saturday Spotlight) and the Indian Express — scam compounds along the Myanmar–Thailand border in Karen State, where Indians are lured by job offers and held in compounds.

Scam compounds and "cyber slavery"

  • Cyber-enabled trafficking: unemployed youths are taken from India to Laos, Cambodia, Myanmar under false promises ("trafficking = taking a person to another country/state under false pretences", e.g. promised a job in Delhi but forced into prostitution or bonded labour).
  • Victims are forced to commit cyber crime in scam compounds: (1) phishing, (2) romance scams, (3) investment fraud, (4) digital arrest fraud.
  • Link-ups: narco-trafficking (Myanmar is part of the Golden Triangle), FICN (Fake Indian Currency Notes) and an ecosystem of organised crime.
  • Why Myanmar: failed state, junta controls only part of territory (>50% area under ethnic armed organisations that need money for armed struggle). Once they realise that scam compounds yield thousands of crores, they will make it their "bread and butter" — as Taliban/Afghan poppy did, as Kashmir terror groups moved from disinformation fundraising to narco-terrorism with drones, and as Lashkar sends fake notes from Nepal.
  • Diplomatic hook: improving relations with Myanmar is a prerequisite; the (now) President of Myanmar Min Aung Hlaing (the transcript garbles the name) visited India first (after China) after the elections and promised Myanmar soil would not be used for internal-security activities. ★ verify. Also mentioned: Vietnam link.
  • Laws used: cyber crime laws + Prevention of Human Trafficking / Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act — "three Acts" in a single case. Do not mix up with the Myanmar "Chin / Sagaing / Rakhine" regions where India has links; compounds are in Karen state.

9. Evolution of cyber crime 2:25 – 2:27

  • Cyber crime is no longer one issue — it is multidimensional: involves human trafficking, a point of cyber fraud, and transnational organised crime. Always box such a diagram and give it a heading: "Evolution of cyber crimes".
  • Example for the box: scam compounds.
  • Police must now catch cyber criminals and check cross-border agencies/terror organisations and use the human-trafficking law.
  • "To be quoted while discussing organised crime as a theme in Paper 3."
  • Different patterns of evolution to quote: single → double extortion (ransomware); evolution of FaaS; emergence of AI-based replicating phishing and deepfakes; online gaming/Dream11-type platforms (3–4 years ago nobody knew them); cyber slavery.

10. Other definitions: cyber terrorism, espionage, warfare 2:27 – 2:35

Heading: "Other definitions" (three, in this order).

1. Cyber terrorism (as per IT Act, 2000): use of digital systems to threaten the sovereignty, integrity, security and public order of the country.
  • Catch: terrorism is defined in law, but law and NCRB data carry no details of cyber terrorism incidents. Example to add: In 2025, the NIA registered a case for commission of cyber terrorism ★ (verify).
2. Cyber espionage: secret cyber operations to steal confidential information, strategic data, defence secrets, diplomatic communication, intellectual property and sensitive government and corporate data. (Israel vs Hezbollah/Hamas/Iran — referenced in the "snooping/surveillance" element.)
3. Cyber warfare: use of cyber operations by states and non-state actors during a strategic conflict to disrupt, degrade, destroy, manipulate and spy on another state's (1) military, (2) governmental, (3) critical infrastructure and information systems.
  • A company cannot wage "war"; only states and non-state actors can.
  • India's need: a cyber warfare defence and deterrence mechanism. Reason: the US has recognised cyberspace as the fifth domain (air, land, water, space + cyber) — if the US has done it, India must follow.

Which of the following is NOT a component of the CIA triad in cyber security?

Cyber security incidents in India are required to be reported to:

B. CERT-In
C. NCRB
D. NCPCR
Answer: B. CERT-In is under MeitY; I4C is under MHA; NCRB compiles crime data; NCPCR protects child rights.
Q3
"Double extortion" in modern ransomware refers to:
A. Demanding ransom twice in two currencies
B. Exfiltrating data and demanding ransom for its release, plus threatening to publish it if the ransom is not paid
C. Attacking two organisations together
D. Demanding ransom from the victim and a bank
Answer: B.
Q4
An attacker secretly intercepts and alters communication between two parties using rogue Wi-Fi or DNS spoofing. This is:
A. DDoS
B. Smishing
C. Man-in-the-middle
D. Ransomware
Answer: C.
Q5
Which statement is correct?
A. Every cyber crime is a cyber attack
B. Almost every cyber attack is a cyber crime, but many cyber crimes are not cyber attacks
C. Cyber attacks and crimes are the same under the IT Act
D. Cyber attacks are only reported to the police
Answer: B. Ethical hacking is a rare attack that is not a crime; digital arrest, stalking and piracy are crimes but not attacks.

Glossary

Cyber hygiene
Routine practices — 2FA, password change, antivirus, backups, avoiding unauthorised sites.
CIA triad
Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability.
Endpoint security
Protection of devices such as computers, smartphones and tablets.
IRP
Incident Response Plan.
Exfiltration
Taking data out of a compromised system; infiltration = entering it.
FaaS
Fraud as a Service — ready-made fraud toolkits sold online.
WormGPT
Malicious chatbot that assists in cyber crime (lecturer: do not use).
Vishing / Smishing
Voice-based / SMS-based phishing.
Spear phishing
Phishing aimed at a specific prominent individual or the head of an organisation.
DDoS
Distributed Denial of Service — flooding a service with traffic to make it unavailable.
MITM
Man-in-the-middle: secret interception and alteration of communication.
Zero-day
An unknown flaw with zero days to respond.
Supply-chain attack
Indirect attack through vendor, software provider, cloud or hardware.
Doxing
Publishing someone's personal information (phone, address, workplace).
CSEM
Child Sexual Exploitative (Abuse) Material.
Digital arrest
Modus operandi of fake officials holding a victim on video call under psychological control.
Scam compound
Fortified sites (Myanmar, Cambodia, Laos) where trafficked people run online scams.
Cyber slavery
Cyber-enabled trafficking and forced cyber crime.
I4C
Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre (MHA).
CERT-In
Computer Emergency Response Team – India (MeitY).
Fifth domain
Cyberspace after land, sea, air and space.
Honey trapping
Luring a person into a compromising relationship for blackmail.
